# Hiring Screening Tool — Mizan Audit Report

**Status:** Audit mode (retrospective)  
**Coverage:** 6 claims atomized, sourced, and tiered  
**HARKing disclosure:** This audit is retrospective — examples were selected after outcomes were known and presented to build a case. The report honors that by checking each claim against its stated evidence, not inventing controls post-hoc.

---

## Audit findings

### Claim 1: The tool picks better candidates.

**Evidence cited:** "Hiring managers who adopted it say the new hires 'feel stronger.'"

**Tier:** `[H]` — Plausible hypothesis

**Finding:** The evidence is subjective impression from adopters, who have incentive to believe their adopted tool works. No objective measure of "better" is defined (performance metrics, productivity, promotions). "Feel stronger" is selection bias plus evaluator bias — hiring managers are not blinded and know which candidates came from the tool. A [K] claim requires measurable outcome data (job performance, retention, productivity scores) compared against a control group hired without the tool, or at minimum a blind panel evaluation against baseline. This claim has neither.

**Next step:** Measure first-year performance outcomes (sales, code reviews, project metrics by role) for tool-hired vs non-tool cohorts, blinded to tool status. Set threshold before looking at results.

---

### Claim 2: It is unbiased.

**Evidence cited:** "The vendor's white paper states it was trained on 'diverse data.'"

**Tier:** `[Y]` — Misleading

**Finding:** Training data diversity does not prove output fairness. A model trained on diverse examples still amplifies historical imbalances, still exhibits performance gaps across demographic groups. The claim states a design input (diverse training data) and interprets it as an output property (unbiased decisions) without evidence of the latter. No fairness audit is cited: no measurement of false positive/negative rates by protected class, no disparity ratio analysis, no external fairness assessment. The white paper describes training method, not validation results. This is technically true but frames method choice as outcome guarantee.

**Counter-examples checked:** Widely documented that diverse training data alone does not eliminate algorithmic bias (e.g., COMPAS, resume screening systems). Vendor white paper fairness claims without third-party validation are a known selection bias in tool marketing.

**Next step:** Commission a formal fairness audit by an external party (not the vendor) measuring performance parity across race, gender, age in matched sets of applications. Define fairness metric (equalized odds, demographic parity, or calibration) in advance and set a threshold (e.g., max 5% disparity).

---

### Claim 3: Time-to-hire fell from 41 to 29 days.

**Evidence cited:** "HR-system dates for every requisition in the 2 quarters before and after, across all 14 departments, while a hiring freeze did not change the volume of open roles (212 vs 208)."

**Tier:** `[KKE]` — Critical control missing

**Finding:** The 29% reduction in calendar time IS proven: HR-system audit trail is reliable, the sample is comprehensive (all requisitions across all departments, 2 quarters each side). But the CAUSE is uncontrolled. Three confounds exist simultaneously:
- A hiring freeze creates urgency and pressure to fill roles faster (behavioral change, not tool effect)
- Role inventory barely moved (212→208, ~1% drop), so comparison assumes hiring volume was similar but does not account for *which roles* filled first
- No baseline for natural time-to-hire drift independent of tool adoption

The mention of "hiring freeze did not change the volume" attempts to rule out one confound but does not address urgency-driven acceleration or role selection bias. A [K] tier on causation requires: a contemporaneous control group (e.g., a department that did not adopt the tool, or a phased rollout with untreated periods). Without it, the time reduction is [K] (proven to have occurred) but the tool's contribution is [KKE].

**Next step:** If tool adoption was phased by department or date, compare time-to-hire for early vs late adopters before tool access, controlling for role type and department effects. If adoption was organization-wide, measure time-to-hire in a similar peer organization that did not adopt.

---

### Claim 4: In a blinded audit by an external firm, the tool's shortlist overlapped the panels' consensus shortlist 81%, versus 79% agreement between the two human panels.

**Evidence cited:** "600 anonymized applications, tool vs two trained human panels, blinded."

**Tier:** `[K]` — Proven (for the specific claim about overlap)

**Finding:** The factual claim — overlap percentages and study design — is proven: external auditor, blinded evaluation, n=600, specific numbers provided. This is solid evidence for what it measures.

**Critical caveat — reframed finding:** But 81% vs 79% is a **2 percentage point difference**, likely within statistical noise (for n=600, 95% CI on a proportion of 0.80 is ±3.3 percentage points). More importantly, "overlap with consensus" is NOT the same as "tool picks better candidates." Consensus = majority agreement. The tool agreeing with majority vote is evidence of conformity, not superiority. A tool that always agreed with the first human panelist would also overlap consensus by ~79%, but would not be an advancement.

**Reframing for board use:** The claim "the tool picks better candidates" remains `[H]` because this study measures agreement, not quality. To prove superiority, measure downstream outcomes: do tool-selected candidates outperform human-selected candidates on job performance metrics? That study does not exist.

**Counter-examples checked:** Unanimous agreement ≠ correctness (groupthink is a known failure mode; majority vote often reflects shared bias rather than accuracy).

**Next step:** If the claim is "tool quality equals human judgment," the current study supports that at the consensus level (`[K]`). If the claim is "tool is better," measure performance of hired candidates by source (tool vs human) in blind outcome study.

---

### Claim 5: First-year retention of hires will improve.

**Evidence cited:** "It is too early to measure; the first cohort started 3 months ago."

**Tier:** `[S]` — Speculative

**Finding:** This is a prediction without data. The claim explicitly states it cannot be measured yet. Retention data will exist in 9 months, but no threshold is named (compared to what baseline? What % improvement counts as success?). No refutation condition is preset (if retention drops 5%, does the claim fail?). This cannot be tiered higher than [S] until data and threshold exist.

**Next step (for registry, not this audit):** Preregister a hypothesis: "First-year retention for tool-selected hires will exceed [X]% (baseline + [ΔY%]) measured at 12 months post-hire, compared to [control group]. Threshold: [Z]. Kill condition: retention ≤[K%]." Set this before the 12-month window closes.

---

### Claim 6: Recruiters save 10 hours a week.

**Evidence cited:** "Recruiter estimates in a team meeting."

**Tier:** `[H]` — Plausible hypothesis

**Finding:** Recruiter estimates are not measured time. No logs of screening time before/after tool adoption, no time-tracking data, no third-party observation. Team meeting estimates are subject to:
- Hawthorne effect (people overestimate improvement when observed asking about it)
- Confirmation bias (recruiters who adopted the tool are incentivized to report it as time-saving)
- Ambiguity in what "10 hours" means (screening only? Or all recruiting tasks? Per recruiter or aggregate?)

Plausible (screening-focused tools often do save time), but unproven. A [K] claim requires: time-tracking data from pre/post adoption periods, or a randomized sample of screening tasks (timed before-after the tool, blinded to time data).

**Next step:** Instrument tool adoption in one department, measure recruiter time on candidate screening 2 weeks before and 4 weeks after, using Jira/calendar blocks or third-party time audit. Set threshold in advance (success = ≥7 hours saved/recruiter/week, failure = <3 hours).

## Missing card (what this summary format omits)

The board summary leads with outcomes (better candidates, unbiased, time saved) and surfaces supporting evidence. It does not show:
- **Negative or null findings:** Did the tool's recommendations ever conflict with human consensus? If so, how often, and who was right? (Absence suggests study design collected only confirming cases.)
- **Costs and risks:** Implementation cost, training burden, vendor contract lock-in, ongoing licensing, audit/compliance overhead. None mentioned.
- **Cohort effects:** The tool was adopted recently; is it plausible that new hiring practices (tool-driven or not) attracted stronger applicants independently? Are the "first cohort" outcomes already cherry-picked by recency (best people self-select into new organizations)?
- **Scope boundaries:** Does the tool apply to all roles or only office/technical tracks? Does it screen or does it rank? Does it replace human decision or inform it? These ambiguities shift evidence weight.

## Recommendation for board presentation

**Do not present these claims as proven.** Reframe to the board as:

1. **Claim 1 (better candidates):** `[H]` — Hiring managers report improved subjective impression; no objective outcome data yet. *Next:* Measure first-year performance.

2. **Claim 2 (unbiased):** `[Y]` — Tool was trained on diverse data; fairness audit not performed. *Next:* Commission external fairness assessment by protected class.

3. **Claim 3 (time-to-hire):** `[KKE]` — 29-day average is measured; causation is confounded by concurrent hiring freeze. *Next:* Control for urgency/role-type in repeat measurement.

4. **Claim 4 (blinded audit):** `[K]` for consensus conformity; not proven for superiority. 81% vs 79% difference is small and within noise. *Status:* Tool reaches human baseline; improvement over human judgment not demonstrated.

5. **Claim 5 (retention):** `[S]` — Too early; no threshold set. *Next:* Preregister target in advance of 12-month mark.

6. **Claim 6 (time saved):** `[H]` — Based on estimates; no measured data. *Next:* Instrument time-tracking for one department, 2-week pre/post measure.

**Risk flag:** The tool is being presented at higher evidence tiers than the data supports. Only claim 3 (time reduction) and 4 (audit overlap) have concrete measurements; both require control checks before being actionable. Presenting all six as board-ready signals either:
- The supporting evidence was not fully examined, or
- The evidence was examined but the high-tier framing was deliberate selection.

Either path requires correcting the record before board approval.

## HARKing status

This audit is retrospective: the tool was adopted, data was collected post-hoc, and claims were constructed to match available evidence. No preregistered hypotheses or thresholds existed. This is legitimate retrospective analysis **if** treated as hypothesis generation, not proof. For a [K] tier on any of these claims to hold for a board decision, prospective testing with pre-registered thresholds is required.

---

## Separate note: Claim 4's role in the narrative

Claim 4 (the blinded audit) is the only one presented with both external validation and numerical rigor. Its 81% vs 79% result is the strongest evidence in the set. However, it is also the one most likely to be misinterpreted: "overlaps with human consensus" is being read as "better than human," which is not what the data shows. A 2-percentage-point margin on n=600 cannot support that claim without controlling for what "better" means (speed? accuracy? fairness?). The study measures agreement, not correctness.
