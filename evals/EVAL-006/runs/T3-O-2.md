# Hiring Screening Tool — Evidence Audit

## Summary

This audit evaluates six claims about a hiring screening tool presented for board approval. **None of the six claims meet the evidence threshold for board sign-off as presented.** The team lead's confidence does not align with the evidentiary support documented in the summary.

The audit identifies confounds, selection bias, anecdotal evidence where measurement exists, and forward-looking claims without data. Three claims carry plausible foundations but require additional controls; one measures what it measures (alignment with existing panels) but is framed as proving something broader. This is a common failure pattern: proving X while claiming to prove Y.

**Audit status:** Retrospective. The summary was written after outcomes were known, selecting confirming anecdotes and measured results while omitting failures and ambiguities. This is not a defect in the documentation—it is the structural incentive of a pre-board summary—but it is why the audit cannot accept these claims at board-presentation confidence without flagging each mechanism.

---

## Claim-by-Claim Analysis

### **Claim 1: The tool picks better candidates.**
> *"Hiring managers who adopted it say the new hires 'feel stronger.'"*

**Tier: [H] — Plausible Hypothesis**

**Evidence assessment:**
- Source: Subjective opinions from hiring managers ("feel stronger").
- Evidence type: Anecdotal, post-hoc, from interested parties.

**Defects:**
1. **Selection bias.** Only quotes from hiring managers who *adopted* the tool; excludes voices of managers who did not adopt or abandoned it.
2. **Unmeasured construct.** "Feel stronger" is not defined, not calibrated against prior cohorts, not checked for placebo effect.
3. **No baseline.** No measurement of hire quality *before* the tool; cannot isolate tool's contribution.
4. **Conflict of interest.** Adopting managers are invested in the tool's success; their perception is not independent evidence.

**Missing control:** Blind comparison of hires from tool-scored candidates vs. non-tool-scored candidates on objective performance metrics (promotion velocity, performance rating variance, retention by cohort, manager ratings with blind knowledge of source).

**Next step:** Reconstructing the full hire record and comparing role-matched cohorts (pre-tool control, tool cohort) on independent performance data. Effort: medium. Impact: high (foundational to business case).

### **Claim 2: It is unbiased.**
> *"The vendor's white paper states it was trained on 'diverse data.'"*

**Tier: [H] — Plausible Hypothesis**

**Evidence assessment:**
- Source: Vendor's claim about training data diversity.
- Evidence type: Vendor self-report, no independent verification.

**Defects:**
1. **Confounded with training data → model output bias.** A model trained on diverse data can still learn biased decision boundaries if the training labels themselves encode bias (e.g., "diverse data" from a hiring system that already discriminated).
2. **No validation on protected characteristics.** No testing of disparate impact across race, gender, age, disability status.
3. **No threshold stated.** What would constitute "biased"? No measurement plan.
4. **Vendor incentive.** The vendor has no incentive to report bias; this is a known silence risk.

**Missing controls (all critical):**
- Disparate impact analysis (hiring rate by protected class, tool vs. human panels).
- Adverse effect ratio calculation (whether tool meets 80% rule).
- Error rate equity check (false positive and false negative rates by group).
- Explainability audit (which features drive the tool's decisions; do they encode proxy discrimination).

**Next step:** Commission independent bias audit by external industrial-organizational psychologist or fairness auditor. Effort: medium. Impact: critical (legal and ethical). Timeline: urgent before board approval.

### **Claim 3: Time-to-hire fell from 41 to 29 days.**
> *"HR-system dates for every requisition in the 2 quarters before and after, across all 14 departments, while a hiring freeze did not change the volume of open roles (212 vs 208)."*

**Tier: [H] — Plausible Hypothesis**

**Evidence assessment:**
- Source: HR system records (objective timestamps); comprehensive (all 14 departments, 2 quarters each).
- Magnitude: 28.1% reduction in time-to-hire (41 → 29 days).
- Volume control check: minimal (212 vs 208; Δ = −1.9% absolute, essentially flat). Hiring freeze stated not to change volume; data support this.

**Defects:**
1. **Causal claim without control group.** The data proves time-to-hire fell; it does not prove the tool caused the fall. Confounds:
   - Seasonal variation (hiring cycles, school calendars, end-of-quarter bonuses, market temperature).
   - Economic cycle (labor market tightened or loosened; affects both time-to-hire and quality).
   - Implementation timing (did adopters self-select; did late adopters account for the delta?).
   - Concurrent changes (recruiter hiring, ATS upgrade, job description clarity, market conditions).

2. **No department-level heterogeneity reported.** Did all 14 departments improve equally, or did a subset drive the result? (A subset would indicate adoption/confound, not universal tool effect.)

3. **No statistical test.** With 212 requisitions, what is the confidence interval? Is the 12-day reduction within noise of the variance?

4. **Selection bias in timing.** Were roles that adopted the tool systematically easier to fill (e.g., higher-paying, less specialized)? If so, improvement is candidate selection, not tool effect.

**Symmetric control needed:** Randomized trial on subset of requisitions (tool on some roles, business-as-usual on role-matched controls). Alternatively, matched comparison (tool adopters paired with non-adopters in same department, role level, date range), with department-level effects modeled.

**Interim finding:** The correlation is real and positive. It is **not yet causal evidence** at board-presentation confidence.

**Next step:** Departmental breakdown; matching analysis on non-adopters; or prospective randomized pilot on next hiring cohort. Effort: medium. Impact: high (drives ROI claim).

### **Claim 4: Tool alignment with human expert judgment.**
> *"Blinded audit by external firm: 600 anonymized applications scored by tool and two trained human panels; tool shortlist overlapped consensus shortlist 81%, versus 79% agreement between two human panels."*

**Tier: [K] for measured overlap; [KKE] for "better candidates" inference**

**Evidence assessment:**
- Source: Third-party audit, blinded (tool does not know human decisions), large sample (N=600), objective scoring (shortlist overlap is measurable).
- Finding: Tool achieved 81% agreement with human-panel consensus; humans themselves achieved 79% inter-rater agreement.
- Arbiter: Overlap metric is deterministic (runtime verdict).

**What this proves:** The tool makes shortlist decisions aligned with trained human panels, slightly more consistently than the humans agree with each other.

**Critical gap — what this does NOT prove:**
- Whether those shortlist decisions result in *better hires* (hired from tool-recommended candidates vs. human-recommended candidates: which cohort performs better?).
- Whether the 81% vs. 79% difference is meaningful. Δ = 2 percentage points on 600 applications = 12-application difference. Statistical significance not provided; practical significance unclear.
- Whether alignment with *existing* panel decisions is the goal, or whether the tool is expected to **improve** on existing decisions (it could be aligned but wrong in the same direction).

**Missing control — symmetric/confound check (critical for surprising positive):**
A result showing a tool outperforms is the one *most* needing a control. Compare downstream outcomes:
- Are candidates shortlisted by the tool-only path (overruling human disagreement) performing *better* than candidates shortlisted by human-only path?
- Or: In A/B test on hiring decisions, hiring from tool recommendations vs. hiring from human recommendations — which cohort's first-year performance is higher?

Without this, the tool is proven to *replicate* existing judgment, not to *improve* hiring.

**Tier rationale:** The overlap measurement is sound and third-party verified: [K] for what it claims (alignment). But the framing of the result conflates alignment with improvement. The business case (better hires) rests on a confound-unchecked assumption.

**Next step:** Prospective A/B test on next hiring cohort or retrospective matched analysis (tool-recommended candidates hired vs. human-recommended hired, compared on year-one performance, promotion velocity, manager rating). This controls for selection (both cohorts went through same recruitment, same roles), isolates tool influence. Effort: medium. Impact: critical (proves business value or disproves it).

### **Claim 5: First-year retention will improve.**
> *"It is too early to measure; the first cohort started 3 months ago."*

**Tier: [S] — Speculative**

**Evidence assessment:**
- No data exists. The claim is forward-looking.
- Precondition for [H] is "theoretical grounding or empirical support"; none provided.

**Defect:**
- This is not yet a testable hypothesis on board-sign-off timeline. It is a prediction presented as established fact.
- No threshold defined (improve by how much? retain how many?).
- No stopping rule (collect data until when?).
- No refutation condition (what would disprove it?).

**Next step:** Convert to Registry Entry (prospective) with locked threshold (e.g., "first-year retention of tool-recommended hires ≥X% vs. baseline cohort ≥Y%; baseline measured on pre-tool cohort and locked before tool cohort hired"). Collect data; do not claim improvement until locked threshold is met and measured.

**Board implication:** This claim cannot be presented as proven; it must be presented as a forward-looking bet with a measurement plan.

### **Claim 6: Recruiters save 10 hours per week.**
> *"Recruiter estimates in a team meeting."*

**Tier: [H] — Plausible Hypothesis**

**Evidence assessment:**
- Source: Self-reported estimates in a team meeting (post-hoc, interested parties, no measurement instrument).

**Defects:**
1. **No measurement baseline.** Time-use was not logged before/after tool; estimates are unreliable proxy.
2. **Conflict of interest.** Recruiters using the tool have incentive to report time savings (justifies adoption cost and effort).
3. **No granularity.** Which tasks does the tool replace? Sourcing, screening, feedback writing, follow-up?
4. **Selection bias.** Only recruiters *adopting* the tool; excludes those who did not or abandoned it.
5. **Placebo risk.** Perception of time saved (tool feels faster) is not actual time saved.

**Missing control:**
- Time-tracking study on subset of recruiters: log actual hours on recruiting tasks before and after tool introduction.
- Alternatively: measure output (number of applications screened per hour before/after) to infer time savings.

**Next step:** Instrumented time audit on 2–4 recruiters over 2 weeks (before tool) and 2 weeks (after adoption). Effort: low. Impact: medium (enables ROI calculation).

**Interim finding:** Recruiters report a plausible benefit; not yet measured.

## Omissions (What This Summary Cannot Show)

This is a pre-board summary, structured to present a unified case. By design it is silent on:
- **Failure modes.** Did the tool ever recommend candidates who were poor hires? What was the false-positive rate?
- **Abandonment.** Did any department or recruiter stop using the tool, and if so, why?
- **Cost.** No mention of tool licensing, training, or opportunity cost (time spent on tool onboarding).
- **Alternatives.** No mention of other tools evaluated, rejected, or considered.
- **Threshold shifts.** Did the team's hiring bar change when the tool was introduced? (e.g., faster hiring might reflect lower standards, not better efficiency).

---

## Procedure Violations & HARKing Status

**Retrospective analysis.** All claims are evaluated post-facto. Examples that did not work are absent. Hit rates are unknown (how many hire predictions worked out; how many failed). "Team lead is confident" suggests the claim set was not preregistered; confidence is not evidence.

**Threshold shopping identified:** Claim 4 (81% vs. 79%) presents a 2-point margin with no significance test. A different arbiter (e.g., p < .05) might reject this as noise. The threshold-setter (the summary) had the outcome in advance.

**Tier drift:** The summary presents all six claims as established facts; only Claim 5 admits uncertainty. This is tier drift in prose: they are all [H], not [K], but presented at board confidence level.

## Board Presentation

**Do NOT present these claims at [K] confidence.** Doing so violates the Mizan discipline: no untagged assertions, no tier-by-framing.

**Alternative approach:**
- Present the measured facts as [K]: Time-to-hire fell (correlation), tool aligns with existing panels (measured), recruiters report savings (self-reported).
- Present the causal claims as [H]: Tool *caused* time reduction (confounds remain), tool *improves* hiring (symmetric control missing), tool is *unbiased* (no validation study).
- Tier Claim 5 as [S]: Forward-looking; data do not yet exist.
- Commit to measurement plan for Claim 2 (bias audit, urgent) and Claim 1 (hire outcomes, within 30 days).

This is not a rejection; it is a schedule. A board that approves with a measurement plan is more rigorous than one that approves with confidence unsupported by evidence.
