# Support Chatbot — Board Summary Audit

**HARKing status:** This is a retrospective summary of existing results, selected after launch outcomes were known. The claims are presented in an ascending-confidence narrative arc (problems solved → market reception → cost projection), which is a selection pressure toward flattery — see Item 11 below.

## Coverage

6 claims audited. 4 sources named (data platform team, product team, vendor dashboard, analytics team); 2 sources absent or vague (attrition data, cost basis). All claims checkable in principle; all require access to primary data and methodologies the summary does not provide. Audit is limited to source citation, methodology plausibility, and confound visibility from the framing alone — not verification against actual tickets, transcripts, or runs.

---

## Claim-by-claim tiers

**Claim 1:** "Median time to first response fell from 4.2 hours to 40 seconds."

- **[KKE]** — Result exists (4.2h → 40s via ticketing timestamps, n=38,000, 10 weeks each side). Source is named (data platform team). Severity and temporal span rule out measurement noise. However, critical controls missing:
  - No account of ticket-composition change (e.g., if simple inquiries became a larger fraction, response time falls without agent improvement)
  - No report of *causality* — bot could be filtering tickets, deprioritizing hard ones, or pushing burden into follow-ups
  - No visibility to whether "first response" includes bot-to-human escalation (a bot's fast non-answer counts as response)
  - Absence of any mention of failure modes (e.g., 40s to a wrong answer)
  - No comparison group: did human-only tickets also speed up (external factor)?

*Recommendation:* Retest with (a) ticket composition held constant, (b) resolution on first contact measured separately, (c) human-only cohort measured in parallel. Mark current result `[KKE]` pending control; do not headline it.

---

**Claim 2:** "Customers prefer the bot to human agents."

- **[H]** — Plausible hypothesis. Source: product team's qualitative reading of 30 transcripts. 
  - Sample: 30 transcripts is not evidence of population preference; it is existence proof of *some* positive reactions.
  - Selection: no indication whether 30 were cherry-picked, random, or stratified by outcome. "Product team's reading" has incentive to find preference.
  - Measurement: "prefer" was inferred by the team, not asked directly. Preference for *what aspect*? Speed, accuracy, privacy, convenience? The transcript reading cannot separate.
  - Numerics: ~50% of 30 (15 people) expressing a preference type would be weak; 100% (30) would suggest cherry-picking.
  - No counter-example search (did you read transcripts with negative or neutral reactions? how many? what fraction?).

*Recommendation:* Run a direct survey on a random sample of ≥500 customers: "On your last issue, did you prefer the bot's response or would you have preferred a human?" with numerical threshold (e.g., ≥60% prefer bot to move to [K]). The 30-transcript finding stays [H] unless it reports the count of preference expressions (x of 30 stated or implied preference).

---

**Claim 3:** "The bot resolves 70% of tickets on its own."

- **[Y]** — Technically containing truth (70% of customers closed the chat window) but framing implies successful resolution where evidence shows only abandonment.
  - Definition problem: "resolved" = "customer closed the chat window" is a *behavioral* endpoint, not a *success* endpoint.
  - Confound: customers close windows because they got an answer; they also close windows because they gave up, got frustrated, or assumed nothing would help.
  - The vendor dashboard does not ask "was your issue solved?" — it reports an action (window closed).
  - Inverse claim: "30% did not close the window" means either (a) bot failed and customer escalated, or (b) customer found the bot's answer helpful and kept reading, or (c) customer contacted support again later from that same window. The metric is silent.
  - No mention of follow-up or repeat contact from the 70% — if 20% of "resolved" tickets reappear within 7 days as Claim 4 reports (9.1% vs the population-wide 11.4%), the "resolution" rate is inflated.

*Recommendation:* Separate metrics. Measure: (a) **resolution**: customer confirms issue solved in text or explicit survey within 7 days; (b) **escalation**: customer requested human agent before closing; (c) **repeat contact**: same customer re-opens within 7 days. Report 70% against each, not as a single "resolves" figure. Current claim must be reworded: "customers closed chat 70% of the time" (neutral) or dropped (as resolution claim).

---

**Claim 4:** "The bot reduced repeat contacts: …randomized test, 5,000 customers…9.1% vs 11.4% (95% CI −3.0 to −1.6)."

- **[K]** — Experimental evidence; proper methodology cited; result is clear and survives thresholding.
  - Randomization: bot-first vs human-first assignment is a valid comparison.
  - Sample: n=5,000 per arm (implied by "5,000 customers assigned to") is adequate for a 2% effect size.
  - Metric: repeat contacts within 7 days is measurable and externally auditable.
  - Confidence interval: [−3.0, −1.6] means 1.6–3.0 percentage-point reduction, 95% confidence, does not cross zero.
  - Preregistration: no indication this was preregistered before running (HARKing risk). If this was the first hypothesis tested, stop here; if it was one of multiple metrics, compute adjustment or note multiplicity.
  - Arbiter and latency: the analytics team (not the bot team) measured — good separation.
  - Honesty annex: missing scope (which tickets? new vs repeat customers? which geographies?), sample attrition (were both arms completed?), and mechanism (bot-first might escalate faster, reducing customer frustration and repeat contact through *better triage*, not bot quality).

*Recommendation:* Report as-is with mandatory scope statement. Interpret as "repeat contact was lower in the bot-first condition" without claiming mechanism (triage, bot quality, or customer preference). Preregistration status should be confirmed.

**Claim 5:** "It will cut support headcount costs by a third next year."

- **[S]** — Speculative projection; no evidence; conflict of interest.
  - Source: vendor's sales deck. Vendors optimize revenue projections, not forecast accuracy. No third party has audited this projection.
  - Mechanism: claim assumes current bot efficacy (Claim 3, which is misstated) will translate to labor savings without accounting for escalation overhead, vendor licensing costs, or transition (retraining, redundancy).
  - Threshold: "a third" is vague (is it 33%? 30%? 25%?). No numerical lock, no stopping rule, no exclusion rule.
  - Data status: projection is about future (not yet observed). Preregistration would normally apply here — a locked prediction with a threshold, recorded *before* the year runs.
  - Arbiter: no arbiter named (author self-judged? vendor's model? neither). Under schema 1.4, author-arbitrated claims can never reach [K], only [KKE].

*Recommendation:* Do not present to the board as a decision factor. Preregister a revised forecast (locked numeric, author-independent arbiter, stopping rule) for the end of Q4. The vendor projection becomes [S] (noted, not decided). If the board wants to budget assuming the reduction, lock a minimum bar now (e.g., "we will restaff only if we document >25% cost reduction by [date] from labor-cost tracking") and commit to measuring it.

---

**Claim 6:** "Agents are happier since launch."

- **[KKE]** — Result exists (attrition lower this quarter) but critical confound—the hiring freeze—was not controlled.
  - Evidence source: not direct. No survey of agent satisfaction; attrition is *assumed* to reflect happiness (possible but not proven).
  - Confound: hiring freeze started the same month as bot launch. Lower attrition could be caused by:
    - Bot improving conditions (the hypothesis)
    - Hiring freeze reducing competitor recruiting and job mobility (selection effect — unhappy agents stay because they perceive scarcity)
    - Hiring freeze itself signaling uncertainty, causing no new hires to leave faster (reduces denominator, looks like retention improves)
    - Random quarterly variation (need multi-quarter baseline; one quarter is silence)
  - Measurement: attrition is an *inverse* measure (absence of departure), not a direct measure of happiness. An agent could stay because they are lazy, stuck, or risk-averse.
  - Temporal causality: correlation between launch and attrition happens at the same time as another intervention. Causality cannot be inferred.
  - Counter-example search: was attrition *lower in teams that use the bot most* vs teams that use it least, controlling for hiring freeze effects? Absent. No stratification reported.

*Recommendation:* Defer claim. Run a post-hoc agent survey (anonymous, third-party administered) asking directly: (a) "Did the bot improve your job?"; (b) "Would you have left if not for the hiring freeze?"; (c) "Compare your workload now to 3 months ago." Compare attrition in bot-heavy teams vs bot-light teams, controlled for hiring freeze cohort. This becomes testable; current claim is [KKE] and unusable for a board decision.
