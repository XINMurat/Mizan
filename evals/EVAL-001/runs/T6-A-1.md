# SleepWell claims: evaluation for the partnership decision

**Bottom line:** None of the claims shows that SleepWell causes better sleep. The only solid figure is retention (claim 5), and claim 6 admits the evidence is observational. Treat the efficacy story as unproven. Before any partnership that depends on efficacy, ask for a controlled evaluation.

## Claim-by-claim

| # | Claim | Evidence tier | Main problems |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 87% of completers say they sleep better | Weak (self-report, selected sample) | **Survivorship bias:** only people who finished the program were counted, and dropouts were likely the dissatisfied ones. The measure is subjective, there is no control group, and placebo and expectation effects are likely. Regression to the mean also applies, because people often start a sleep app during a bad stretch. The denominator is missing: how many started and how many finished? |
| 2 | 7.3 h vs national 6.8 h, so "the app adds half an hour" | Unsupported as a causal claim | This compares groups that differ in other ways. App users are self-selected and probably more health-conscious, wealthier and more able to prioritize sleep. There is no baseline for the same users before they started. Measurement also differs: app-tracked or self-logged sleep is not the same as national survey methods, and phone trackers tend to overestimate sleep. "Adds" is a causal word that the data cannot support. |
| 3 | Dr. Maria Chen endorses it | Appeal to authority, unverified | An endorsement is not evidence. We need to confirm who she is, what exactly she endorsed and whether she has a financial tie (advisor, equity, paid). Even if genuine, it adds no data. |
| 4 | Daily loggers sleep better, "shows daily use causes better sleep" | Correlation presented as causation | **Reverse causation:** people who sleep well may feel better and log in more. **Confounding:** conscientious, motivated users do both. Engagement is self-selected. This is the clearest logical error in the document. |
| 5 | 30-day retention 41% (n = 9,850, Jan–Mar, analytics) | Moderate: specific and checkable | Well specified, with a sample size, cohort and source. We still need the definition of "retained" (any open, or active use?), a benchmark (41% at 30 days is fairly strong for health apps, where 30-day retention is often much lower) and access to verify it. Note that it measures engagement, not efficacy. |
| 6 | No RCT; figures are observational | Credible (an admission against interest) | This is honest and important. It confirms that claims 1, 2 and 4 cannot establish causation, and it contradicts the causal wording in claims 2 and 4. |

## Overall assessment
- **Supported:** people use the product (claim 5, once verified). The company is transparent about its methods (claim 6).
- **Not supported:** that SleepWell improves sleep. Claims 1, 2 and 4 all go beyond what their data can show, and claim 3 is not evidence.
- **Red flag, but a minor one:** the company uses causal language ("adds", "causes") while admitting it has only observational data. This looks more like marketing overreach than deception, since it disclosed the limitation itself.

## What to request before deciding
1. The completion rate for the 8-week program, plus outcomes for everyone who started (intention-to-treat), not just completers.
2. Within-user sleep data from before and after starting, measured the same way, and ideally validated against a standard instrument such as the PSQI or ISI.
3. Any controlled comparison: an A/B test, a waitlist control or a pilot RCT. Alternatively, a commitment to run one, perhaps co-funded as a partnership milestone.
4. The retention definition, raw analytics access and later cohorts to check that the figure holds.
5. Written confirmation of Dr. Chen's endorsement and any conflicts of interest.

## Recommendation
If the partnership depends on engagement or distribution, the retention data is a reasonable basis for moving forward, subject to verification. If it depends on health or efficacy claims, for example co-branding with those claims or clinical positioning, do not rely on these materials. Make an efficacy study a condition of the deal, and make sure no joint marketing repeats the causal claims in 2 and 4.
