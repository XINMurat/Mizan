# Review of SleepWell evidence summary

**Short version:** Claims 5 and 6 are adequately supported. Claims 1–4 do not support the causal or efficacy conclusions drawn from them. Right now the evidence shows that people use the app. It does not show that the app works.

## Claim 1 — "87% of completers say they sleep better"
- **Evidence:** A self-reported survey. The sample size, the question wording and the response rate are not given.
- **Baseline/control:** There is none. Poor sleep often improves on its own over 8 weeks (regression to the mean: people tend to sign up at their worst), so some improvement is expected without any app. Placebo and expectancy effects add to this.
- **Who judged:** The users judged themselves, and the company collected the answers. Nothing about sleep was measured objectively.
- **Survivorship bias:** Only people who completed the program were counted. People who dropped out, likely including those it did not help, are excluded. With 41% 30-day retention (claim 5), completers are probably a small, self-selected minority of users.
- **Overreach:** "Improves sleep quality" is a causal claim. The data supports only "most completers who answered report feeling better."
- **Verdict:** Not supported as an efficacy claim.

## Claim 2 — "7.3 h vs national 6.8 h; the app adds half an hour"
- **Comparison groups differ:** People who download a sleep app are not like the general population. They differ in age, income, health interest and schedules.
- **Measurement differs:** App-tracked or self-logged sleep is not measured the same way as national survey data. Phone and wearable tracking tends to overestimate sleep.
- **No before/after:** Nothing shows what these same users slept before they started.
- **Overreach:** "Adds half an hour" is a causal claim resting on a cross-sectional comparison between different groups.
- **Verdict:** Not supported. The half-hour figure is not evidence of any effect.

## Claim 3 — Dr. Maria Chen endorses it
- **This is an appeal to authority.** An endorsement is not data.
- **Unverified:** We don't know her credentials, how independent she is, or whether she has a financial tie to the company (advisor, equity, paid endorsement). We also don't know what exactly she endorses or whether she has seen data.
- **Verdict:** Evidentially irrelevant until the endorsement is verified and any conflicts of interest are disclosed. Even then, it does not replace outcome data.

## Claim 4 — daily users sleep better, "which shows daily use causes better sleep"
- **Confounding and reverse causation:** More motivated, healthier or more routine-oriented people may both log in daily and sleep better. People who sleep well may also simply enjoy opening the app more.
- **Selection:** How often people log in is chosen by the users themselves, not assigned.
- **Overreach:** The company's own claim 6 admits the data is observational, so this claim contradicts their own disclaimer. Correlation is presented as causation.
- **Verdict:** Not supported. At most it is an association that could justify a hypothesis worth testing.

## Claim 5 — 30-day retention 41% (n = 9,850, Jan–Mar cohort)
- This claim is specific, gives the sample size and time window, names its source, and makes no causal leap. It is adequately supported as a descriptive engagement metric, assuming the analytics are accurate.
- **Minor points to check:** How "retained" is defined (any open vs. an active session), and whether one cohort is representative.
- It says nothing about whether the app is effective. It also implies that roughly 59% of installers had left by day 30, which strengthens the survivorship concern in claim 1.
- **Verdict:** Adequately supported, within its narrow scope.

## Claim 6 — no RCT; the figures are observational
- **Verdict:** Accurate and appropriately candid. However, it undercuts claims 1, 2 and 4, which are all worded causally.

## Cross-cutting problems
- Causal language is built on observational data in claims 1, 2 and 4.
- None of the claims has a control group or pre-registered outcome.
- All outcome data is self-reported or collected by the company, with no independent judge.
- Survivorship and selection bias.
- The source is not independent: the company is promoting itself for a partnership.

## What to request before deciding
1. Before/after data for the same users, including people who dropped out (intention-to-treat), with sample sizes and response rates.
2. A validated outcome measure, such as PSQI or ISI, ideally backed by actigraphy.
3. A controlled comparison: a waitlist or randomized pilot, preferably run or audited by an independent party.
4. The details of Dr. Chen's endorsement and any conflict-of-interest disclosures.
5. The retention definition and figures from other cohorts.

**Bottom line:** Treat SleepWell as an app with moderate engagement and an unproven effect. Any partnership should not depend on efficacy claims, or should include an independent evaluation.
