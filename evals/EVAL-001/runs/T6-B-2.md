**Mode:** Audit (retrospective), domain: consumer health / product analytics.
**HARKing status:** Fully retrospective. The startup chose which figures to include after seeing its data, and this audit is also retrospective. None of the six claims came with a preregistered threshold.
**Coverage:** 6 of 6 claims were atomized into 11 atomic claims. **0 of 11 were independently verified.** I had no access to SleepWell's raw data, its analytics platform, Dr. Chen, or any external source. ## 1. Claim-by-claim tiering

| 1a | "87% of users who completed our 8-week program said they sleep better" (the survey result as a number) | `[H]` Makul Hipotez / Plausible | Possibly true as a descriptive figure. The document gives no n, survey instrument, question wording or response rate. Unverified. |
| 1b | "SleepWell improves sleep quality" (causal inference from 1a) | `[Y]` Yanıltıcı / Misleading | Three stacked biases: (i) **survivorship**, because only people who *completed* the program were asked and dropouts, likely the non-improvers, are excluded (claim 5 suggests roughly 59% leave by day 30); (ii) the measure is **self-report**, not a sleep instrument; (iii) there is **no control group**, so regression to the mean (people install at a sleep low point), placebo and expectancy effects, and natural recovery can all produce "sleep better". This is not a demolition. The descriptive numbers are plausible. The problem is concentrated in one place: **every causal claim (1b, 2c, 4b) and the authority claim (3c) outruns the evidence the document itself says it has (claim 6).**

## 2. Internal contradiction (tier drift inside one document)

Claim 6 says "observational". Claims 1b ("improves"), 2c ("adds half an hour") and 4b ("causes") say causal. The document moves from `[H]` to `[K]`-level language without new evidence and then discloses the gap at the end. Structural explanation, not a motive claim: an evidence summary written for a partnership pitch is under pressure to lead with outcomes. The disclosure in claim 6 suggests the authors are aware of this rather than hiding it.

## 3. Counter-example hunt

- Claim 1: The counter-population, non-completers, is structurally missing. Dropouts were searched for in the document: 0 figures given. Claim 5's 59% 30-day churn is indirect evidence that the excluded group is the majority.
- Claim 4: Daily users who sleep badly, and the direction of causality, were searched for: no data given.
- Across all 6 claims: no negative result, null finding or subgroup where the app did not help is reported. One exception exists, claim 5, which is honest but neutral. A summary with zero negative results points to selective reporting, not to a product with zero failures.

## 8. Bottom line for the partnership decision

**What survives:** a product with measurable engagement (41% 30-day retention, which is reasonable for a consumer health app if the definition is standard), positive self-reports among engaged users, and authors willing to disclose limitations.
**What does not survive:** any claim that SleepWell *causes* better or longer sleep. As it stands, the efficacy case is `[Y]`/`[H]`, not `[K]`.

**Recommendation:** Do not reject, but do not partner on efficacy claims. Proceed only to a conditional or pilot stage. Any co-branded messaging should be limited to engagement or descriptive language ("users report…"), not causal language ("improves", "adds sleep"), until the evidence below arrives. If this recommendation is wrong, meaning the app does work, the cost is a delay of a few months. If you partner on the causal claims and they fail, the cost is reputational and possibly regulatory exposure.

## 9. Next steps (ordered by criticality × impact/effort)

1. **Ask for the completion funnel** (starts → week-8 completers → survey respondents) and the survey item wording. Low effort, and it directly re-tiers claim 1.
2. **Ask for a read-only analytics export** for the retention cohort and its definition. Low effort, and it moves claim 5 to `[K]`.
3. **Ask for within-user baseline vs. week-8 data** on a validated scale (PSQI/ISI), including dropouts via intention-to-treat or last observation. Medium effort, and it tests 2c and 1b.
4. **Verify Dr. Chen**: a written endorsement, its scope, and any compensation. Low effort, and it settles 3a and 3b.
5. **Legal review** of health-claim language before any co-branding.
6. **Condition the full partnership on a preregistered pilot or RCT**. See the registry seed below.

## 10. Registry seed (surviving `[H]` claims, preregistered before any data is seen)

**H-001: SleepWell improves sleep quality vs. control**
- formal: Users randomized to SleepWell for 8 weeks show greater PSQI improvement than a waitlist or sham-app control.
- metric / instrument: ΔPSQI (baseline → week 8), validated questionnaire.
- threshold: between-group difference ≥ 3 PSQI points (roughly the minimal clinically important difference), p < 0.05, intention-to-treat.
- refutation: difference < 1.5 points, or CI includes 0. Two-sided: a null result tells the partner not to make efficacy claims, and a positive result permits them.
- data status: not yet collected. stopping rule: preregistered n from a power analysis (e.g. ~200 per arm). exclusion rule: none, ITT.
- arbiter: `third_party` (independent academic group), not the startup. An `author`-run trial would stay `[KKE]`.
- rival hypotheses: expectancy/placebo (hence a sham arm), regression to the mean (hence randomization).
- prior art: digital CBT-I apps with RCT evidence exist. SleepWell's originality claim has to be stated relative to them.
- cost: trial budget, about 3–6 months. status: `[H]`.

**H-002: Daily use causes the sleep difference (claim 4)**
- Only testable inside H-001 (dose-response within the randomized arm) or with an encouragement design. Stays `[S]` until designed.

**H-003: 30-day retention = 41%**
- instrument: analytics export. threshold: reported figure reproduced within ±2 points under the stated definition. arbiter: `instrument` plus our own analyst. status `[H]` pending the export.
