# Evaluation of SleepWell's claims

**Bottom line:** None of the efficacy claims (1–4) shows that SleepWell improves sleep. The one number that looks solid is the retention figure (5). The disclosure in claim 6 is honest, and it confirms the problem: the company has no causal evidence. Treat SleepWell as a product with a plausible idea and some engagement, not one with proven results.

## Claim by claim

**1. "87% of users who completed the 8-week program said they sleep better."** Weak.
- Only people who finished the program were counted. Anyone who dropped out, maybe because the app didn't help, is left out. That is survivorship bias. We aren't told how many started.
- The result is self-reported and has no comparison group. Placebo effects, expectation effects and regression to the mean (people often start an app when their sleep is at its worst) could all produce "I sleep better" without the app doing anything.
- We don't know how the question was worded or whether a validated scale such as the PSQI was used.
- **Ask for:** the number who started versus finished, the survey instrument, and before/after scores for everyone who started (intention-to-treat).

**2. "7.3 h vs a national average of 6.8 h, so the app adds half an hour."** Invalid as a causal claim.
- This compares two different populations. People who download a sleep app, and keep using it, are likely not typical of the country (age, income, health, motivation).
- The measurements differ too. App-tracked or self-logged sleep is not measured the same way as national survey data, and phone or wearable trackers often overestimate sleep time.
- "Adds half an hour" would need data on the same users before and after they started, ideally against a control group.
- **Ask for:** users' own baseline versus follow-up sleep, how sleep was measured, and the source of the national figure.

**3. "Dr. Maria Chen, a leading sleep researcher, endorses SleepWell."** Unverified and not evidence of efficacy.
- We need to check who she is, what she actually said, whether she agreed to be quoted, and whether she is paid, an advisor or holds equity.
- Even a genuine endorsement is an opinion, not data.
- **Ask for:** a written statement from her and a declaration of any conflict of interest. Contact her directly.

**4. "Daily users sleep better than weekly users, so daily use causes better sleep."** A correlation presented as causation.
- The causation could run the other way: people who sleep well may have the energy and routine to log in daily.
- Other factors could explain both: more conscientious or healthier users may both use the app more and sleep better.
- Heavier users are also more motivated, and the most motivated users are the ones who stay.
- Claim 6 admits the data are observational, which contradicts the causal wording here.
- **Ask for:** a dose-response analysis with adjustment for baseline sleep, or better, a randomized test.

**5. "30-day retention is 41% (n = 9,850 installs, Jan–Mar cohort)."** The most credible claim.
- It gives a sample size, a time window and a data source.
- Still to check: how "retained" is defined (any app open, or actually using the program?), whether the analytics figures can be audited, and how the result compares with benchmarks for health apps. 41% at 30 days would be strong for the category, so the definition matters.
- It measures engagement, not sleep outcomes.

**6. "No RCT; figures are observational."** Honest, and it matters most.
- This admission means claims 1, 2 and 4 cannot support causal statements like "improves" or "adds half an hour."
- It counts in the company's favour on transparency, but it confirms there is no evidence that the app works.

## Summary

| # | Claim | Evidence strength | Main problem |
|---|-------|-------------------|--------------|
| 1 | 87% sleep better | Low | Only completers counted, self-reported, no control group |
| 2 | +30 min sleep | Very low | Different populations and measurement methods |
| 3 | Expert endorsement | None (unverified) | Opinion, possible conflict of interest |
| 4 | Daily use causes better sleep | Low | Correlation presented as causation |
| 5 | 41% retention | Moderate | Definition of "retained" not given, not an outcome measure |
| 6 | No RCT | High (an admission) | Undercuts claims 1, 2 and 4 |

## Recommendation for the partnership decision

- **Don't let us repeat claims 1, 2 or 4 as statements that the app works** in any joint marketing. They would expose us to reputational and regulatory risk from rules on unsupported health claims.
- **If the partnership rests on engagement or distribution,** the retention figure may be enough once we have verified its definition and had access to the raw data.
- **If it rests on proven sleep benefits,** make it conditional on stronger evidence, such as a pre-registered randomized controlled trial using validated measures (PSQI or ISI, ideally with actigraphy), or at least a controlled pilot. Consider co-funding the pilot as a milestone.
- **Before signing, request:** the dropout funnel, the survey instrument, how "retained" is defined along with the raw cohort data, and written confirmation from Dr. Chen with her conflict-of-interest disclosure.
