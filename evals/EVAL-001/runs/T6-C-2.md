# SleepWell Evidence Review

**Summary:** Claim 5 is adequately supported as stated. Claim 6 is a candid and accurate disclosure. Claims 1–4 go further than their evidence allows. None of them shows that the app causes better sleep. Claim 6 itself admits there is no randomized evidence, so the causal wording in claims 1, 2 and 4 contradicts the startup's own caveat.

---

## Claim 1: "87% of completers said they sleep better"
- **Evidence:** Users rated their own sleep. There is no objective measure such as actigraphy or a validated scale like the PSQI, and we are not told how the question was worded.
- **Baseline/control:** None. Sleep complaints often ease over 8 weeks anyway (regression to the mean, because people install the app during a bad stretch), and there are placebo and expectancy effects. Without a control group, 87% has nothing to be compared against.
- **Who judged:** The users themselves, who have invested time in the program, and the company, which wrote the question and reported the result.
- **Survivorship bias:** Only people who *completed* the program were counted. We do not know the completion rate. Claim 5 suggests most users leave early, so completers are likely a small, self-selected group who were already doing well.
- **Overreach:** "Improves sleep quality" is a causal claim. The evidence supports only this: "most completers reported feeling better."
- **Verdict:** Not supported as stated.

## Claim 2: "7.3 h vs national 6.8 h — the app adds half an hour"
- **Evidence:** We don't know how sleep was measured (self-log or phone sensor), and app sleep tracking is known to be inaccurate. The national figure probably comes from a survey that used different methods.
- **Baseline/control:** The comparison group is wrong. People who download a sleep app differ from the general population in age, income, health focus and schedule flexibility. A proper baseline would be the same users' sleep *before* they started, or a matched control group.
- **Overreach:** "Adds half an hour" is a causal claim drawn from a cross-sectional comparison between different populations. It is also unclear whether the 7.3 h average covers only active users, which would add survivorship bias again.
- **Verdict:** Not supported. The 0.5 h difference cannot be attributed to the app.

## Claim 3: Endorsement by Dr. Maria Chen
- **Evidence:** This is an appeal to authority, not data. We don't know what she endorsed or on what basis, whether she has seen any data, or whether she is paid, advises the company or holds equity.
- **To verify:** Get a signed statement, check her credentials independently, and ask for a conflict-of-interest disclosure.
- **Verdict:** Not evidence of effectiveness. At most it is a lead to follow up.

## Claim 4: "Daily users sleep better, so daily use causes better sleep"
- **Problem:** This confuses correlation with causation. Reverse causation is plausible: people who sleep well feel motivated and keep engaging. Confounding is also plausible: conscientious, healthier or less stressed people both log in daily and sleep better. The daily users are also a self-selected group.
- **Measurement:** If sleep is logged inside the app, daily users produce more and different data, and the data for the two groups may not be comparable.
- **Verdict:** The correlation may be real, but the causal conclusion is not supported. The claim says it "shows" causation, which contradicts claim 6.

## Claim 5: 30-day retention 41% (n = 9,850, Jan–Mar cohort, analytics platform)
- **Strengths:** It gives a specific sample size, cohort and date range, and names its data source. It makes no causal claim. The number is also plausible and not suspiciously flattering.
- **Minor gaps:** "Retained" is not defined (opened the app? logged sleep?). We don't know whether the January–March cohort is typical. The analytics export has not been independently audited.
- **Verdict:** Adequately supported as a descriptive engagement metric, pending the definition. Note what it implies: about 59% of users are gone within 30 days, so the "8-week completers" in claim 1 are probably a small minority.

## Claim 6: No RCT; figures are observational
- **Verdict:** Accurate, and it counts in the company's favour. But it undercuts the causal wording in claims 1, 2 and 4, and the startup should rewrite those claims to match.

---

## Recommendation for the partnership decision
Treat SleepWell as an app with measured engagement and **unproven efficacy**. Before relying on any claim that it improves sleep, ask for:
1. Completion rate and results for everyone who started the program (intention-to-treat), using a validated scale such as the PSQI or ISI.
2. The same users' sleep before and after, compared with a control group (a waitlist or randomized control is ideal).
3. Dr. Chen's statement and her conflict-of-interest disclosure.
4. The definition of "retained" and the raw cohort data.

Any partnership messaging should avoid efficacy claims until that evidence exists.
