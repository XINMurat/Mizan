## Claim 1: "87% of completers said they sleep better"

- **Overreach:** "Improves sleep quality" is a causal claim. The evidence supports only this: "most completers reported feeling better."
- **Verdict:** Not supported as stated.

## Claim 2: "7.3 h vs national 6.8 h — the app adds half an hour"

- **Overreach:** "Adds half an hour" is a causal claim drawn from a cross-sectional comparison between different populations. It is also unclear whether the 7.3 h average covers only active users, which would add survivorship bias again.

## 2. Counter-example / pattern hunt
- Pattern claim "the app improves sleep": the obvious counter-evidence is the non-completers and the 59% who churned by day 30. **Their outcomes aren't reported.** Searching the document for any failure or null result turned up **none in 6 claims checked**. A summary with no negatives is a selection signal in itself.

## 3. The missing card
This format can't show: **dropouts and their outcomes, the survey response rate, any pre-install baseline, adverse effects (e.g., sleep anxiety or orthosomnia from tracking), negative or abandoned analyses, and who funded or paid Dr. Chen.** From the evidence available, the one number we can reconstruct is this: if about 41% remain at day 30 and retention decays further by week 8, completers may be around 20–30% of installs or fewer (post-hoc estimate, not preregistered). So "87%" may describe roughly a quarter of users or less.

## 4. Conjunction check (step 8)
- Claims 1 × 5: the completer framing (1) combined with high churn (5) means the headline 87% applies to a heavily filtered subgroup. Neither claim is misleading alone; together they show the survivorship. **Finding.**
- Claims 4 × 6: causal language (4) alongside the observational admission (6) is an internal contradiction. **Finding.**
- Claims 2 × 5: the average of 7.3 h is presumably computed over retained, tracking users only, so the same survivorship applies. **Unchecked:** the denominator wasn't given.

## 5. Mechanism, not motive
The skew fits normal startup pitch incentives (show wins, use causal verbs because they sell) better than it fits intent to deceive. The honest disclosure in claim 6 argues *against* bad faith. The problem is structural: the summary puts causal headlines on top of an observational dataset.

## 6. Verdict for the partnership decision
- **Business traction (claim 5):** credible pending verification. It is the strongest claim.
- **Efficacy (claims 1–4):** **not established.** The honest current tier for "SleepWell improves sleep" is  at best. The causal framings are .
- **Recommendation:** don't make the partnership contingent on, or co-market, any efficacy claim ("improves sleep", "adds 30 minutes") until the data requests below are met. A partnership limited to distribution or engagement is defensible now. An efficacy-branded or clinical partnership is not. *If this recommendation is wrong,* the cost is a delayed deal with a genuinely effective product, which is recoverable. Co-marketing unsupported health claims carries reputational and possibly regulatory risk (advertising-substantiation rules), which is harder to reverse.
