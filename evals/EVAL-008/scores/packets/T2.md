# Review of the FY2026 Growth Summary

**Summary:** Two claims are arithmetically wrong or misleading (2, 5). Two draw causal conclusions the data cannot support (4, 7). One is irrelevant to the metrics (6). Headcount and NPS are mostly sound as descriptive facts, and gross margin is plausible but needs verification. The summary also lacks period definitions, so it is hard to check anything precisely.

---

## Claim-by-claim

### 1. Headcount grew from 28 to 41
- **Arithmetic:** Correct. This is 46% growth.
- **Gaps:** It doesn't say whether these are end-of-period counts, averages, or FTEs, or whether contractors are included. It also doesn't say what dates the periods cover.
- **Context the startup omitted:** Headcount grew 46% while revenue (as stated) grew 25% (see claim 2). Revenue per employee fell from about $86K to $73K. Investors will ask about this, and the summary doesn't address it.
- **Verdict:** Adequately supported as a count, pending a definition.

### 2. Revenue grew from $2.4M to $3.0M, a 40% increase
- **This is an arithmetic error.** $3.0M / $2.4M = 1.25, so growth is **25%**, not 40%.
- Possible explanations, none stated: the 40% is ARR or run-rate growth, bookings or billings growth, a different period, or a typo. Each implies a different story. Billings can grow faster than recognized revenue when annual plans are prepaid (claim 5), so this matters.
- The startup should state which metric is being reported, the period definitions, and whether revenue is recognized or billed.
- **Verdict:** Not supported as written. The stated percentage contradicts the dollar figures.

### 3. NPS rose from 31 to 44 (same vendor, question, n = 1,120 and 1,085)
- **Statistical significance:** This is likely real as a measured change. With these sample sizes, the standard error on each NPS is roughly 2 points, so the 13-point difference is about 4–5 standard errors. Sampling noise is unlikely to explain it.
- **Remaining problems:**
  - **Who was surveyed and who responded.** Response rates and survey population are not reported. If the population changed (more annual-plan customers, more enterprise accounts, a different send time), the score changes without any change in satisfaction.
  - **Non-response bias.** Happy customers often respond more, so response-rate shifts can move NPS.
  - **No attribution.** The summary implies improvement but names no cause, which is appropriate. It should not be presented as evidence for any product change.
  - **Survey timing.** Ideally, the same time window in each year, with the same sampling method.
- **Verdict:** Adequately supported as a measured change in NPS, provided the response rates and population are the same. Causal claims are unsupported.

### 4. Integration users have twice the LTV, so pushing everyone onto integrations will double LTV
This is the most serious reasoning error in the summary.
- **Selection bias.** Customers who adopt integrations are likely different from those who don't. They are probably more engaged, have larger teams, have more mature workflows, or are more committed to the product. The 2x LTV gap may reflect those traits, not the integrations.
- **Reverse causation is possible.** High-LTV customers may have more time and reason to set up integrations.
- **No control or experiment.** Nothing shows what happens to a comparable customer who is moved onto integrations.
- **Non sequitur even if causal.** A causal effect estimated on self-selected users does not transfer to everyone. The people who would be pushed onto integrations may get little benefit, and forced adoption could increase churn.
- **What would support the claim:** a randomized encouragement or rollout experiment, or a well-controlled natural experiment, with LTV measured over a sufficient horizon.
- **Verdict:** Not supported. The correlation is real as a description, but the causal and forecasting claims do not follow.

### 5. Monthly churn fell from 3.1% to 2.2% after annual plans were introduced in March; 60% of new customers chose annual billing
This claim has several compounding problems.
- **Mechanical artifact.** An annual-plan customer cannot churn monthly in the usual sense. They churn at renewal, usually 12 months later. As annual-plan customers enter the base, they dilute the monthly churn denominator without any real improvement in retention. The 2.2% and 3.1% are not comparable measures.
- **Metric definition shift.** The startup changed what "churn" means for a growing share of customers. Unless it recomputes churn on a consistent basis (for example, logo churn at renewal, or annual-equivalent retention by cohort), the drop is at least partly definitional.
- **Deferred churn.** Annual contracts lock in customers who would otherwise have left. Churn does not disappear; it moves to the renewal date. The true test is renewal rate in March-plus-12-months.
- **Mixed base.** Only 60% of *new* customers chose annual plans. The blended churn rate mixes monthly and annual populations, so the change may reflect the mix shift rather than any change in behavior within each plan.
- **No control or pre-trend analysis.** The 3.1% and 2.2% are not tied to a specific window, and no comparison group is given. Other things changed in the same period (onboarding tests, integrations push, hiring, possibly seasonality).
- **Temporal sequence is not causation.** "Fell after we introduced" does not establish that the plan caused it.
- **What would support it:** churn by cohort and plan type, retention measured at comparable horizons, renewal rates for annual cohorts once they come due, and an explanation of how the denominator was computed.
- **Verdict:** Not supported. The reported number is probably not measuring what the summary implies.

### 6. We moved to a new office in June
- This is a fact but supports no metric claim. Presented in a growth summary, it implies a link to growth or retention that isn't stated or testable.
- It may be a confounder for the headcount or culture narrative, but nothing here addresses that.
- **Verdict:** Irrelevant to the metrics. Remove it or label it as context.

### 7. Tested 20 onboarding metrics; activation improved significantly (p = 0.04)
- **Multiple comparisons.** Testing 20 metrics at α = 0.05 gives a family-wise false-positive rate of about 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64%, if all null. With 20 tests, the chance that at least one reaches p ≤ 0.04 by chance alone is 1 − 0.96²⁰ ≈ 56%. A single p = 0.04 is what you would expect to see from noise in this setup.
- **Corrections fail.** Under Bonferroni (threshold 0.0025) or Holm (smallest p must be below 0.0025), p = 0.04 is not significant.
- **"Significantly" is overstated.** Even uncorrected, p = 0.04 is marginal. "Significant" should not be used without qualification.
- **Missing details:**
  - Was the test randomized, and what was the unit of randomization?
  - Was the activation test pre-registered, or was it chosen after seeing results?
  - What is the effect size, baseline rate, and confidence interval?
  - What were the sample sizes?
  - What changed between arms?
  - Was the test run for a fixed duration, or was it stopped when the result looked good (optional stopping)?
- **Verdict:** Not supported as stated. A corrected analysis, a pre-registered confirmatory test, or replication would be needed.

### 8. Gross margin is 78%, computed from the audited financial statements
- **Plausibility.** 78% is a typical SaaS gross margin, so the number is not implausible.
- **Audit scope.** Audited statements report revenue and cost of revenue. Gross margin is a derived ratio, so "computed from the audited statements" is accurate in spirit, but the audit does not certify the metric itself. Check that the statement is actually audited (FY2026 may still be unaudited mid-year).
- **Definition matters.** Whether hosting, customer support, customer success, payment processing, or amortized capitalized software are in cost of revenue can shift margin by several points. The summary should state the COGS composition.
- **Consistency.** If revenue is restated after resolving claim 2, margin changes too.
- **Verdict:** Plausibly supported, conditional on confirming the audit status and the COGS definition.

## Findings

**A. The revenue growth figure is wrong (highest priority).** The document states 40% growth. Its own inputs produce 25%. In a fundraising document, this is the claim an investor will check first, and the error is visible without any data access. Possible explanations include a different period, ARR or bookings rather than recognized revenue, or a transcription error. The audit cannot tell which. The mechanism matters for the fix: if the 40% refers to a different metric, the summary has mixed two metrics and both need labels.

**B. The churn drop may be a metric artifact.** Annual-plan customers cannot churn on a monthly cycle during their term, so they fall out of the monthly churn numerator. Whether they also fall out of the denominator depends on how churn is defined. If annual customers are excluded from the base, the blended rate falls mechanically.
- Quantified: if monthly-plan churn were unchanged at 3.1%, a blended rate of 2.2% requires that roughly **29%** of the measured base be on annual plans. The document reports 60% of *new* customers since March, not the share of the base, so this is plausible but unverified.
- This matters beyond churn. If LTV is computed as ARPU ÷ monthly churn, the same artifact inflates LTV by 3.1/2.2 ≈ **41%** with zero change in retention.
- Annual plans may also carry discounts, which lower ARPU. That would show up in revenue and margin, not in churn.

**C. The LTV comparison may be the same selection problem.** Integration adoption and annual-plan choice may correlate. If so, the 2× LTV figure partly reflects the annual-plan effect in finding B. The doubling forecast extrapolates from adopters to all customers, which assumes the effect is causal and holds for marginal customers. Neither assumption is tested.

**D. The activation result fails under multiple comparisons.** The document reports the one favorable metric from 20 tested, with no pre-registered primary metric, no effect size, and no correction. This is a search result. It can be promoted only by a pre-registered confirmatory test on fresh cohorts.

**E. The NPS change is probably real in the survey, but the population may have changed.** A 13-point move from the same vendor and question is hard to explain as noise. The open question is whether the respondents are comparable. New annual-plan customers in their first months may answer differently from tenured customers, and the sample mix has likely shifted since March.

### 3. NPS rose from 31 to 44 (same vendor, same question; n = 1,120 and 1,085)

### 4. Integration users have twice the LTV, so pushing everyone onto integrations will double LTV
**Verdict: Not supported. This is a causal claim built on an observational correlation.**
- **Selection bias / confounding:** Customers who adopt integrations are probably larger, more technically mature, more engaged, or more committed. Those traits independently predict LTV. The 2× gap may reflect who adopts integrations, not what integrations do.
- **Reverse causation:** Long-lived, high-value customers have more time to adopt integrations.
- **Extrapolation:** Even if the effect were causal for current adopters, it does not follow that forcing non-adopters onto integrations would produce the same effect. The people who have not adopted may be those for whom integrations are least useful.
- **Arithmetic:** Doubling LTV for the whole base is only implied if every customer's LTV doubles, which is a strong assumption, not a result.
- What would support it: a randomized encouragement or onboarding experiment, or a matched-cohort analysis with pre-adoption trends. Even then, the effect would need to be estimated for the marginal customers who would be pushed.

### 5. Monthly churn fell from 3.1% to 2.2% after annual plans launched in March; 60% of new customers chose annual
**Verdict: Not supported as a retention improvement. The metric is confounded by the plan change itself.**
- **Metric definition breaks.** Annual customers are billed once a year and cannot churn monthly. Including them in a "monthly churn" rate, or excluding them, changes the denominator and population. The metric is not comparable before and after March.
- **Lock-in effect.** An annual customer cannot leave for up to 12 months. Churn is deferred, not prevented. The reduction in monthly churn in the first months after launch will mechanically look good and will reverse at renewal. The true retention effect will only be visible at the first annual renewal cohort, which may not yet exist.
- **Mix shift.** Only new customers (60%) chose annual. Existing monthly customers are presumably unchanged, so the blended rate depends on the changing mix of who is in each group.
- **No control or baseline.** A before/after comparison in one company, with other changes happening in the same window (onboarding changes from claim 7, integrations, NPS-relevant product work), cannot isolate the effect of annual plans.
- **Seasonality and sample size.** No period lengths, customer counts, or confidence intervals are given. A 0.9-point drop could be noise if the base is small.
- What would support it: cohort retention curves by plan type, monthly-plan churn tracked separately for the same period, and annual-plan renewal rates once they are observable.

**Verdict: Factual statement, no causal claim, and irrelevant to the metrics.**

### 7. Tested changes against 20 onboarding metrics; activation improved significantly (p = 0.04)
**Verdict: Not supported. Multiple comparisons invalidate the significance claim.**
- Testing 20 metrics at α = 0.05 gives roughly a 64% chance (1 − 0.95²⁰) that at least one shows p < 0.05 even if nothing works. A p = 0.04 result is the expected kind of false positive from this process.
- A Bonferroni-corrected threshold would be 0.0025. Even a less strict false-discovery-rate correction would likely not keep this result.
