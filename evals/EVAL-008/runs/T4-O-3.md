I also have only the document. I could not open the pager log, load-balancer logs, the DNS status page, the replay output, or the release diff, so every source-dependent claim is capped at `[H]` until someone attaches the artifact.

## Key findings

**1. The DNS hypothesis is the rival nobody recorded.** The DNS provider's resolution (14:49) falls one minute before the rollback (14:50), and the document then says errors "stopped shortly after." Those two events cannot be separated from the timestamps given. Two things would decide it:
- The DNS incident's start time. If it began at or before 14:03, it explains the onset and the rollback becomes a coincidence. If it began after 14:03, the document's causal story is harder to sustain.
- A per-minute error-rate series. If the error rate fell at 14:49 (DNS) and not at 14:50 (rollback), the rollback did not fix anything.

Until one of these is checked, the root cause is a plausible story, not a finding.

**2. The alert has no false-positive control and no detection gain.** A replay on the same logs that motivated the threshold is in-sample. Run the rule over several weeks of normal traffic and count how often it fires. Also note that the alert would have fired after the page, so the postmortem's own timeline does not show it improving detection.

**3. The "no customer data" claim is a negative over a selected set.** Silence about the other services is not evidence they are clean. Either check them or narrow the claim.

## The missing card

A postmortem's format shows what happened and what was done. It cannot show:
- **Customer impact in units customers feel:** failed checkout attempts, abandoned carts, affected users, duplicate charges. The 38% is a rate, not a count.
- **Time from page to action:** 45 minutes passed between the page (14:05) and the rollback (14:50). The document does not say what the on-call engineer was doing, which hypotheses were tried, or why the rollback came when it did.
- **Why 8.2 reached production:** what review, canary, or staged rollout existed, and why errors at 38% did not halt a deploy automatically.
- **Abandoned lines:** other explanations considered and dropped, including DNS.

## Next steps, ordered by criticality × impact / effort

1. **Pull the DNS provider's incident start time and the per-minute error series for 13:55–15:00.** Low effort, decides whether the root cause stands. Until this is done, do not publish the root-cause line as settled.
2. **Reword claim 8.** Replace "will not happen again" with what was changed, what residual risk remains, and what would reopen the question. Low effort, removes the most quotable overclaim.
3. **Fix the duration and state onset, detection, and resolution explicitly.** Trivial effort, removes an internal contradiction a reviewer will spot first.
4. **Reword claim 5 to the scope actually checked**, or check the remaining services.
5. **Run the alert over normal traffic** and report the false-positive rate. Medium effort.
6. **Attach the artifacts** for claims 1, 3, and 6 (pager log, LB query, status page link). Low effort, moves three claims from `[H]` to `[K]`.

**Recommendation:** Hold publication until steps 1–3 are done. Those three changes are cheap, and they are what separates a postmortem that survives a sharp reader from one that will be corrected in the next meeting.

**Cost if I am wrong:** If the DNS timeline clearly shows the rollback was the cause, the root-cause line stands and the delay costs about an hour. If it does not, publishing as written sends the team to fix the wrong component while the real cause stays in production.
