## Failures, in detail

**Claim 2: the growth rate is wrong.**
($3.0M − $2.4M) / $2.4M = 25%, not 40%. The summary overstates growth by 15 percentage points, which is 60% more than the real rate. A 40% rise would have produced $3.36M, a $0.36M gap. The likely mechanism is a different base or period, or ARR mixed with recognized revenue, but I cannot confirm which. Until the figure is corrected, this line should be treated as refuted. Revenue should be restated as "+25% ($2.4M → $3.0M)," with the basis (recognized revenue or ARR) stated.

**Claim 5: the churn drop is mostly mechanical.**
- A customer on an annual plan cannot register a monthly churn event until renewal. The March cohort's first renewal is roughly 12 months out, so if the fiscal year ends before then, those customers contribute zero churn exposure in the window.
- Sixty percent of *new* customers are on annual plans, so the blended monthly rate falls simply because a growing share of the base cannot churn in-period.
- A 3.1% → 2.2% drop is a 29% fall. Holding monthly-plan churn constant, that drop is what you would get if about 29% of the base had zero monthly churn exposure. That is plausible for a base where annual plans have been growing, so the number cannot be read as a retention improvement.
- "After March" is a temporal ordering, not evidence of causation. Seasonality and any other March changes are uncontrolled.

**Claim 4: selection, not a lever.**
Customers who adopt integrations are probably more engaged or larger from the start, so the 2× gap may reflect who chooses integrations, not what integrations do. If the gap is entirely selection, forcing non-adopters onto integrations yields roughly zero LTV gain. The claim "pushing every customer onto integrations will double LTV" is a causal prediction that has never been tested, so it is `[S]` dressed as `[K]`. The summary also gives no n, no time window, and no definition of LTV.

**Claim 7: the p-value does not survive the 20 tests.**
- Twenty tests with a 0.05 threshold give a 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64% chance of at least one false positive under the null. The chance that the *best* of 20 null results lands at p ≤ 0.04 is 1 − 0.96²⁰ ≈ 56%.
- Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.04 × 20 = 0.80. Holm gives the same conclusion for this metric.
- The word "significantly" implies the result survives correction, and it does not. This is the most likely HARKing pattern in the set: one metric was selected from twenty after the fact.
- Record it as a near-miss, not a win. Do not raise the threshold to rescue it.

## Claims that survive

- **Claim 3 (NPS 31 → 44) survives sampling error.** My estimate, assuming typical promoter and detractor shares because you didn't supply them: each NPS has a standard error of about 2 points, so the difference has a standard error of about 3 points and a 95% interval of roughly +7 to +19. The rise is unlikely to be noise. Two open checks remain: response rate (nonresponse can move NPS by more than sampling error) and whether the surveyed population changed (the new annual-plan cohort may be a different mix). Same vendor and same question are both good.
- **Claim 8 (78% gross margin) is credible if the audited lines are as named.** Confirm that revenue in the denominator matches the $3.0M figure, that COGS includes hosting and support, and that the audit period matches FY2026.

## Cross-claim finding the summary omits

Headcount grew 46% (28 → 41) while revenue grew 25%. Revenue per head fell from about $85.7k to $73.2k, a 15% decline. An investor will compute this from the same numbers, so it is better disclosed than discovered.
