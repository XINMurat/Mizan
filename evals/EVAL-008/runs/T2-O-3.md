## Failures, with mechanism and magnitude

**Claim 2 (`[R]`).** The stated 40% does not follow from the stated figures, which give 25%. Either a different base or window was used, or the figure is a different metric. A plausible candidate is bookings or billings. Annual plans billed upfront inflate billings in the period they are sold, so the 40% may reflect cash timing, not revenue. This is a hypothesis to check, not a finding. The next step is to state the metric and its definition.

**Claim 7 (`[KKE]`, would be `[R]` under correction).** Twenty tests at α = 0.05 give a 64% chance of at least one false positive under the null (1 − 0.95²⁰). The p = 0.04 result would need to pass p ≤ 0.0025 under Bonferroni, and the first rank under Benjamini–Hochberg at q = 0.05 also requires 0.0025. It fails both. The document also reports no effect size, no confidence interval, no direction, and no record of which metric was the pre-specified hypothesis. The number of tests may exceed 20 if other metrics were tried across the year.

**Claim 5 (`[KKE]`).** Three mechanisms could produce the churn drop without any change in individual behavior:

1. **Denominator composition.** If annual subscribers do not appear as monthly churners during their term, the blended monthly rate falls as the annual share of the active base rises. Holding monthly-plan churn at 3.1%, a fall to 2.2% requires an annual share of about 1 − 2.2/3.1 = **29% of the active base**. If the annual share is below that, composition cannot explain the whole drop. The company knows this share and should report it.
2. **Renewal cliff.** Annual customers churn at renewal, roughly 12 months after March. The summary contains no annual-cohort renewal data, so the "annual plans reduce churn" claim is untested. Lock-in is not retention.
3. **Concurrent changes.** Onboarding changes (claim 7) and the June move overlap the same window. Temporal sequence is not attribution.

Note also that 60% refers to *new* customers, not the base.

**Claim 4 (4a `[KKE]`, 4b `[Y]`).** Integration adoption is self-selected. Teams that connect integrations are likely more engaged, larger, or more committed before they connect. The 2× gap is consistent with that selection alone. The doubling projection assumes the effect transfers to customers who would not adopt integrations on their own, who are plausibly lower-engagement. The required controls are LTV for adopters vs. matched non-adopters using *pre-adoption* engagement, and ideally a randomized integration prompt.

**Claim 3 (`[KKE]`, credited in part).** With n ≈ 1,100 per wave and NPS in the typical range, the standard error on each NPS is roughly ±2 points, so the standard error of the 13-point change is about ±3. The change is well outside sampling noise. It is not yet attributable to product, because the response rate, the respondent mix (the post-March cohort is about 60% annual), and the survey's timing are unreported.

## Questions with recommendations

- **Which growth metric is the 40%?** Recommend restating it as recognized revenue and noting billings separately. If the company prefers billings, it must say so on the page, or investors will recompute it.
- **Can you share the annual-plan share of the active base and March-cohort renewal rates?** Recommend pulling them before the round. Cost if wrong: a delayed data pull, not a lost claim.

**Summary:** Of eight claims, one is refuted as stated (growth), one survives in part with a real signal (NPS), two are plausible pending sources (headcount, gross margin), and four rest on controls that have not run (churn, LTV, activation, and the NPS attribution). The strongest-looking figures (2× LTV, 29% churn drop, p = 0.04) are the ones most affected by the same denominator and selection problems. The summary should not go to investors in its current form.
