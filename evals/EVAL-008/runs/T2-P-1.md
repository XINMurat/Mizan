## Failures

**Claim 2: Revenue growth, `[R]` Reddedildi / Refuted**
($3.0M − $2.4M) / $2.4M = 25%, not 40%. The error overstates growth by 15 points, or 60% relative to the true figure. A 40% growth rate would imply revenue of $3.36M.
- Possible mechanisms: an arithmetic slip, a different base (ARR, bookings, or a different period), or a figure carried over from a draft.
- **Next step:** Find which revenue definition produces 40%, label it explicitly, and restate the headline as 25% unless the source reconciles. Do not present 40% alongside $2.4M and $3.0M.

**Claim 4: LTV doubling, `[Y]` Yanıltıcı / Misleading**
The 2× figure compares customers who adopted integrations with those who did not. Adopters are probably more engaged to begin with, so the gap is partly selection. The claim then goes further than the data in two ways:
- It treats the observed gap as the effect of adoption.
- It assumes the effect holds for the customers you would push, who are presumably less engaged and so likely to show a smaller effect.

Even a true causal effect would not double LTV across the base.
- **Next step:** Compare adopters and non-adopters matched on pre-adoption engagement, or better, randomly push a holdout of non-adopters to integrations. Lock the threshold before measuring. As a proposal: uplift ≥1.5× in the randomized group → `[K]`; uplift ≤1.1× → `[R]`; in between → `[H]`.

**Claim 5: Churn decline, `[KKE]` plus `[Y]` on attribution**
Monthly churn is measured across the active base. Annual-plan customers cannot churn monthly. They leave only at renewal. So adding them to the denominator mechanically lowers the monthly churn rate.
- Quantitatively: if the monthly-plan churn rate is 3.1% and annual customers are a fraction f of the active base, the blended rate is 3.1% × (1 − f). A blended 2.2% implies f ≈ 29%. If annual customers are about 29% of the active base, the entire drop is arithmetic. The 60% figure applies to *new* customers, and the active base is mostly older monthly customers, so f is probably lower. The mechanical share is still likely to be a large part of the 0.9-point drop.
- Annual customers who joined after March have not reached renewal yet. Their retention cannot have been observed. The 2.2% therefore reflects the monthly base only.
- "After" is a timing fact, not a causal one.
- **Next step:** Report churn for monthly-plan customers alone, and state the annual share of the active base. Report renewal rates for annual cohorts when the first renewals arrive, not before. Until then, the attribution to annual plans is `[H]` at best.

**Claim 7: Activation, `[Y]` Yanıltıcı / Misleading**
Twenty metrics were tested and one was reported as significant at p = 0.04. With 20 independent tests and no true effects, the chance of at least one p < 0.05 is 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64%. Bonferroni correction requires p < 0.0025, and Holm would also reject 0.04 if it was the most significant of the 20. It was not preregistered, and the threshold appears to have been chosen after the fact.
- **Next step:** Name activation as the single primary metric now, and confirm it on a fresh cohort with the threshold locked in advance. Until then, report it as an exploratory result. If it was the designated primary metric from the start, say so and show the pre-test plan. That would change the verdict.

## Survivals and conditional passes

- **Claim 1, `[H]`:** Headcount is a plausible count. Cite the payroll source. It becomes `[K]` once the source is named and checked.
- **Claim 3, `[KKE]`:** The NPS rise is probably real at the sampling level. Assuming plausible promoter and detractor shares, the standard error of each NPS is about 1.9 points and the difference has SE ≈ 2.6, so 13 points is roughly 5 SE. This is a rough check, since the distribution isn't given. The unchecked confounds are response rate, whether the respondent mix changed (annual-plan customers may answer differently), and whether the same customers are compared or different ones. **Next step:** Report response rates and a same-segment comparison. If the rise holds within segments, it upgrades to `[K]`.
- **Claim 6, `[K]`:** The office move is a fact. It is not evidence for anything in this summary. Don't let it appear as an explanation for the NPS, churn, or activation changes without a test.

Confirm that the 78% uses FY2026 statements, that the audit is complete (FY2026 audited statements may not yet exist for a round in progress), and how hosting and support costs are classified in COGS. Those choices can move gross margin by several points.

## Document-level finding

The summary as a whole is `[Y]`. It is built to imply a growth-and-retention story in which headline growth is overstated (claim 2), the retention gain may be partly accounting (claim 5), the product lever may be selection (claim 4), and the only "significant" result is a multiple-comparison artifact (claim 7). The one line that might have been strongest, the NPS rise, is also the one that would have needed the most confound checking before headlining.
