# Review: Checkout Outage Postmortem, 2026-09-18

**Bottom line:** Only the timestamps and the error-rate measurement are reasonably supported. The root cause is asserted rather than established. The "no customer data affected" claim has a scope problem. "This will not happen again" should be removed. The duration contradicts its own timestamps.

## Claim-by-claim

**1. "Paged at 14:05."** *Partially supported.* This needs a source, such as a pager log entry. Without one it is a recollection. It is also worth stating the two-minute gap between the 14:03 start and the page, and whether any automated alert fired before the human page.

**2. "Outage lasted 47 minutes, from 14:03 to 14:52."** *Not supported as written.* 14:03 to 14:52 is 49 minutes. The 47-minute figure matches 14:03 to 14:50, which is the rollback time. Either the end time or the duration is wrong, or they measure different things. The postmortem also never defines "outage." Was it a threshold on error rate, a customer-visible failure, or a status-page declaration? The definition matters for every downstream number.

**3. "Error rate peaked at 38% (load balancer logs)."** *Adequately supported as a measurement,* with caveats. The source is named. However, LB logs may miss client-side failures, retries that succeed, and requests that never reach the LB. Give the sampling interval and say what "peak" means, such as a one-minute average.

**4. "Root cause: the cache eviction change in 8.2. Rolled back at 14:50, errors stopped shortly after."** *Not supported as a root cause.* This is the central problem:
- **Temporal correlation only.** The rollback preceded the recovery, but nothing shows the change caused the errors. There is no reproduction, no mechanism (for example, evicted entries causing a specific failure path), and no trace linking the change to failed requests.
- **Confounder not addressed.** Claim 6 says the DNS provider's incident was resolved at 14:49, one minute before the rollback. The postmortem never mentions this. Recovery could have been caused by DNS, by the rollback, or by both. The document does not show which.
- **"Shortly after" is vague.** The exact time error rates returned to baseline is needed. If errors dropped at 14:49, the rollback cannot be credited.
- **Deploy timing is missing.** When did 8.2 deploy relative to 14:03? A deploy before the onset would strengthen the case. A deploy long before onset would weaken it.
- **Other changes.** Did the rollback revert anything besides the cache change?

A defensible statement would be: "8.2 is the leading suspect; rollback coincided with recovery; the DNS incident overlapped and has not been ruled out." The current wording is a firm conclusion the evidence does not yet support.

**5. "No customer data was affected: we checked the three services we suspected might have written partial orders."** *Not supported.* The check covers only the services someone suspected, so it cannot establish absence of impact. A complete check would list every service that writes order or payment state and was reachable during the window, then verify each one, for example by reconciling orders against payment records. Also state who performed the check and what it compared against. "Did not find" is the supportable claim.

**6. "The DNS provider's status page records an incident, resolved at 14:49."** *Supported as a fact about the status page,* which is a named external source. It does not establish that DNS affected this service. The postmortem needs to say whether the company's own resolution or connection errors show the DNS issue, and why it was ruled out or included. Provider status times can also be imprecise.

**7. "New alert fires at 14:06 when replayed against this incident's logs."** *Supported as a replay result, with two problems:*
- **Overfitting.** The threshold (5% for 2 minutes) was chosen after seeing this incident, so the replay tests the alert against the data that shaped it. The postmortem needs the false-positive rate over a comparable period of normal operation.
- **Little detection gain.** The page already happened at 14:05, so an alert at 14:06 would not have improved detection here. The claim sounds like an improvement but mainly shows the alert works on this data.

**8. "This will not happen again."** *Not supported and should be removed.* No system guarantees this. Replace it with the specific preventive measures, each with an owner and a date. For example: a pre-deploy check for the cache eviction path, a canary stage, or a DNS-failure runbook. Then claim only the risk each one reduces.

**9. "The team held a retrospective on Friday."** *Process fact, not a finding.* It is unverifiable as stated. 2026-09-18 is a Friday, so "Friday" is ambiguous. It does not support any conclusion and should not be used as evidence of thoroughness. Name the date and attendees if relevant.

## What the postmortem is missing

- **Customer impact:** number of failed requests or orders, and affected users or revenue, not just peak error rate.
- **Timeline with sources:** deploy time, first error, first alert, page, diagnosis steps, rollback decision, recovery.
- **Time-to-mitigate analysis:** the time from page to rollback decision, and why the cache change was not identified sooner.
- **Contributing factors** beyond the trigger, such as test coverage for the eviction path or the lack of an earlier alert.
- **Action items** with owners and deadlines.
