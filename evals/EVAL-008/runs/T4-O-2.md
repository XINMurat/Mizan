## Findings in detail

**Root cause (4a) is the weakest load-bearing claim.** The postmortem has two candidate causes that the timeline cannot separate:

- **Rival hypothesis A:** release 8.2's cache eviction change caused the errors.
- **Rival hypothesis B:** the DNS provider incident caused them, and errors fell as DNS recovered.

The DNS incident resolved at 14:49. The rollback happened at 14:50. Both events precede "errors stopped shortly after," so the rollback is confounded with DNS recovery. The postmortem treats the rollback as the perturbation that identified the cause, but it did not test whether DNS recovery alone would have produced the same curve.

The symmetric control: segment the per-minute error rate by (a) instances running 8.2 versus not, (b) requests that touched the DNS-resolved dependency versus not. If 8.2 is the cause, errors should concentrate on 8.2 instances and should not fall at 14:49 when DNS recovered. If DNS is the cause, errors should track DNS-dependent calls and fall at 14:49 regardless of the rollback. Until that control runs, the root-cause claim stays `[KKE]`.

**The 45-minute gap is the biggest omission.** Paged at 14:05, rolled back at 14:50. The postmortem never explains what happened in those 45 minutes. That is the missing card: diagnostic time to rollback, which is the actual lever on outage duration. Did the team suspect 8.2 early? Was rollback considered and deferred? Was the DNS incident visible in the team's tooling? A postmortem that omits this has not explained the 47 minutes, only recorded them.

**The data-integrity claim (5) needs a scope and a method.** A checkout outage with 38% errors can leave payments captured without orders, or orders without payments. The defensible statement is: "We reconciled payment captures against order records for [list of all services on the checkout write path] over [window] and found [N] mismatches." Currently it says "three suspected services" and then "no customer data affected."

**The alert (7) is a real improvement with a wrong justification.** Adding it is fine. Presenting it as faster detection is not supported: its replay fires after the page that actually happened. The honest claim is that it would have caught the incident about as fast as the existing path, and that its false-positive rate is unknown.

## What this postmortem's format cannot show

- Contributing factors: why 8.2 passed review and canary, and why the cache eviction change had no load test that would have exposed it.
- Detection latency decomposed: incident start, detection, page, acknowledgement, diagnosis, rollback.
- Customer impact in orders and currency lost, not only error percentages.
- Concurrency: the 8.2 rollback and the DNS recovery coincided within one minute. That conjunction is itself a finding about how the incident was read.

## Domain probe (checklist item 12)

Scenarios the postmortem should have been tested against, none supplied by a domain owner yet (`supplied_by: none`): a rollback during a concurrent dependency outage; a partial write during a rollback; the on-call engineer unreachable for the first 10 minutes; a second deploy during the window. Each is either handled by a documented procedure, or it is a finding. The postmortem records none of them.

## Next steps, ordered by criticality × impact/effort

1. **Fix the duration arithmetic and confirm the start time from the pager and LB logs.** Effort: minutes. Impact: every downstream timing claim depends on it.
2. **Run the 8.2-versus-DNS segmented error analysis** (per-minute, per-instance-version, per-dependency). Effort: a query. Impact: it decides whether the root cause stands.
3. **Scope the data-integrity claim** to the full write path and state the reconciliation method. Effort: moderate. Impact: the highest-stakes claim in the document.
4. **Explain the 45-minute diagnosis window** from the on-call timeline. Effort: an interview. Impact: it is where the outage duration was actually decided.
5. **Replace "will not happen again"** with a falsifiable prevention claim. Example: "A cache-eviction load test in CI fails on the 8.2 change at ≥X% error rate; kill condition: test passes on the 8.2 change under the same load." Effort: low. Impact: it converts a promise into a claim the next retrospective can check.
6. **Measure the alert's false-positive rate** on the prior 4–8 weeks of error-rate logs and record the threshold's date relative to this incident. Effort: moderate. Impact: determines whether the alert is safe to ship.
7. **Link the DNS status page and state whether this system depends on that resolution path.** Effort: minutes.
