# Review of FY2026 Growth Summary

**Overall:** The summary contains an arithmetic error in a headline number, two causal claims that the data cannot support, a statistical claim that fails once multiple comparisons are considered, and a mix of metrics with no definitions. Only the headcount figure and, with caveats, the NPS difference look adequately supported.

---

### 1. Headcount grew from 28 to 41
**Verdict: Adequately supported as a count, but incomplete.**
- The figures are plausible and easy to verify against payroll.
- Missing: whether these are end-of-period or average headcount, and whether contractors are included.
- Context matters: headcount grew 46% while (per claim 2) revenue grew 25%. That is a meaningful efficiency signal that the summary does not address, and investors will ask about it.

### 2. Revenue grew from $2.4M to $3.0M, a 40% increase
**Verdict: Incorrect.**
- ($3.0M − $2.4M) / $2.4M = **25%**, not 40%.
- This is the most serious problem in the document because it is the headline growth number. Either the revenue figures or the percentage is wrong. If 40% came from a different base, period, or metric (ARR vs. recognized revenue, a different cohort), that must be stated.
- Also unspecified: whether "revenue" is recognized revenue, ARR, or bookings. Annual prepaid plans (claim 5) can make bookings and recognized revenue diverge substantially.

### 3. NPS rose from 31 to 44 (same vendor, same question; n = 1,120 and 1,085)
**Verdict: Probably real as a measured change, but the claim needs qualification.**
- Strengths: same instrument, same question, and reasonably large samples. With n ≈ 1,100, the sampling error on each NPS is roughly ±3–4 points, so a 13-point change is likely larger than noise.
- Gaps:
  - No response rate or number surveyed. Non-response bias can move NPS a lot.
  - Survey timing and who was surveyed (all customers, active customers, only recent ones) are not stated.
  - Population mix may have changed, e.g., more annual-plan customers (claim 5), which could shift sentiment.
  - No significance test or confidence interval is reported.
- NPS is a sentiment measure. It does not show that anything in the list caused the change, and the summary does not claim it does, which is appropriate.

### 4. Integration users have twice the LTV, so pushing everyone onto integrations will double LTV
**Verdict: Not supported. This is a causal claim built on an observational correlation.**
- **Selection bias / confounding:** Customers who adopt integrations are probably larger, more technically mature, more engaged, or more committed. Those traits independently predict LTV. The 2× gap may reflect who adopts integrations, not what integrations do.
- **Reverse causation:** Long-lived, high-value customers have more time to adopt integrations.
- **Extrapolation:** Even if the effect were causal for current adopters, it does not follow that forcing non-adopters onto integrations would produce the same effect. The people who have not adopted may be those for whom integrations are least useful.
- **Arithmetic:** Doubling LTV for the whole base is only implied if every customer's LTV doubles, which is a strong assumption, not a result.
- What would support it: a randomized encouragement or onboarding experiment, or a matched-cohort analysis with pre-adoption trends. Even then, the effect would need to be estimated for the marginal customers who would be pushed.

### 5. Monthly churn fell from 3.1% to 2.2% after annual plans launched in March; 60% of new customers chose annual
**Verdict: Not supported as a retention improvement. The metric is confounded by the plan change itself.**
- **Metric definition breaks.** Annual customers are billed once a year and cannot churn monthly. Including them in a "monthly churn" rate, or excluding them, changes the denominator and population. The metric is not comparable before and after March.
- **Lock-in effect.** An annual customer cannot leave for up to 12 months. Churn is deferred, not prevented. The reduction in monthly churn in the first months after launch will mechanically look good and will reverse at renewal. The true retention effect will only be visible at the first annual renewal cohort, which may not yet exist.
- **Mix shift.** Only new customers (60%) chose annual. Existing monthly customers are presumably unchanged, so the blended rate depends on the changing mix of who is in each group.
- **No control or baseline.** A before/after comparison in one company, with other changes happening in the same window (onboarding changes from claim 7, integrations, NPS-relevant product work), cannot isolate the effect of annual plans.
- **Seasonality and sample size.** No period lengths, customer counts, or confidence intervals are given. A 0.9-point drop could be noise if the base is small.
- What would support it: cohort retention curves by plan type, monthly-plan churn tracked separately for the same period, and annual-plan renewal rates once they are observable.

### 6. We moved to a new office in June
**Verdict: Factual statement, no causal claim, and irrelevant to the metrics.**
- Fine as stated. It is worth checking whether it is included to imply that the office contributed to any metric. If so, nothing in the summary supports that. Its presence is also a reminder that the document is not restricted to the metrics that matter.

### 7. Tested changes against 20 onboarding metrics; activation improved significantly (p = 0.04)
**Verdict: Not supported. Multiple comparisons invalidate the significance claim.**
- Testing 20 metrics at α = 0.05 gives roughly a 64% chance (1 − 0.95²⁰) that at least one shows p < 0.05 even if nothing works. A p = 0.04 result is the expected kind of false positive from this process.
- A Bonferroni-corrected threshold would be 0.0025. Even a less strict false-discovery-rate correction would likely not keep this result.
- Also unclear:
  - Was this a randomized A/B test, or a before/after comparison?
  - Was the activation metric chosen before the test (pre-registered)?
  - What is the effect size and confidence interval? "Improved significantly" conflates statistical significance with practical importance.
  - How many onboarding changes were tested, and was the 20 metrics count the same as the number of variants?
- What would support it: a pre-registered primary metric, a holdout group, a reported effect size with CI, and a replication or confirmation test.

### 8. Gross margin is 78%, computed from the audited financial statements
**Verdict: Plausible but needs verification of the label and the definition.**
- Gross margin is not usually a line item in audited statements. It is derived as (revenue − cost of revenue) / revenue. An audit confirms the underlying figures, not the derived metric or its definition.
- Specify what sits in cost of revenue (hosting, support, payment fees, implementation, customer success). Definitions vary widely and can move margin by 10+ points.
- Confirm the audit status. If FY2026 is still in progress, audited statements for it cannot exist yet. This may be a reviewed or management-accounts figure.
- Check consistency: if revenue is $3.0M, the implied gross profit is about $2.34M.

**Before this goes to investors:** correct the growth figure, define every metric (revenue type, churn population, NPS sampling), and either remove the causal claims or present them as hypotheses to be tested. The LTV and churn claims in particular are the kind of statements a diligence team will challenge first.
