# Review of the FY2026 Growth Summary

**Summary:** One claim contains an arithmetic error (revenue growth). Two causal claims (integrations → LTV, annual plans → churn) are unsupported by the evidence presented. One claim (activation) is a likely false positive from uncorrected multiple testing. The NPS change is probably real as a descriptive difference but is not evidence of anything else. Headcount, office move, and gross margin are mostly fine but need definitions or verification.

---

### 1. Headcount grew from 28 to 41
**Mostly supported, but undefined.** Is this end-of-period, average, or full-time only? Are contractors included? Growth is 46%, which matters alongside claim 2: headcount grew far faster than revenue (see below). Investors will ask about revenue per employee.

### 2. Revenue grew from $2.4M to $3.0M, a 40% increase
**Arithmetic error.** $3.0M / $2.4M = 1.25, a **25%** increase, not 40%. Either the 40% comes from a different metric (ARR, bookings, a different period) or the figures are wrong. This must be resolved before the round.

Also undefined: is this recognized revenue, ARR, or billings? Claim 5 introduces annual prepaid plans, which can make billings and recognized revenue diverge substantially. Mixing these definitions can inflate apparent growth.

### 3. NPS rose from 31 to 44 (same vendor, same question, n = 1,120 and n = 1,085)
**Adequately supported as a descriptive change, with caveats.** Samples of roughly 1,100 per wave are large enough that a 13-point NPS gap is unlikely to be pure sampling noise, assuming random sampling. Same vendor and wording is the right design for comparison.

Missing: response rates and who responded. If response rates changed, or if the survey population shifted (for example, a larger share of annual-plan customers, who may differ systematically), the change may reflect composition rather than sentiment. Timing of the survey relative to product changes is also not stated. The NPS rise should be described as a change in reported score, not as evidence that any particular initiative worked.

### 4. Integration users have twice the LTV, so pushing everyone onto integrations will double LTV
**Unsupported. Clear selection-bias problem.** Customers who adopt integrations are probably more engaged, more mature, larger, or more technically invested. Those traits would raise LTV regardless of integrations. The comparison is observational, with no control for these confounders and no experiment.

The further leap is also unsupported even if the association were causal. A doubling for current adopters does not imply the same effect for customers who would be pushed onto integrations, who are likely less motivated and may never use them. Diminishing returns and heterogeneous effects are both likely.

To support this claim, you would need a randomized encouragement test (e.g., randomly prompting some accounts to set up integrations) and measurement of LTV uplift among the prompted group, including the marginal customers.

### 5. Monthly churn fell from 3.1% to 2.2% after annual plans launched in March; 60% of new customers chose annual
**Unsupported. The metric is not comparable across the change.** This is the most serious methodological issue in the document.

- **Denominator/mix shift:** Annual-plan customers do not churn monthly in the same way; they churn at renewal. As the share of annual customers grows, measured *monthly* churn mechanically falls even if no individual customer behaves differently. The 60% figure is direct evidence that the population has shifted.
- **Lock-in, not retention:** Annual plans defer churn. Customers signed in March–June cannot leave until their renewal, so the lower rate in recent months reflects contract structure rather than improved retention.
- **No control or cohort analysis:** A before/after comparison of an aggregate metric with no comparison group. The office move (June), onboarding changes (claim 7), and any other changes overlap the same period.
- **Immature data:** Most annual contracts started in March have not reached renewal, so annual-plan retention is essentially unobserved.
- **Seasonality:** Not addressed.

To support the claim, report monthly-plan churn by cohort before and after March, and annual-plan renewal rates once renewals occur. Also note that a 0.9-point drop in a churn rate is material only if the denominator is consistent.

### 6. We moved to a new office in June
**Fact, not a performance claim. Supported as stated.** It is irrelevant to growth metrics unless the company is implying an effect. Including it without a claim suggests padding; if it is cited as a factor in any result, there is no evidence for that.

### 7. Tested 20 onboarding metrics; activation improved significantly (p = 0.04)
**Not supported. Multiple comparisons and selective reporting.**

- Testing 20 metrics at α = 0.05 gives roughly a 64% chance (1 − 0.95²⁰) of at least one false positive if all were null and independent. A single p = 0.04 is what you'd expect from that process by chance.
- Under Bonferroni correction the threshold is 0.0025; under Benjamini–Hochberg, the result would likely not survive either.
- The word "significantly" conflates statistical significance with practical importance. No effect size, baseline activation rate, confidence interval, or sample size is given.
- It is unclear whether this was a pre-registered A/B test or post-hoc analysis (peeking at results or choosing the best of 20 would both inflate the result).
- Whether the 20 metrics were all reported is unknown.

To support this, pre-specify activation as the primary metric, report the effect size with a CI, and replicate in a fresh holdout.

### 8. Gross margin is 78%, computed from audited financial statements
**Probably supported, but verify two things.**
- **Audit timing:** If FY2026 is still in progress, the statements cannot be audited yet. Confirm the period is closed and audited.
- **Definition:** Which costs are in cost of revenue (hosting, support, customer success, payment processing, amortized integration costs)? Definitions differ widely and can move margin by several points. The audit opinion covers the financial statements, not whether the 78% follows a standard definition.

---

### Overall

| # | Claim | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Headcount 28→41 | Supported; define the measure |
| 2 | Revenue +40% | **Wrong** (+25%); define revenue |
| 3 | NPS 31→44 | Supported as descriptive; check response and composition |
| 4 | Integrations double LTV | **Unsupported** (selection bias, no causal test) |
| 5 | Annual plans cut churn | **Unsupported** (denominator/lock-in artifact, no control) |
| 6 | Office move | Fact; irrelevant as a performance claim |
| 7 | Activation improved (p = 0.04) | **Not supported** (multiple comparisons) |
| 8 | 78% gross margin | Likely supported; verify audit timing and definition |

**Investor-relevant implications:** Revenue growth (25%) is well below headcount growth (46%), which suggests declining efficiency and may be the real story. The churn improvement is likely partly an accounting artifact of the annual-plan shift. The most defensible headline metrics are NPS (with caveats) and gross margin (once verified). Before the round, correct the growth figure, define revenue and churn consistently (ideally cohort-based), and remove or qualify the causal claims.
