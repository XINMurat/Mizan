## Claim 1: Median time to first response fell from 4.2 hours to 40 seconds

**Formal claim:** Time to first response decreased by 98.4% (4.2h → 40s).

**Evidence:** Ticketing-system timestamps for all 38,000 tickets in the 10 weeks before and after launch, pulled by data platform team.

**Assessment:** Direct evidence. Timestamps are deterministic; no interpretation layer. Sample (N=38,000) is large. Before/after measurement is clear. Source is stated to be independent (data platform team, not bot team). The 98.4% reduction meets any reasonable threshold for material improvement.

**Tier:** `[K]` Kanıtlanmış | Proven. Direct evidence supports the claim; source cited; threshold met.

---

## Claim 2: Customers prefer the bot to human agents

**Formal claim:** Customer preference favors bot over human agents.

**Evidence:** Product team's reading of 30 chat transcripts.

**Assessment:** 
- Sample size (N=30) is small for a population preference claim.
- Measurement method ("reading") is subjective interpretation; no stated coding scheme, inter-rater reliability, or blinding.
- Selection mechanism unknown (random draw, convenience sample, or curated?).
- No explicit preference measurement (e.g., "On a scale of 1–5, prefer bot: __ ").
- Vulnerable to confirmation bias: product team has incentive to see preference in favor of their product.

**Counter-example search:** High potential. Subjective reading without systematic process is likely to miss or reframe negative reactions as neutral. The method cannot separate strong preference from polite toleration in transcripts.

**Tier:** `[H]` Makul Hipotez | Plausible hypothesis. Theoretical grounding exists (product team did read transcripts), but empirical support falls below the threshold for a proven preference claim. Requires systematic measurement (e.g., random sample with blinded coding, explicit customer rating, hold-out control group).

**Next step:** Obtain explicit preference metric from a random sample of 500+ customers post-interaction, or run A/B test where customers are asked to rate bot vs. human.

## Claim 3: The bot resolves 70% of tickets on its own

**Formal claim:** The bot achieves independent resolution on 70% of tickets.

**Evidence:** Vendor dashboard metric, defined as "resolved" = customer closed the chat window.

**Assessment:**
- Critical issue: conflation of closure with resolution.
- Closure behavior does not prove resolution outcome:
  - Customer closes window ≠ problem solved. Alternative outcomes: frustration, timeout, context switch.
  - No independent follow-up (e.g., "Was your issue resolved?" survey, repeat-contact rate, outcome verification).
- Vendor has direct financial incentive to inflate resolution rate in reported dashboard.
- No third-party validation mentioned.

**Verdict mechanism:** Vendor's own dashboard (self-judged arbiter). Biased.

**Tier:** `[Y]` Yanıltıcı | Misleading. Technically contains true data (windows were closed) but frames it to imply outcome (resolution) that the evidence does not support. The 70% metric is a false proxy. A customer closing a window is necessary but not sufficient for resolution.

**Next step:** Independent verification — either (a) post-chat survey asking "Was your issue resolved?", or (b) 7-day repeat-contact rate by outcome (customers who closed window without resolution will return), or (c) spot-check of 100 "resolved" tickets to confirm problem actually solved.

## Claim 4: The bot reduced repeat contacts (9.1% vs 11.4%, CI −3.0 to −1.6 points)

**Formal claim:** Bot-first routing reduced 7-day repeat contacts by 2.3 percentage points (absolute).

**Evidence:** Randomized test; N=5,000 customers; 6-week period; random assignment to bot-first vs human-first; metric computed by analytics team (independent from bot team).

**Assessment:**
- Randomization present: reduces confounds.
- Sample size adequate for observed effect size.
- Metric clear and objective: repeat contacts within 7 days (verifiable from ticketing system).
- Confidence interval: −3.0 to −1.6 points; excludes zero; statistically significant at p<.05.
- Arbiter: runtime (ticketing system clock, deterministic). Independent arbiter (analytics team, not bot team).
- Test pre-specified elements stated (6 weeks, 5,000 customers, 7-day window).

**Counter-example search:** Very unlikely. Randomized design with objective outcome measure resists most confounds.

**One note:** The test measures repeat contact reduction but does not directly measure customer satisfaction or problem resolution. However, repeat contact *is* a legitimate proxy for resolution quality (unresolved issues drive repeat contacts).

**Tier:** `[K]` Kanıtlanmış | Proven. Direct evidence from randomized test with clear metrics and independent computation. Threshold met. Arbiter is deterministic.

## Claim 5: It will cut support headcount costs by a third next year

**Formal claim:** Support headcount will be reduced by 33% within one year of launch.

**Evidence:** Projection from the vendor's sales deck.

**Assessment:**
- This is a prediction, not evidence of outcome.
- Vendor incentive: sales team benefits directly if projection appears credible. Projection bias is structural, not personal.
- No historical track record of this vendor's projection accuracy given.
- No preregistration of the metric, stopping rule, or measurement plan.
- Claim is prospective (future); no data yet exist to test it.
- Projection ≠ preregistered hypothesis. Projections are typically optimistic; actual outcomes are often 30–50% lower than vendor projections in enterprise software.

**Testability:** This claim *will* be testable next year if actual headcount is measured. But today, it is not.

**Tier:** `[S]` Spekülatif | Speculative. Interesting; not currently testable against real data. When next year arrives, this becomes a measured claim and should be re-audited.

**Next step (deferred to next year):** Measure actual support headcount in month 13 post-launch. Create a registry entry with preregistered kill condition: if headcount reduction < 20%, the claim is refuted; if 20–30%, mark `[KKE]` (competing explanations, e.g., hiring freeze, team churn); if ≥ 33%, mark `[K]`.

## Claim 6: Agents are happier since launch

**Formal claim:** Support agent job satisfaction or well-being improved post-launch.

**Evidence:** (1) No survey conducted. (2) Attrition this quarter was lower. (3) Hiring freeze started the same month as launch.

**Assessment:**
- Direct measurement missing: No survey of agent satisfaction, no morale assessment.
- Indirect proxy used: attrition rate.
- **Critical confound:** Hiring freeze started simultaneously.
  - Hiring freeze mechanically reduces attrition: fewer people leave because roles are frozen, uncertainty is high, or external job market is less attractive (common pattern when organizations signal contraction).
  - Cannot isolate bot causation from freeze causation with this evidence.
  - Example: If agent attrition *without* bot but *with* freeze typically falls 15%, and it fell 15% here, the bot effect is indistinguishable from zero.
- No baseline provided: What was attrition before? How much lower is "lower"?
- Attrition is a poor proxy for happiness (it measures retention behavior, not satisfaction).

**Counter-example: Very easy to construct.** Agent happiness is not caused by the bot; it is caused by the hiring freeze creating job security anxiety, which reduces job-switching.

**Arbiter:** Author-judged (team selected attrition as evidence after launch). No independent measurement; no preregistered threshold.

**Tier:** `[Y]` Yanıltıcı | Misleading. Claim states "agents are happier" but provides no direct evidence of happiness. Attrition is a confounded proxy: the hiring freeze, not the bot, explains the attrition change. Framing the finding as evidence of agent happiness is unsupported.

**Next step:** To prove the claim, one of the following:
- (a) Survey 80%+ of agents with standardized job-satisfaction scale (e.g., two-sided Likert), completed pre-launch and post-launch, with >70% response rate.
- (b) Establish a control group: compare attrition of agents in teams where bot was not deployed (during the same freeze period), confirming bot teams have *lower* attrition than control.
- (c) Measure attrition trajectory: does attrition in bot teams continue to improve *after* the hiring freeze ends? If bot were the driver, improvement should persist.

## Missing Cards (Format Limitations)

The board summary omits:
- Baseline attrition rates and historical trends (needed to contextualize claim 6).
- Customer churn rate (are fewer tickets due to bot improvement or customer loss?).
- Error rate or escalation rate (does bot performance degrade on complex tickets?).
- Agent workload distribution (does bot shift work to agents only, hurting their time?).
- Cost-benefit analysis (response-time gain vs. system maintenance and bot training costs).
- Failure modes or edge cases (when does bot harm exceed help?).

## Coverage & HARKing Statement

This audit is **retrospective.** The board summary reports results after launch; threshold-locking decisions made beforehand are not visible. The project could not preregister metrics (they ran the project, then audited it). This means:

- Threshold shopping is a risk (results near a boundary may reflect threshold choice).
- Surprising positives (claims 1 and 4) were not preregistered with symmetric controls; control 3 and 6 are notable findings of *weak* design rather than strong evidence.
- The audit assumes the stated evidence sources are truthful (data platform pulled 38,000 timestamps, analytics team computed the CI) but cannot verify source quality.

For ongoing tracking, a registry entry should be created for each `[H]` or `[KKE]` claim, with preregistered thresholds, to prevent this cycle from repeating.
