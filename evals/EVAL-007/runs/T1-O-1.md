# Audit Report: Support Chatbot Board Summary

**Auditor's Declaration**: This is a retrospective audit of claims presented for board sign-off. The team lead's confidence is not evidence — each claim is assessed independently against available evidence. Selection bias is likely: only positive results are presented; no negative or null findings appear. **HARKing status**: High. The claims were selected after launch outcomes were known.

---

## Coverage & Scope

Six claims about a support chatbot are audited: response time, customer preference, resolution rate, repeat contacts, cost projection, and agent satisfaction. Evidence sources range from direct in-house data (claim 4) to vendor projections (claim 5) to confounded observational data (claim 6). One claim (claim 2) relies on subjective reading of a small sample. The audit covers all claims as presented; evidence accessibility is noted in each entry.

**Missing from this summary**: (1) failure cases — no customer complaints, escalation rates, or cases where the bot worsened outcomes; (2) temporal stability — do these metrics persist over weeks 8+ or degrade?; (3) interaction effects — does the bot harm the agent experience even if aggregate metrics improve?; (4) cost basis for claim 5 — no detail on how "one-third" was derived; (5) counterfactual — what would repeat contacts or agent satisfaction have been without the bot?

---

## Claim-by-Claim Assessment

### Claim 1: Median response time fell from 4.2 hours to 40 seconds

**Extracted claim**: Median time-to-first-response decreased by >99.7% after launch.

**Evidence stated**: Ticketing-system timestamps for all 38,000 tickets across 10 weeks before and 10 weeks after launch, pulled by the data platform team.

**Assessment**:
- The measurement (median across 38,000 tickets) is direct and verifiable.
- Sample size (38,000) is large; systematic bias from data collection is unlikely.
- The data platform team is independent of the bot team, reducing measurement bias.
- **Critical gaps**: 
  1. No control for confounds: Did on-call staffing increase? Did humans change routing? Did ticket volume shift to easier issues?
  2. Temporal scope: Is the 40-second metric from weeks 1-2 (when users may learn the bot) or weeks 8-10 (steady state)?
  3. Definition: Is 40 seconds the median across ALL tickets (including those where the bot immediately escalates) or only bot-handled tickets? The claim says "fell," implying the system-wide median.
  4. Causation: The decrease is large enough that *something* changed, but was it the bot or the process change that came with bot launch?

**Tier**: **`[KKE]`** — Critical control missing. The raw metric is measured correctly, but causation is uncontrolled. A staffing change or routing change on launch day would produce the same result. The claim needs a comparison group (e.g., a randomized rollout across regions, or a human-only control cohort in the same period) to move to `[K]`.

---

### Claim 2: Customers prefer the bot to human agents

**Extracted claim**: Customer preference favors the bot over humans.

**Evidence stated**: Product team's reading of 30 chat transcripts.

**Assessment**:
- Sample size (30) is small for a preference claim across the customer base (imply: tens of thousands).
- "Reading" is subjective interpretation, not a quantified preference score.
- No comparison metric: Did the product team count explicit preference statements? Satisfaction signals? Or infer preference from lack of complaints?
- No counter-search: Were there transcripts where customers explicitly preferred a human or expressed frustration with the bot? Were those excluded?
- Selection: Were the 30 transcripts chosen randomly, or cherry-picked from successful bot interactions?

**Tier**: **`[H]`** — Plausible hypothesis. Customers may indeed prefer the bot; 30 positive-leaning transcripts suggest the possibility. But this does not meet the threshold for a board claim. A quantified preference score (e.g., "X% said 'prefer bot'" from structured feedback) and a larger sample (ideally stratified by customer segment) are needed for `[K]`.

---

### Claim 3: The bot resolves 70% of tickets on its own

**Extracted claim**: The bot independently closes 70% of tickets.

**Evidence stated**: Vendor dashboard; "resolved" means the customer closed the chat window.

**Assessment**:
- **Measurement validity failure**: "Customer closed the chat window" ≠ "issue was resolved."
  - Customer may close in frustration, planning to contact support via phone or email.
  - Customer may close, then reopen — not counted as repeat in this metric.
  - "Resolved" is a proxy for resolution, and a weak one.
- No ground truth: Is there a follow-up check (e.g., did the customer open a new ticket on the same issue within 7 days)? 
- Vendor incentive: The vendor benefits if the resolution number is high (proof of value). The dashboard is the vendor's own measurement, not independent.
- No counter-search: Among the 30% not closed, what fraction return as repeat issues? (Claim 4 provides this partially, but claim 3 doesn't.)

**Tier**: **`[Y]`** — Misleading. The 70% number is technically measured (customer closed the window), but the framing "resolves" overstates what the metric captures. The bot may be excellent at *satisfying* customers enough to close the chat, while being poor at *solving* the underlying issue. The metric is not false, but it is framed to imply resolution when the data only shows session closure.

### Claim 4: The bot reduced repeat contacts

**Extracted claim**: Bot-first routing reduces repeat contacts within 7 days compared to human-first routing.

**Evidence stated**: Randomized trial, 5,000 customers, 6-week duration, bot-first: 9.1% repeat, human-first: 11.4% repeat, 95% CI on the difference: −3.0 to −1.6 percentage points, computed by the analytics team (independent of the bot team).

**Assessment**:
- **Randomization** controls for confounds (customer segment, issue type, timing).
- **Sample size** (5,000) is large; confidence interval is tight.
- **Independence**: Analytics team computed the result, not the bot team, reducing allegiance bias.
- **Definition**: "Repeat contacts within 7 days" is clear and verifiable.
- **Gaps** (minor):
  1. 6-week trial is adequate for short-term effect, but long-term persistence is not shown.
  2. No statement of pre-registration (was the success threshold and stopping rule locked before the trial ran?).
  3. The CI does not include zero, but at the low end (−1.6 pp) the effect is modest.

**Tier**: **`[K]`** — Proven. The experimental design is sound; the effect is real; independence is established. The finding should be promoted to the board with the caveat that it applies to repeat contacts within 7 days under the tested routing scheme, and long-term effects are unknown.

---

### Claim 5: It will cut support headcount costs by a third next year

**Extracted claim**: Support labor costs will decrease by 33% within 12 months.

**Evidence stated**: Projection from the vendor's sales deck.

**Assessment**:
- **Vendor conflict of interest**: The vendor profits if adoption is high. Projections in a sales deck are not evidence; they are marketing materials.
- **No basis**: The audit does not see the vendor's model, assumptions, or labor data. A "projection" without visible calculation is an assertion, not evidence.
- **Timing**: The claim is about "next year" (future), but the audit is present-tense. This is a forecast, not a measured result.
- **Scope**: The claim does not specify: Is this per-ticket cost, total labor, or salary budget? Seasonal variation? Geographic variation?

**Tier**: **`[S]`** — Speculative. The vendor may have a well-founded model (plausible), but the audit cannot verify it. The claim should not be presented to the board as a proven cost saving. At best, it is a vendor's *estimate*, labeled as such, and subject to assumption review before commit.

---

### Claim 6: Agents are happier since launch

**Extracted claim**: Agent job satisfaction or morale has improved post-launch.

**Evidence stated**: No survey. Attrition this quarter was lower. A hiring freeze started the same month as the launch.

**Assessment**:
- **Measurement**: No direct measure of happiness (no survey, no engagement score). Attrition is a proxy that can work, but in this case it is confounded.
- **Confound 1 — Hiring freeze timing**: A freeze creates job security fear, which *reduces* attrition independently of bot experience. If agents feared layoffs when the bot arrived, they may have stayed longer even if unhappy.
- **Confound 2 — Survivorship**: The unhappiest agents (those who left immediately after launch) are not in the attrition count for later quarters. Only those who stayed are observed, creating selection bias.
- **Confound 3 — Temporal order**: Did attrition actually fall after launch, or was it already falling? No baseline trend is provided.
- **Counter-search**: No data on internal complaints, sick days, or support-team turnover specifically (attrition is company-wide or unknown scope). Did the bot team's own attrition rise?

**Tier**: **`[H]`** — Plausible hypothesis. Agents *might* be happier; lower attrition is consistent with improved morale. But the confounds are severe enough that attrition is not useful evidence here. A real survey or structured feedback is needed to promote to `[K]`.

## Missing Card & Domain Probes

**What this summary omits**:
1. **Negative outcomes**: No data on customer frustration, escalation requests after failed bot attempts, or cases where the bot made problems worse.
2. **Heterogeneity**: No breakdown by ticket type, customer segment, or time of day. Does the bot excel at FAQ-type issues and fail at complex problems?
3. **Cost actuals**: Claim 5 is a projection; no actual headcount reduction is reported or planned.
4. **Agent impact details**: Claim 6 touches morale but misses: workload, job composition (more escalation review?), or skill degradation.

**Domain probes not answered** (using the Mizan seed list from checklist.md item 12):
- **End of life**: What happens when the bot's training data is 12 months old? Does performance degrade?
- **Boundary crossing**: Does the bot handle multi-language support or regulatory edge cases (GDPR, health data)?
- **Undo and exit**: Can a customer recover a conversation if the bot closed the chat prematurely?
- **Who may see it**: Is customer data retained in the vendor's system? Is there an audit trail?

These scenarios were not supplied by the domain owner (the support ops team) and are not verified in this audit. A full audit would include the domain owner's list of edge cases.

## Conjunctions Not Checked

The claims interact in ways the piece-by-piece audit cannot see:

1. **Claims 3 + 4**: If the bot "resolves 70%" (claim 3, but likely means "closes the chat"), and repeat contacts are only 9.1% (claim 4), then either:
   - The 30% not closed by the bot are not repeating (odd — you'd expect escalated issues to reopen), OR
   - The 70% includes many false resolutions (closed chats that become repeat issues within 7 days).
   
   **Finding**: These metrics are not contradictory but they fail to reconcile. The audit needs: among the 70% closed by the bot, what fraction reappear as repeat issues within 7 days? If high (say, 20%), then claim 3's 70% resolution is mostly chat closure, not issue closure, and claim 4's benefit is overstated.

2. **Claims 1 + 2**: If response time fell 99%, why would customers still prefer the bot? If the bot is that fast, the preference might be about speed (data-backed, claim 1) rather than quality (anecdotal, claim 2). **Finding**: The claims are consistent but the preference claim adds no information beyond what response time already shows.

## Recommendations (ordered by criticality × urgency)

1. **Before board presentation** (Critical):
   - Demote claim 5 to `[S]`: Remove the cost projection unless the vendor's model is made available for review.
   - Upgrade claim 4 to board-ready: It is the only `[K]` claim and should anchor the narrative.

2. **For next quarter** (High):
   - Run a pre-registered survey on agent satisfaction (claim 6). Hire freeze as a confound is real; measure morale directly.
   - Reconcile claims 3 and 4: Measure repeat-issue rate among the 70% bot-closed tickets.
   - Extend claim 4 trial to 12 weeks to establish sustained effect.

3. **For rigor** (Medium):
   - Add a control cohort to claim 1 (e.g., business line that didn't get the bot, measured in parallel).
   - Expand claim 2 sample to 300+ transcripts, stratified by customer segment, with a structured coding scheme for preference signals.
