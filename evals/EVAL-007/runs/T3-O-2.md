# Hiring Screening Tool — Board Summary Audit

**Audit type:** Retrospective review of six claims presented to board.

**HARKing status:** These claims were selected and framed after the tool has been in use (claims 1–4, 6 report past outcomes; claim 5 is prospective). Examples show outcomes before thresholds were locked. This is a post-hoc narrative, not a preregistered hypothesis.

---

## Claim 1: "The tool picks better candidates."

**Evidence cited:** "Hiring managers who adopted it say the new hires 'feel stronger.'"

**Assessment:**
- **Atomization:** Two claims: (a) the tool picks candidates, (b) they are "better" (or at least perceived as stronger)
- **Sourcing:** Evidence is anecdotal — open-ended manager impressions in conversation, not quantified
- **Counter-example hunt:** No measurement of actual candidate performance metrics (retention, productivity, skill markers, promotion rate, performance reviews). "Feel stronger" has no objective referent; perceptions reverse over time and are subject to selection bias (managers justify their use of the tool by post-hoc positive interpretation)
- **Missing:** Baseline — how did candidates picked *before* the tool "feel" to these same managers? Placebo test — do managers rate new hires as "stronger" without knowing whether the tool was used?
- **Tier:** `[H]` — Plausible hypothesis. Manager perception is a signal but not evidence of selection quality without objective follow-up measurement.

---

## Claim 2: "It is unbiased."

**Evidence cited:** "The vendor's white paper states it was trained on 'diverse data.'"

- **Atomization:** Two claims: (a) the model was trained on diverse data, (b) this ensures it is unbiased in output
- **Sourcing:** Vendor claim only. No third-party bias audit reported. "Diverse" is unspecified — diverse across which dimensions (demographics, geography, industry, role level)?
- **Confound check:** Training-data diversity does not guarantee output fairness. Known failure modes: (i) a model trained on diverse data can still learn demographic proxies; (ii) "diverse" can mean balanced gender in names but skewed on other protected categories; (iii) fairness is task-dependent — equal error rates across groups, equal true-positive rates, or equal false-positive rates? Each can differ.
- **Counter-example hunt:** No report of bias testing on shortlist outputs. No disaggregated performance metrics by race, gender, protected class, or hire origin.
- **Missing:** Independent bias audit, fairness metric definition, disaggregated performance data
- **Tier:** `[H]` — Plausible hypothesis grounded in sound principle (diverse training data is a good practice) but no evidence that this tool is bias-free in practice. Vendor self-report does not meet the audit threshold.

---

## Claim 3: "Time-to-hire fell from 41 to 29 days."

**Evidence cited:** "HR-system dates for every requisition in the 2 quarters before and after, across all 14 departments, while a hiring freeze did not change the volume of open roles (212 vs 208)."

- **Atomization:** Four checkable claims: (a) baseline was 41 days, (b) post-tool metric was 29 days, (c) this difference is due to the tool, (d) confound control (hiring-freeze volume stability)
- **Sourcing:** Claims (a) and (b) are verifiable from HR system — **accessible**.  Claim (c) causation is **not directly evidenced by what is presented**.
- **Measurement:** 41 → 29 days, a 29% reduction, large effect size. The reduction is **real and quantified**, meeting the data threshold.
- **Confound analysis:** 
  - The hiring freeze volume (212 vs 208 requisitions, a 1.9% variance) addresses ONE confound (whether the organization ramped down hiring). Good partial control.
  - **Missing controls:** Seasonality (Q3 hiring differs from Q4), market labor-supply changes, other process improvements deployed at the same time (AI-assisted screening predate this tool?), recruiter turnover or training, job-description quality changes.
  - **Single-variable bias:** A 29-day improvement over 2 quarters in a high-volume hiring process could reflect normal noise, especially if either period was an outlier.
- **Counter-example hunt:** Is there evidence the time-savings occurred in all 14 departments equally, or concentrated in a subset? No breakdown provided. Non-uniform effect raises the possibility the tool enabled quick hiring in some roles (e.g., technical roles with faster consensus) while adding friction elsewhere.
- **Tier:** `[KKE]` — Critical control missing. The time reduction is real and measured, but causation is **not separated from confounds**. Seasonality and concurrent process changes are the strongest rival hypotheses. Promote to `[K]` only after: (i) time-series with year-over-year comparison (controlling for quarter effects), (ii) audit showing no other process changes deployed 2+ weeks before the 29-day measurement began, (iii) per-department breakdown showing consistent effect.

## Claim 4: "In a blinded audit by an external firm, 600 anonymized applications were scored by the tool and by two trained human panels; the tool's shortlist overlapped the panels' consensus shortlist 81%, versus 79% agreement between the two human panels."

- **Atomization:** (a) External firm conducted blinded audit, (b) 600 applications, (c) tool scored shortlist, (d) tool-consensus overlap was 81%, (e) human inter-rater agreement was 79%, (f) the tool thus meets/exceeds human performance.
- **Sourcing:** External audit is **cited but not shown**. Methodology details required to tier this claim are **not provided**: How was "consensus shortlist" defined? (majority vote, unanimous, or weighted consensus?) How many candidates were in each shortlist? Were raters assigned the same decision rule? How were candidates anonymized (name removal sufficient, or were dates-of-graduation, location proxies retained)?
- **Measurement:** 81% vs 79% is a 2 percentage-point difference on 600 samples (~12 candidate decisions). Is this within noise? Statistical significance testing is **not reported**.
- **Confound check:** "Blinded" is stated but the kind matters — blinded to *what*? If the tool's shortlist was shown to the human panels for comparison, that is not blinded comparison. If raters knew they were being compared to a machine, anchoring/reactance bias is possible.
- **Missing:** 
  - Hypothesis was locked *before* seeing results? Or data-dependent threshold shopping ("81% looks good, let's call that a win")?
  - Per-candidate detail: on which types of candidates does the tool outperform or underperform humans? (E.g., is the tool better at technical-role screening, worse at leadership screening?)
  - Reproducibility: would a rerun with different human panels yield 81% again, or was this panel composition lucky?
  - Definition of "shortlist" — is overlap measured at the same rank, or just binary shortlist membership?
- **Tier:** `[KKE]` — Critical control missing. The evidence is **stronger than anecdotes** (N=600, external firm, quantified), but **methodology is underspecified**. The 2-point difference (81 vs 79) might not be statistically significant and could reflect measurement noise, not real superiority. Promote to `[K]` only after: (i) significance test reported with p-value and effect size, (ii) per-domain breakdown (candidate type, role level, demographic subgroup), (iii) methodology detail on consensus definition and anonymization, (iv) evidence the threshold was locked before results.

## Claim 5: "First-year retention of hires will improve."

- **Evidence cited:** "It is too early to measure; the first cohort started 3 months ago."
- **Atomization:** Prospective claim with *explicitly no data*.
- **Tier:** `[H]` — Plausible hypothesis. The tool may improve hiring quality and thus retention, but no test has run. This is speculation, not measurement. Retention is noisy (3-month tenure teaches little; first-year retention is typically measured at 12 months; some departures are external market/personal factors).
- **Next step:** Lock a preregistered entry for this outcome now (formal hypothesis, metric, threshold, stopping rule, confounds) so the 12-month data point later is comparison of prediction to outcome, not post-hoc narrative. If you do not do this, the outcome will not be auditable.

---

## Claim 6: "Recruiters save 10 hours a week."

- **Evidence cited:** "Recruiter estimates in a team meeting."
- **Atomization:** (a) The tool saves time, (b) specifically 10 hours/week
- **Sourcing:** Anecdotal estimates in a meeting, not measured. Estimates are subject to:
  - Recall bias (did they track it, or guess?)
  - Social desirability bias (in a meeting, recruiters may exaggerate benefit to show enthusiasm to leadership)
  - Confounding with normal efficiency gains (process learning, tools unrelated to this system)
- **Counter-example hunt:** Do recruiters report any *increased* load from the tool (e.g., tool auditing/review work)? Time-savings is net only after overhead is subtracted.
- **Missing:** Objective measurement (time-tracking software, sample week before/after, task-level logging). Baseline — what were recruiters doing in those 10 hours before? Can that work be fully displaced or does it just shift to different tasks?
- **Tier:** `[S]` — Speculative. This is plausible but not measured. The number "10 hours" is a guess offered in a meeting, not a calibrated metric. Even if recruiters genuinely feel faster, unmeasured subjective time-savings is not evidence.

## Hit rate and missing cards

Six claims presented to the board. Under Mizan audit:
- Claims surviving at `[K]` (Proven): **0 of 6**
- Claims at `[KKE]` (Critical control missing): **2** (claims 3, 4)
- Claims at `[H]` (Plausible): **2** (claims 1, 2, 5)
- Claims at `[S]` (Speculative): **1** (claim 6)
- Claims at `[Y]` or `[R]`: 0

**This is not a "quick sign-off" scenario.** The board summary presents a narrative of success, but every claim either has no measurement (1, 2, 5, 6) or measurement without controlled causation (3, 4). Tier drift is visible: the team lead's confidence ("all proven") does not match the evidence tier.

**Missing entirely from the board summary:**
- Comparative cost (does the vendor tool cost less than the time saved by recruiters? What is the per-hire cost?)
- Failure cases — any cohorts or departments where the tool *hurt* hiring speed or quality?
- Adoption rate — did all 14 departments use it equally, or is time-savings concentrated in early adopters?
- Demographic breakdown — does the tool work equally well for all role types and hiring profiles?
- Risk: if the tool *is* biased (claim 2 unproven), and is being used across 14 departments with no oversight, the harm accumulates over time while a single anecdote ("feel stronger") masks it.

## Recommendations (criticality × effort⁻¹)

**HIGHEST PRIORITY:**
1. **For claim 4 (the strongest evidence):** Obtain and read the external audit report. Request significance testing on the 81% vs 79% difference and per-role breakdown. This is a half-hour review and could upgrade the tier to `[K]` if methodology is sound. Cost: ~0.5 hours.

2. **For claim 5 (prospective, hence preventable):** Stop and preregister the retention hypothesis NOW, before the 12-month data point arrives. Lock the metric (definition of "retention," voluntary vs involuntary), threshold, and confound controls (comparing new-hire cohorts on other dimensions to ensure the only difference is tool use). If you wait, this will become HARKing and unterable. Cost: ~1 hour.

3. **For claims 1, 2, 6 (no objective measurement):** Design a follow-up study. Pick one to start: either (a) objective candidate quality (performance reviews, promotion rate at 6/12 months for the 200+ hires screened by tool vs. historical control group), or (b) bias audit (screened candidates disaggregated by protected category, fairness metrics per group), or (c) time-tracking study (one week before/after, tracked at task level). Each is 2–4 weeks of setup + measurement. Cost: ~3–8 weeks per claim.

**BOARD COMMUNICATION:**
Recommend **tabling this approval for 2–4 weeks**. Tell the board: "We have promising signals (especially the external audit showing 81% agreement with human consensus on 600 candidates). We are locking in measurement of longer-term outcomes (retention, bias fairness) and one productivity claim (recruiter time) before board sign-off. This prevents the finding from being contradicted by data we will collect later." 

Tier-K claims are approval-ready; tier-KKE and tier-H are "continue the work, do not yet commit to the vendor for 3+ years on this evidence."

---

## HARKing status (audit item 10)

This review is retrospective on retrospective data: the tool has been in use, outcomes are selected from observed results, and claims are framed post-hoc. The board summary reads as a *success narrative*, not a preregistered prediction. Example: if adoption had *hurt* time-to-hire (42 days instead of 41), would claim 3 be presented? The selection is conditioned on observed success, so this is high-HARKing risk. A tier-K conclusion is not compatible with this evidence path — only tier-H or tier-KKE.
