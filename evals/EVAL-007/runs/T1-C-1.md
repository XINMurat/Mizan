# Support Chatbot Board Claims — Audit Report

## Audit mode (retrospective); HARKing status declared

This is a retrospective audit of six claims in a board summary. The claims were presented after launch and results were known, so all tiers below [K] carry HARKing risk; threshold selection after observing outcomes is flagged where applicable. No preregistration documents were provided for these claims, so I cannot confirm whether thresholds and stopping rules were locked before results arrived. This audit therefore assumes post-hoc framing throughout.

---

## Individual claims, atomized and tiered

**Claim 1: Median time to first response fell from 4.2 hours to 40 seconds**

- Atomic claim: *The bot launch reduced first-response time by a factor of 630×, from 4.2 hours to 40 seconds, as measured on all 38,000 support tickets in the 10 weeks before and after launch.*
- Source: Ticketing-system timestamps pulled by data platform team.
- Evidence type: Quantitative, large sample (N=38,000), direct operational measurement.
- Data status: Exists; timestamps are primary records, not derived.
- Stopping rule: All tickets in the defined window; no exclusions mentioned.
- **Confound: Symmetric control missing.** A 100× improvement is exactly the type of surprising positive that requires confound check (rule 5, item 5). The bot may resolve *easier* tickets (e.g., password resets, account lookups) while humans handled the mixed queue. If the ticket distribution changed post-launch (more easy tickets routed to bot, harder to humans), the response-time improvement is confounded with ticket composition shift. No control group comparison is provided (e.g., human-first bot-available control; same-difficulty ticket subset). The claim conflates speed of response with the bot's causal role.
- Tier: **[KKE]** (Critical control missing). The measurement exists and appears valid as an operational fact, but the *causal claim* — "the bot caused this improvement" — requires comparison against a control for ticket difficulty or mix. Without it, faster responses might indicate easier tickets, not better handling.
- One-line summary: Timestamp data confirms speed improvement, but confound with ticket routing remains unchecked.

**Claim 2: Customers prefer the bot to human agents**

- Atomic claim: *A qualitative review of chat transcripts shows customers prefer the bot.*
- Source: Product team's subjective reading of 30 chat transcripts.
- Evidence type: Small qualitative sample, no quantified metric, no coding scheme mentioned, no inter-rater reliability.
- Issues:
  - "Prefer" is not operationalized. Over what dimension? Speed? Clarity? Ease? None specified.
  - N=30 is below the threshold for pattern claims in most domains.
  - Subjective reading introduces selection and interpretation bias: which 30 transcripts were selected? Were they chosen to illustrate preference, or random?
  - No mention of coding reliability (did multiple raters agree on "preference"?) or exclusion rules for ambiguous cases.
- Tier: **[H]** (Plausible hypothesis). The team observed something worth recording, but the evidence falls below the threshold for a claim. A quantified metric (e.g., "15 of 30 transcripts contain an explicit preference statement, 1 contains explicit preference for human") paired with a 95% lower-bound CI and a random or stratified sample would earn this tier, but none of those are present.
- One-line summary: Anecdotal; no quantified threshold or coding scheme reported.

---

**Claim 3: The bot resolves 70% of tickets on its own**

- Atomic claim: *The bot closes 70% of support tickets without human escalation.*
- Source: Vendor dashboard; "resolved" operationalized as "customer closed the chat window."
- Evidence type: Quantitative (70%), vendor system, binary outcome.
- Critical issue: **Definition does not match claim.** A customer closing the chat window is not evidence that a problem was resolved. The customer may have:
  - Closed the window in frustration (the bot didn't help).
  - Opened a new support channel (phone, email).
  - Decided to work around the issue.
  - Given up.
  Each of these looks like "resolution" (chat closed) in the vendor dashboard but means the opposite (unresolved).
- No mention of:
  - Follow-up contact within 7 days (re-opening, escalation, repeat issue) — which claim 4 does measure.
  - Customer satisfaction survey.
  - Whether the customer's issue was actually resolved (symptom gone, use case working).
- Confound with claim 4: If the bot truly resolved 70% with lasting effect, claim 4's repeat-contact rate (9.1% for bot-first) should be *lower* than 30% (the unresolved fraction). It's not — it's close to the claimed 30% unresolved rate — which suggests the 70% count is optimistic.
- Tier: **[Y]** (Misleading). The figure is technically true (70% of chats close without escalation) but the wording "the bot resolves" implies the problem was fixed, which the evidence does not support. The framing imparts a value judgment the data cannot sustain.
- One-line summary: "Resolves" (problem fixed) conflated with "closes chat" (customer left); no outcome data provided.

**Claim 4: The bot reduced repeat contacts in a randomized trial**

- Atomic claim: *In a randomized controlled trial (N=5,000, 6 weeks, bot-first vs. human-first support), repeat contacts within 7 days were 9.1% (bot-first) vs. 11.4% (human-first), 95% CI for the difference −3.0 to −1.6 percentage points.*
- Source: Analytics team (independent of bot team); randomized assignment.
- Evidence type: Quantitative, experimental design, preregistered-looking threshold (95% CI), large sample, independent analyst.
- Strengths:
  - Randomization controls confounds in ticket selection.
  - 5,000-customer sample is large.
  - Independent measurement (analytics team, not bot team).
  - Clear stopping rule: 6 weeks.
  - Clear outcome metric: repeat contact within 7 days.
  - 95% CI does not cross zero (−3.0 to −1.6 is entirely negative), so the difference is statistically significant.
  - **Symmetric control present:** The randomization serves as a control (human-first is the reference). The design compares bot-first against the alternative rather than against the status quo alone.
- Weakness (minor):
  - Text does not explicitly state that threshold and analysis plan were preregistered *before* seeing data. The CI looks preregistered-style (standard 95%), but I cannot confirm the specific hypothesis and stopping rule were locked. However, the randomized design and independent analyst substantially reduce HARKing risk.
- Tier: **[K]** (Proven). The claim meets the threshold: direct evidence from a large, well-controlled study; independent measurement; clear numerical outcome; statistical significance; reasonable evidence for the specific mechanism (repeat contacts, not other metrics). The symmetric control (human-first comparison) satisfies rule 5. This is the one claim that survives scrutiny.
- One-line summary: Randomized trial with independent measurement and statistical significance; symmetric control present.

---

**Claim 5: It will cut support headcount costs by a third next year**

- Atomic claim: *Support costs will fall by 33% in the next 12 months due to the bot.*
- Source: Projection from vendor's sales deck.
- Evidence type: Vendor prediction with no underlying data disclosed.
- Issues:
  - This is a *future claim* (not testable until 12 months hence).
  - No evidence provided for the projection model (cost per ticket? ticket volume changes? salary assumptions? overlap during transition?).
  - Vendor has a vested interest: inflating projected ROI is standard practice in vendor materials.
  - No independent verification of the projection method.
  - No mention of sensitivity analysis or ranges (point estimate only: "a third").
- Tier: **[S]** (Speculative). It is a plausible candidate for tracking, but zero evidence supports the specific magnitude. This belongs in a forward-looking registry with a preregistered refutation threshold (e.g., "costs fall by more than 25% within 12 months, measured by [audited payroll data], or the hypothesis is falsified"), not in a board summary as fact.
- One-line summary: Unsubstantiated vendor projection; no underlying cost model disclosed.

**Claim 6: Agents are happier since launch**

- Atomic claim: *Support staff have increased job satisfaction due to the bot.*
- Source: Proxy measurement only: attrition this quarter was lower; hiring freeze started the same month.
- Evidence type: Indirect; two confounded observations, no direct measurement.
- Critical confound: **Hiring freeze effect dominates.** A hiring freeze *mechanically* reduces attrition by eliminating departures that would otherwise occur (people take jobs elsewhere). Attrition *during* a hiring freeze is not a valid measure of happiness — it conflates satisfaction with opportunity. This is a classic confounder that makes the claim un-interpretable:
  - **If attrition fell:** Could be due to bot (staff happier) or freeze (fewer outside options). Cannot distinguish.
  - **If attrition rose anyway:** Proves unhappiness despite freeze. Did not happen, so no strong evidence either way.
- No survey on satisfaction, engagement, or intent to leave.
- Alternative hypothesis: Attrition fell because the hiring freeze was announced the same month, creating fear of job loss or preventing voluntary departures. The bot is irrelevant.
- Counter-example search: Comparison to other months with hiring freezes (no data provided) would show whether this attrition pattern is normal for freezes.
- Tier: **[KKE]** (Critical control missing). The claim requires either (a) a satisfaction survey independent of attrition, (b) a natural experiment (hiring freeze without bot, or bot without freeze) to separate effects, or (c) comparison of attrition trajectories in hiring-freeze periods before vs. after the bot was added. None of these controls are present.
- One-line summary: Attrition proxy confounded with hiring freeze; no survey; cannot separate mechanisms.

## Missing card (format limitation)

This summary omits:
- **Costs of the bot:** training overhead, platform maintenance, prompt engineering, oversight labor, tokens spent (if cloud-based).
- **Failure modes:** tickets the bot cannot resolve, escalation handling, error rates, customer frustration (dropped chats treated as resolved in claim 3).
- **Segment performance:** does the bot help all customer types equally, or does it primarily resolve easy tickets while humans still handle hard ones? (Directly relevant to confound in claim 1.)
- **Temporal dynamics:** did improvements decay over time (e.g., after launch novelty wore off, customer adaptation, prompt drift)?
- **Counterfactual:** what would have happened without the bot (impossible to infer without a control group)?

## Domain probe (required for full audit)

Mizan audit step 7 requires asking: *What scenarios actually occur in support operations, and can this bot handle them?* I cannot list these alone; this must be supplied by the ops/product domain owner. Typical probe categories (from `checklist.md` § 12):

- **Boundary crossing:** Customer's issue spans multiple products or requires admin escalation. Can the bot recognize this and hand off?
- **Negative path:** Customer is angry or stuck. Does the bot have a de-escalation strategy, or does it worsen sentiment?
- **One vs. many:** Should the bot handle a bulk request (e.g., 100 license resets)? Can it?
- **Undo and exit:** If the bot applied a wrong solution (disabled an account, wrong permission), can it walk back the change?
- **Ordering:** Does the bot understand that "add user to group then grant permission" must happen in sequence?
- **Absence:** A customer reports something NOT happening (e.g., "my email is not syncing"). Can the bot diagnose absence-based failures?
- **Who can see it:** Does the bot leak information between customers? Can it verify customer identity for sensitive requests?

**These scenarios were not supplied, so this audit cannot claim full coverage of the bot's problem domain. A tier-K coverage claim would require them.**

## Conjunction audit (interactions, not just features)

Step 8 requires: *Put the claims back together. Does claim 4's evidence (repeat contacts) remain valid while claim 3's framing (bot resolves 70%) is operating?*

If 70% are truly resolved (claim 3, generously interpreted), then repeat-contact rates for bot-handled tickets should be ≤30% (only the unresolved 30% return). 
- Bot-first repeat rate (claim 4): 9.1% of ALL tickets (both resolved and escalated by the bot). 
- For this to make sense, the bot-handled subset must have much lower repeat rates than 9.1% (since escalated tickets likely have higher repeat rates).
- The fact that 9.1% is close to 30% suggests one of:
  1. The 70% "resolved" is optimistic (many close without actually fixing the problem).
  2. The 9.1% counts both repeat contacts due to bot failure and repeat contacts on bot-escalated tickets (combining two processes).

**Finding:** Claims 3 and 4 conflict in their implied mechanics. If claim 4 is believed (well-designed study), claim 3's 70% number should be lower or differently justified.

## Retrospective HARKing declaration (step 10)

This audit is retrospective: results were known before the analysis. The board summary shows no evidence that these claims were articulated and thresholds locked before launch. Therefore:
- Metric selection is at risk: the team may have chosen which metrics to highlight *after* seeing which looked good.
- Threshold shopping is at risk: a claimed 95% CI (claim 4) without evidence it was preregistered.
- Sample selection is at risk: did the analysts perform an exhaustive audit of all possible metrics, or did they report the five that looked best?

I do not assume bad intent; this is structural risk, not accusation. **The cure is prospective registration going forward.**

## Recommendations, ordered by criticality × (impact / effort)

1. **Before board presentation (CRITICAL):** Revise claim 3's language from "bot resolves 70%" to "bot closes 70% of tickets without escalation" and disclose the definition. The current wording is misleading. Consider adding outcome verification (7-day repeat-contact rate by resolution code).

2. **Before next quarter (HIGH):** Establish a prospective registry (Mizan mode 2) for bot-performance hypotheses. Lock thresholds, stopping rules, and analysis plans *before* collecting results. This audit's HARKing risk is structural; forward-looking discipline eliminates it.

3. **Within one week (HIGH):** Conduct the domain probe (step 7): interview ops/product leads on 5–8 real support scenarios the bot should handle (e.g., boundary crossing, negative sentiment, bulk requests, back-out). Test bot behavior on each. This determines whether the audit's coverage claim can advance from [H] to [K].

4. **In next bot update cycle (MEDIUM):** Add a post-interaction customer survey (single question: "Was your issue resolved?") to ground the 70% claim in actual outcomes rather than chat-close events. Sample: 5–10% of chats, weekly rolling average to detect decay.

5. **For cost projection (MEDIUM):** Replace the vendor deck estimate with an independent cost model. Lock assumptions (payroll ramp, ticket volume growth, training hours, token costs). Preregister the refutation threshold: "Costs do not fall by at least 25% within 12 months" → claim falsified.

## Closing note

Claim 4 (the randomized trial on repeat contacts) is the only claim that survives rigor. The others range from defensible but indirect ([H]) through confounded ([KKE]) to misleading ([Y]). The team's confidence ("all proven") does not match the evidence. A board conversation that leads with claim 4 and candidly discusses the limitations of the others will be more persuasive in the long term than presenting a uniform [K] that an audit would dismantle the next day.
