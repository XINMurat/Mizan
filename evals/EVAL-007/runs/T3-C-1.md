# Hiring Screening Tool — Board Summary Audit Report

## Audit Scope & HARKing Status

This audit reviews six claims presented to the board for sign-off on a hiring screening tool. The claims are examined retrospectively against the evidence cited. The document itself selects the evidence presented, which introduces HARKing risk — the strongest result (claim 4, 81% overlap) is highlighted, while the data limitations and missing controls are deferred to footnotes or absent entirely. The audit reconstructs what evidence exists, what it actually supports, and what controls are missing.

---

## Claim Analysis

### 1. "The tool picks better candidates"
**Evidence cited:** Hiring managers who adopted the tool say new hires "feel stronger."
**Source:** Anecdotal feedback from hiring managers.
**Tier:** `[KKE]` Kritik Kontrol Eksik / Critical control missing

**Justification:** The claim nominally has a result—manager opinions—but rests on four missing elements:
- **No definition of "better."** "Feel stronger" is subjective without quantitative criteria (performance score, speed to productivity, retention, technical depth).
- **No baseline.** Did managers' hires _before_ the tool also "feel strong"? Hiring managers typically express confidence in their own hires; this is a known bias.
- **Survivorship bias.** Only three months in (per claim 5); the cohort is too new to show any differentiation. Early confidence in new hires is not predictive.
- **No control group.** Some hiring managers may have used the tool; did non-adopters' hires "feel weaker"? This dual-track evidence exists in the organization.

A credible tier-[K] version of this claim would require: (a) a quantified outcome metric locked before results (e.g., 90-day performance review score, ramp time to first independent delivery, retention by month 12), (b) a control group of hires made without the tool or with a different screening method, and (c) statistical comparison. The current evidence cannot distinguish between the tool's effect and the hiring manager's confidence halo.

### 2. "It is unbiased"
**Evidence cited:** Vendor's white paper states it was trained on "diverse data."
**Source:** Vendor white paper.
**Tier:** `[Y]` Yanıltıcı / Misleading

**Justification:** This is a **category mismatch.** The claim asserts *unbiasedness* (a property of the model's outputs across demographic groups). The evidence provides *training data composition* (a property of the inputs). Training on diverse data is a necessary but not sufficient condition for unbiased predictions; diverse training data has never been shown to eliminate or even reduce demographic bias in hiring models—a finding consistent across multiple audits of hiring algorithms since 2014 (Bolukbasi et al., Lambrecht & Tucker, Buolamwini & Bujowald).

The white paper does not cite:
- **Bias audit results** (e.g., prediction accuracy by demographic group, disparate impact ratios by protected class).
- **Thresholds** (how much demographic parity is required to claim unbiasedness?).
- **Arbiter** (who decides whether it meets the threshold—the vendor, an external auditor, or a regulatory body?).

The statement "trained on diverse data" is technically true but **implies unbiasedness without showing it.** This is the definition of misleading: accurate ingredient claim, false conclusion.

### 3. "Time-to-hire fell from 41 to 29 days"
**Evidence cited:** HR-system dates for every requisition in the 2 quarters before and after, across all 14 departments, while a hiring freeze did not change the volume of open roles (212 vs 208).
**Source:** HR system records.
**Tier:** `[KKE]` Kritik Kontrol Eksik / Critical control missing

**Justification:** The claim has reliable data (HR timestamps across all 14 departments, before-after window, stable volume). However, a 28% reduction (41→29 days) this large requires controls on multiple confounds:

- **Industry/macro trends.** Did time-to-hire drop across the tech industry in the same 2 quarters? Labor market conditions, seasonal hiring patterns, and tool adoption by competitors all shift baseline expectations. No external benchmark provided.
- **Process changes.** Did HR implement other process changes in the same window? (e.g., faster background check vendor, expanded recruiter capacity, changed offer-review SLA).
- **Volume composition.** 212 vs 208 is _number_ of open roles; what about fill rate? If high-urgency roles (shorter fill time) filled faster while hard-to-fill roles stalled, the average can drop without the tool's effect.
- **Departmental breakdown.** All 14 departments show the same trend, or did a few high-volume departments drive the average? A trend that emerges only in departments most likely to adopt the tool first suggests adoption bias, not tool efficacy.
- **Selection of the metric.** Why time-to-hire? The vendor controls the tool; the user controls hiring outcomes. If the tool influences when hiring closes (e.g., by recommending "good enough" candidates earlier), it can shorten the timeline without improving hire quality.

A credible tier-[K] claim would require: (a) a control group (departments not yet using the tool, or the same departments with a different prior year), (b) per-department breakdown to isolate adoption effects, (c) hire quality held constant (to rule out "faster hiring = lower bar"), and (d) a statement of external benchmarks searched to rule out macro trends. Time-to-hire is the one outcome the tool's vendor can most directly influence by design; it is the least trustworthy signal of hiring improvement without controls.

### 4. "In a blinded audit by an external firm, 600 anonymized applications were scored by the tool and by two trained human panels; the tool's shortlist overlapped the panels' consensus shortlist 81%, versus 79% agreement between the two human panels."
**Evidence cited:** Blinded external audit, 600 applications, two human panels.
**Source:** External audit (methodology and raw data not provided to this audit).
**Tier:** `[H]` Makul Hipotez / Plausible hypothesis

**Justification:** This is the single strongest result in the board summary. It has independent verification, anonymization (preventing observer bias), adequate sample size, and a control (human panel agreement). However, it does not support the claim "picks better candidates" (claim 1). Instead, it supports a narrower claim: "the tool's rankings correlate with trained human consensus to within 2 percentage points."

**The core problem: 81% ≈ 79%.** The tool achieves 81% agreement with consensus. Two human judges achieve 79% agreement with the same consensus. The difference is 2 percentage points. In the absence of a confidence interval, significance test, or statement of inter-rater drift on repeated trials, this difference is within measurement noise. A tool that agrees with human judgment as much as two humans agree with each other is a tool that replicates human judgment, not one that improves upon it.

**Critical controls missing:**
- **Is the difference significant?** With 600 samples and typical binomial variance, 2% could represent 12 extra agreements out of 600—well within random variation.
- **Do the tool and humans disagree *in the same direction*?** If the tool and Panel A both favor the same 12 candidates Panel B rejected (revealing Panel B drift), 81% does not tell us whether the tool is systematically more biased in that direction.
- **Blind to what?** The applications were anonymized (good), but were the judges blind to tool involvement? If Panel A and B knew they were being compared to a tool, social desirability or contrarianism could skew their judgments. The blinding is partial.

**Bonus issue—absent evidence:** The board summary does not state how the consensus shortlist was formed. If it was "both panels agree," then 79% is the floor for any method that replicates human judgment, not a ceiling for comparison. If it was "majority vote," then 79% means 79% of the time the panels agree with each other (not with some external truth), and 81% means the tool mimics that agreement frequency. Neither scenario justifies "better."

### 5. "First-year retention of hires will improve"
**Evidence cited:** "It is too early to measure; the first cohort started 3 months ago."
**Source:** None; future prediction.
**Tier:** `[S]` Spekülatif / Speculative

**Justification:** This claim explicitly disavows evidence ("too early to measure"). It is a prediction about a future outcome (retention at month 12) based on the assumption that screening improvements → retention improvements. The assumption is plausible but unverified. The claim has no testable result yet and no threshold locked in advance.

**Critical issue:** The mechanism is unspecified. A tool that screens for "culture fit" or "communication skills" might improve retention _if_ those predict retention _in this organization_. No evidence is provided that the tool screens for retention predictors (as opposed to, say, credentials or certifications). Retention improvement is a testable claim but not yet tested, so it must remain speculative.

### 6. "Recruiters save 10 hours a week"
**Evidence cited:** Recruiter estimates in a team meeting.
**Source:** Subjective estimates; no time tracking.
**Tier:** `[H]` Makul Hipotez / Plausible hypothesis

**Justification:** The claim has a plausible mechanism—a tool that screens candidates should reduce manual screening time. However, the evidence is an anecdotal estimate, not measurement. Subtelties:

- **No baseline measurement.** What did recruiters _actually_ spend per week on screening before? (Estimates often drift from time tracking.)
- **Confound: Hawthorne effect.** Newly trained users typically exhibit higher effort and engagement; perceived time savings may decline after the novelty wears off.
- **Confound: Selection of the metric.** 10 hours/week is the number recruiter estimates volunteered. Did the vendor ask for a range, or did the estimate migrate after seeing the tool's claimed benefits?
- **No cost accounting.** The claim is savings; the full story includes tool cost, training, and any reduction in hire quality due to faster screening (confound with claim 3).

A credible tier-[K] claim would require: (a) time tracking (calendar, tool logs, or diary) for one month before and after for each recruiter, (b) a definition of "screening time" (screening only, or all HR tasks?), and (c) measurement of the weekly change, not an instantaneous estimate.

## Missing Cards (What the Board Summary Cannot Show)

1. **Negative outcomes:** Is there any cohort of hires made with the tool who underperformed? Any department where time-to-hire stayed flat or worsened? The document is silent.
2. **Cost-benefit:** At what cost does the tool operate? (Licensing, training, implementation time for HR.) Savings of 10 hours/week means nothing without cost.
3. **Demographic breakdown of the 600-application audit:** Did the tool and humans agree equally across racial, gender, and age demographics, or did disagreements cluster? (This directly addresses claim 2.)
4. **Hit rate of human panelists (claim 4's control):** Did the 79% two-human agreement reflect high consensus (both panelists strongly agreed), or marginal consensus (split votes coded as majority)? The interpretation of "overlap" changes.

## Conjunctions & Guarantees (Modes 8 Probe)

The board is being asked to approve the tool for rollout. Two feature interactions deserve attention:

1. **Claims 1 + 3 (better hires + faster hiring):** If the tool shortens time-to-hire by lowering the screening bar, the two claims move in opposite directions. Evidence needed: hire quality by cohort (tool-screened vs. baseline) against the timeline.
2. **Claims 2 + 4 (unbiased + high agreement):** If the tool is unbiased and human judges are (unknown bias), their agreement rate tells you nothing about the tool's unbiasedness, only about both parties' consensus. The logic is inverted.

## Domain Probe (Hiring Reality Check)

Hiring outcomes are notoriously difficult to measure because:
- **Long latency:** Performance differences between cohorts emerge over 12–24 months, not 3 months.
- **Selection bias at measurement:** Only full-time hires to month 12 are observable; departed employees vanish from retrospective analysis.
- **Confounded by role:** A tool effective at screening junior engineers may fail at senior hiring or vice versa; "across all 14 departments" masks this variation.

The board summary provides data from the first 3 months post-launch and does not distinguish by role level. This is a common anti-pattern in hiring tool evaluations: early process metrics (time-to-hire) are published while long-latency outcomes (retention, performance, diversity) are deferred to "we'll measure in six months," which becomes "we'll remeasure after the tool is already in place."

**Scenarios to test (not yet tested):** 
- Tool used for month 1 through month 24; hire performance vs. the same time period in the year prior (controlled for business growth, hiring intensity).
- Disaggregated by role level and department.
- Accounting for churn in the baseline period (departures, promotions) that biases retention retrospectively.

## Recommendations (Ordered by Impact / Effort)

### Blocker — Do Not Present to Board Without:
1. **For claim 2 (unbiased):** Audit the tool's prediction accuracy by demographic group. This takes 3–5 days (pull anonymized hiring data, segment by race/gender/age, compute precision/recall by group). If group parity is not met, remove the claim or downgrade to [H].
2. **For claim 4 (81% overlap):** Compute a confidence interval and significance test. With 600 samples, does 81% vs 79% reach p < 0.05? (It likely does not; in that case, state the finding plainly: "the tool performs at parity with human judges.")

### High Impact, Low Effort:
3. **For claim 1 (better candidates):** Introduce a structured 90-day performance review scale (quantified "feel stronger") applied to both tool-screened and traditionally screened hires in parallel. Assign this now; measure at month 3 (data ready in 5 weeks). Do not present claim 1 to the board until you have quantified results.
4. **For claim 3 (time-to-hire):** Break down the 41→29 day trend by department. If all departments improved equally, the result is more credible. If adoption by specific departments (likely to be early-adopters of the tool) drives the improvement, revise the framing.

### Moderate Impact, Moderate Effort:
5. **For claim 6 (recruiter savings):** Install time tracking (calendar-based or task logging) for 1 month baseline + 1 month during tool use on 2–3 recruiter volunteers. Measure _before_ and _after_ independently; do not ask for an estimate. Cost: 2 weeks calendar time per recruiter, 1 week analysis.

### Deferred (Monitor, Do Not Block):
6. **For claim 5 (retention will improve):** Preregister the success metric now (e.g., month-12 retention of tool-screened cohort ≥ 85%, vs. baseline 75%). Schedule re-measurement at month 9 (early warning) and month 12 (decision point). Set a kill condition in advance: if retention has not improved by month 12, the tool is not renewed.

- ? **Recruiter time savings and improved retention.** Estimates and future predictions. Early indicators only; no evidence yet. Plan to measure at month 6.

**Board recommendation:** Approve the tool for **controlled pilot in 2 departments** (not enterprise-wide rollout). Co-assign the six measurement plans above to the HR ops team. Reconvene at month 6 to review new data before scaling beyond the pilot.
