# Support Chatbot Board Summary — Evidence-Tiered Audit

## Executive Summary

This audit reviews six claims presented to the board for sign-off. Two claims survive rigorous scrutiny; four fail on evidence or methodology grounds. The team lead's confidence that all are proven is not supported by the evidence provided. This is a retrospective audit conducted after outcomes were observed, which raises HARKing risk — claims selected for presentation because they succeeded.

## Audit Procedure and Coverage

**Mode:** Audit mode (retrospective).
**Scope:** Six claims, all checkable from provided evidence.
**HARKing status:** Retrospective selection. The board summary presents outcomes already known to have succeeded or been measured; threshold-setting occurs after results were known. This introduces selection bias and threshold shopping risk. For claim 5 (cost projection), the HARKing direction reverses: the claim anticipates the future and cannot be tested yet.
**Coverage:** 6 of 6 claims reviewed. No claims left out.

## Claim-by-Claim Analysis

### Claim 1: Median time to first response fell from 4.2 hours to 40 seconds

**Tier:** `[K]` Proven

**Justification:** Direct measurement from authoritative source (ticketing-system timestamps); comprehensive sample (38,000 tickets); clear before-and-after boundary (10 weeks each period); computed by data platform team (neutral party, not the team shipping the feature). The magnitude (four orders of magnitude improvement) is large enough that measurement error is unlikely to reverse the conclusion. Threshold is met: the metric is objective, time-stamped, and independently verifiable.

**Evidence chain:** Claim → measurement exists → data platform holds it → timestamps do not lie → [K].

---

### Claim 2: Customers prefer the bot to human agents

**Tier:** `[H]` Plausible hypothesis

**Justification:** The evidence (30 chat transcripts) is below threshold for a preference claim. "Product team's reading" indicates qualitative interpretation, not a systematic instrument (e.g., a preference survey, a rating scale, a choice metric). 

**Failure mode — Selection bias + interpretation:** Which 30 transcripts? If selected from successful chats (transcripts where issues were resolved), the sample is biased. If selected randomly, the method ("reading") is still non-standardized and allows different raters to reach different conclusions. Three interpretive decisions hide here: (a) which chats are evidence for preference, (b) what "preference" means in a transcript, (c) how to aggregate across 30 cases.

**Counter-example search:** The claim is stated as an absolute ("customers prefer"), but the evidence admits contrary cases. Transcripts showing customers frustrated with bot responses, requesting escalation to humans, or reporting follow-up contacts would all contradict the claim. No search for such cases is mentioned.

**Hit rate:** The product team read 30 transcripts and concluded preference. No base rate is provided: how many transcripts show no preference or a stated preference for humans? "Reading of" without a denominator cannot establish a hit rate.

**Next step:** Operationalize preference (survey, rating scale, or binary choice). If retrospective, use the first 30 consecutive chats in chronological order (not curated). Report the proportion showing preference, the inter-rater agreement if multiple people coded transcripts, and any confounds (e.g., customers praised ease of access, not quality of solution).

---

### Claim 3: The bot resolves 70% of tickets on its own

**Tier:** `[Y]` Misleading

**Justification:** The definition of "resolved" is the crux: "the customer closed the chat window." This is a completion metric, not a resolution metric. A customer may close the window because:
- The problem was solved (true resolution).
- The bot failed and the customer gave up (abandonment, not resolution).
- The customer escalated to a human off-platform (unresolved, but chat ended).
- The customer became frustrated and quit (unresolved).

**Mechanism:** The vendor dashboard measures window-closure rate, not problem-solution rate. Vendor has economic incentive to report high resolution (higher fees, contract renewal). No independent audit of what "closed window" actually means in the vendor's code is provided.

**Evidence chain:** Claim "resolves 70%" → evidence "customer closed window 70%" → these are different metrics → [Y].

**Next step:** Audit the vendor's definition in code. Cross-check with post-resolution surveys (did customers report their problem as solved?), repeat-contact rates (did customers return for the same issue?), or escalation patterns (were humans contacted later?). The RCT in claim 4 measures repeat contacts and shows 9.1% vs 11.4% — use that as a ground-truth measure of effective resolution and compare to the vendor's 70% on the same cohort. If 70% of chats end but 9.1% of customers return, the resolution claim is unsupported.

### Claim 4: The bot reduced repeat contacts (randomized test: 9.1% vs 11.4%, 95% CI −3.0 to −1.6 points)

**Tier:** `[K]` Proven

**Justification:** This claim meets the highest evidentiary threshold.

- **Design:** Randomized controlled trial (bot-first vs. human-first assignment).
- **Scale:** 5,000 customers, 6 weeks follow-up window (adequate for the test question).
- **Metric:** Repeat contacts within 7 days, operationally defined.
- **Compute:** Analytics team, not bot team (reduces conflict of interest).
- **Precision:** 95% confidence interval provided (−3.0 to −1.6 percentage points). The interval excludes zero, confirming statistical significance.
- **Magnitude:** 2.3 percentage-point difference at the point estimate, 60% reduction in repeat-contact rate (from 11.4% to 9.1%), is meaningful.

**Refutation test:** The claim would fail if repeat contacts were higher in bot-first or indistinguishable from human-first. The data show the opposite.

**Controls:** Randomization addresses confounds (bot users were not self-selected). Analytics team independence addresses reporting bias.

**Next step:** None required for this claim. Archive as earned [K].

### Claim 5: It will cut support headcount costs by a third next year

**Tier:** `[S]` Speculative

**Justification:** This claim refers to a future state. It cannot be tested until the year ends. The evidence is "a projection from the vendor's sales deck" — the vendor has financial incentive to overstate cost savings. No methodology is disclosed (Did they model support volume? Assume constant volume and lower cost per ticket? Assume reduced headcount?). 

**Thresholds:** None locked. "Cut costs by a third" is precise-sounding but depends on headcount decisions (will the company actually reduce headcount, redeploy staff, or pocket the margin?).

**HARKing direction (reversed):** Unlike claims 1–4, this claim precedes the evidence. However, presenting it to the board as established ("We present these claims to the board tomorrow") when it is speculative inverts the preregistration discipline — it should be presented as a prediction with a locked threshold and a one-year measurement rule, not as a proven claim.

**Next step:** Reframe as a preregistered bet: "We project cost savings of $X by date Y, measured as (method). If savings fall below $X by date Y, the claim will be marked [R]." Lock the threshold and measurement rule before the year ends.

---

### Claim 6: Agents are happier since launch

**Tier:** `[KKE]` Critical control missing

**Justification:** The claim asserts an emotional/psychological state (happiness). The evidence:
1. No direct measure (no survey of agent satisfaction, mood, or engagement).
2. Indirect behavioral proxy: attrition this quarter was lower.
3. Confound: "A hiring freeze started the same month."

**Mechanism of the confound:** A hiring freeze signals job insecurity. Agents already employed become less likely to leave (fear of unemployment or difficulty finding jobs during a hiring wave reversal). This competes with a second hypothesis: "Agents are happier because the bot reduces routine tickets, leaving more interesting cases." Both explain lower attrition; only the first is an artifact of the hiring freeze.

**Evidence chain to refute the "happiness" claim:**
- Attrition drops when hiring freezes → people fear job loss.
- Attrition drops when people fear job loss → people stay in their current job.
- People staying in their current job ≠ people being happier in their current job.

The confound is documented in the claim itself ("a hiring freeze started the same month"), which is good practice — but it bars any tier above [KKE].

**Counter-example:** If agents were happier, one would expect not just lower attrition but also: reduced sick leave, increased productivity metrics, higher internal engagement scores, reduced escalation-to-manager complaints. None of these are reported. The absence of supporting measures is not counter-evidence (they were not collected), but it is a missing card.

**Next step:** Survey agents on job satisfaction, workload, and preference for bot-first vs. human-first cases. Measure pre- and post-launch in a cohort unaffected by the hiring freeze (e.g., agents hired after launch, who have no history to compare). Or, if the hiring freeze is permanent, defer the claim [S] until 12 months after normal hiring resumes and attrition can be compared without the confound.

## Missing Card: What This Board Summary Cannot Show

**Format omission:** The board summary reports only positive or neutral outcomes. Negative findings, if they exist, are not presented. Examples:
- Do users abandon bot chats without escalating (bot chats that end without reaching a human or a resolution)?
- Are any metrics worse after launch (e.g., first-response time for escalated chats, if the bot slows triage)?
- Was there a cost to bot development or maintenance?

**Business impact omission:** The board summary reports operational metrics but not business outcomes. Examples:
- Net revenue retention (would cost savings exceed any lost revenue from customer churn due to bot dissatisfaction?).
- Competitive position (are competitors also adopting bots, and is the gain temporary?).

## Retroactive HARKing Risk

All claims were selected AFTER the measurement period ended. This introduces threshold shopping risk: thresholds may have been set AFTER seeing near-misses. Example: Claim 1's 4.2→40-second improvement is so large that a post-hoc threshold is implausible. But claims 2–3 lack visible thresholds at all; if the product team read transcripts and found weak evidence, the team may have lowered the bar for "proves customer preference."

**Mitigation:** Preregister identical audit for Q3 next year using thresholds locked today.

---

## Recommendations (by criticality × impact/effort)

1. **Claim 6 (agent happiness) — BLOCK.** Do not present "agents are happier" to the board without either (a) a survey, or (b) a waiting period post-hiring-freeze. The evidence does not support it, and the confound is large enough to flip the conclusion. Current claim: REJECT.

2. **Claim 3 (70% resolution) — REVISE.** Replace "resolves 70%" with "customer closes chat in 70% of cases" or link to an independent resolution metric (e.g., "repeat-contact rate 9.1%, suggesting 90.9% were resolved on first contact" — this leans on claim 4, which IS proven). Vendor dashboard alone is not trustworthy; triangulate with claim 4's data or post-resolution surveys.

3. **Claim 2 (customer preference) — DOWNGRADE TO ANECDOTE.** Report as "Product team reviewed 30 chat transcripts and observed customer satisfaction in [specific patterns]" rather than "customers prefer the bot." If preference is important to the business case, commission a survey.

4. **Claim 5 (cost projection) — FRAME AS COMMITTED BET.** Move from vendor deck to a preregistered prediction: "We commit to delivering $X cost savings by [date] via [method]. If we miss, claim status is [R]." This makes it testable.

5. **Claims 1 & 4 — PRESENT AS PROVEN.** These are high-quality. State sample sizes, confidence intervals, and compute teams (analysts) prominently.

---

## Audit Conclusion

Two of six claims are proven [K]. Two are speculative or lacking evidence [S], [H]. One actively misleads via metric definition [Y]. One has a critical confound [KKE]. Presenting all six to the board as "proven and ready for sign-off" would misrepresent the evidence base.

**Next step:** Revise the board summary using the tiers above, defer claims needing more work (2, 3, 5, 6), and table a schedule to resolve them before the next board meeting.
