# Review of Care App Board Claims

I can't give a blanket sign-off. Only one of the six claims (claim 5) is adequately supported as worded. One more (claim 2) comes from a strong design, but its own numbers don't show a clear effect. The other four rest on evidence that can't support the conclusion.

## 1. "Reduces hospital readmissions by 30%" — NOT PROVEN
- **Evidence:** an observational comparison of users and non-users.
- **Baseline/control:** there is no valid control. The note says users were younger and healthier, and younger, healthier people are readmitted less anyway. That is confounding by selection (healthy-user bias).
- **Overreach:** "reduces" is a causal claim, and the evidence only shows a correlation. The 30% figure can't be separated from differences in age and health.
- **Conflicts with claim 2:** the company's own randomized trial found a much smaller difference: 11.2% vs 13.9%, about 19% relative or 2.7 points absolute. When a confounded estimate is larger than the randomized one, trust the randomized one. Drop this claim or replace it with the trial result.

## 2. Randomized trial: 11.2% vs 13.9% at 90 days — NOT PROVEN (as evidence that the app works)
- **Strengths:** this is the best evidence in the deck. It was preregistered and randomized, with a real control group (usual care) and an independent data monitoring board. The reported figures are credible as a description of what happened.
- **Problem, statistical significance:** assuming about 620 patients per arm, the gap of 2.7 points has a standard error of about 1.9 points. That gives z ≈ 1.4 and p ≈ 0.15, and the 95% confidence interval runs from roughly −1 to +6.4 points, so it includes no effect. As presented, the trial does not show a reliable reduction.
- **Missing information:**
  - Was this the preregistered primary endpoint?
  - What were the confidence interval and p-value?
  - What were attrition and the intention-to-treat results?
  - Is there a publication or an independent analysis?
- **What you can say:** "A randomized trial showed a trend toward fewer readmissions (11.2% vs 13.9%). It was not statistically conclusive." Unless the full analysis shows otherwise, don't present it as proof.

## 3. "Doctors recommend it" — NOT PROVEN
- **Evidence:** an endorsement from the company's own medical advisory board.
- **Who judged:** people with a conflict of interest who are paid by or tied to the company. They are not independent, and they are not a representative sample of doctors.
- **Overreach:** "Doctors" implies doctors in general. What you can say is "Our medical advisory board endorses the app," with the relationship disclosed.

## 4. "Patients love it" — NOT PROVEN
- **Evidence:** a 4.8-star rating in the app store.
- **Problems:**
  - Reviewers choose themselves, and happy users and prompted users are more likely to leave a rating.
  - Reviewers aren't confirmed to be patients. Some may be caregivers or casual downloaders, and fake or incentivized reviews are possible.
  - There's no review count and no comparison to competing apps.
  - People who dropped out rarely leave a rating.
- **Overreach:** "love" goes beyond an average star rating. What you can say is "4.8 stars from N app store reviews." A validated satisfaction survey or retention data would make a stronger case.

## 5. 52,000 monthly active users — PROVEN (adequately supported)
- **Evidence:** measured by an analytics platform using a standard MAU definition. The claim is descriptive and doesn't overreach.
- **Minor caveats:**
  - Give the month the figure covers.
  - Confirm that test, internal and bot accounts are excluded, and that one person on several devices isn't counted more than once.
- **Scope:** MAU measures activity, not benefit or patient outcomes.

## 6. "It is safe" — NOT PROVEN
- **Evidence:** no adverse events reported to the company's support email.
- **Problems:**
  - This is passive reporting through a channel that isn't built to collect safety events. Patients and clinicians wouldn't think to report harms there, and a harm such as a missed deterioration or delayed care may never be linked to the app.
  - Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
  - There is no systematic surveillance, no denominator and no comparison group.
- **Better source:** the randomized trial with its independent monitoring board is where adverse events should have been recorded. Report the trial's safety data instead.
- **Overreach:** "safe" is an absolute claim. What you can say is "No adverse events have been reported to us through support channels," or better, cite the trial's safety results.

## Bottom line
Tell the team lead that these claims are not all proven. For the board:
- **Claim 1:** drop it, because the trial contradicts it.
- **Claim 2:** reframe it honestly, with the confidence interval.
- **Claims 3 and 4:** reword them to say what the evidence actually is.
- **Claim 5:** keep it.
- **Claim 6:** replace it with the trial's safety data.
