# Review: Care app board summary

I can't sign off on all six claims as proven. Two are adequately supported. Four have specific problems that the board should hear about before anyone relies on them.

## 1. "Reduces hospital readmissions by 30%": NOT PROVEN
- **Evidence:** This is an observational comparison of people who used the app and people who didn't.
- **Problem:** The two groups weren't comparable. The users were younger and healthier. Those factors lower readmission on their own, so the gap may have nothing to do with the app. This is confounding plus self-selection: healthier, more engaged patients are the ones who choose to use the app.
- **Missing:** There was no randomization and no adjustment for the differences between groups. The causal wording ("reduces") goes further than a correlation can support.
- **Conflict with claim 2:** Your own randomized trial (claim 2) shows a relative reduction of about 19% (13.9% down to 11.2%). The 30% figure is inflated and should be dropped or replaced with the trial result.

## 2. RCT, 11.2% vs 13.9% 90-day readmission: PROVEN (as stated)
- **Why it holds:** The trial was preregistered and randomized, it compared against usual care, it was reasonably large (n = 1,240), and an independent data monitoring board oversaw it. That is strong evidence, and the claim only reports what was observed.
- **Caveats to disclose:**
  - Report the absolute reduction: 2.7 percentage points, about 37 patients treated per readmission avoided.
  - Report the confidence interval and p-value. With about 620 patients per arm, a 2.7-point difference is near the edge of significance.
  - Confirm that the primary endpoint matches the preregistration.
  - Say whether the analysis was intention-to-treat.
  - Don't generalize beyond the trial population.

## 3. "Doctors recommend it": NOT PROVEN
- **Evidence:** The company's own paid medical advisory board endorses it.
- **Problem:** These doctors have a conflict of interest and aren't a sample of doctors in general. There's no independent survey and no uptake by clinicians. At most, this supports "our advisory board endorses it."

## 4. "Patients love it": NOT PROVEN
- **Evidence:** A 4.8-star rating in the app store.
- **Problems:**
  - The raters chose themselves, and satisfied users are overrepresented.
  - Reviews can be solicited or fake.
  - We don't know how many ratings there are.
  - There's no comparison with similar apps.
  - Ratings may come from caregivers rather than patients.
  - "Love" goes well beyond what a rating shows.
- **What the evidence supports:** "4.8 stars from N app store ratings."

## 5. 52,000 monthly active users: PROVEN (as a descriptive metric)
- **Why it holds:** The number comes from an analytics platform using the standard MAU definition. It's a direct measurement and the claim doesn't go beyond it.
- **Caveats:**
  - State the date or period.
  - Check for duplicate devices, bots, or test accounts.
  - MAU counts activity, not clinical benefit.

## 6. "It is safe": NOT PROVEN
- **Evidence:** No adverse events were reported to the support email.
- **Problem:** Absence of reports is not evidence that the app is safe. Passive reporting through a support inbox badly undercounts harm: patients don't link harm to an app, and they don't email support about clinical events.
- **Missing:** There was no systematic monitoring, no comparison group, and no independent adjudication.
- **Better source:** The trial in claim 2 is where safety data should come from. Report its adverse-event data, if collected, as monitored by the independent data monitoring board. Also consider regulatory obligations: if the app is a medical device, formal post-market surveillance may be required.

## Recommendation
Present claims 2 and 5 with the caveats above. For the other four:
- **Claim 1:** Replace it with the trial result.
- **Claim 3:** Reword it as "our advisory board endorses it."
- **Claim 4:** Reword it as the rating with the number of ratings.
- **Claim 6:** Replace it with the trial's safety data, or drop it.
