# Board Review: Care App Claims Assessment

## Detailed Analysis

**Claim 1: "The app reduces hospital readmissions by 30%"**

This claim rests on an observational comparison of users versus non-users, but the evidence explicitly notes that users were younger and healthier. This is a critical confounding variable that invalidates the causal inference. Younger, healthier individuals naturally experience fewer readmissions regardless of app use. No randomized control, no matching, no adjustment for baseline risk is mentioned. The 30% reduction likely reflects selection bias rather than app efficacy. The conclusion extends far beyond what an uncontrolled observational comparison can support.

**Claim 2: "In a preregistered randomized trial (n = 1,240) with an independent data monitoring board, 90-day readmission was 11.2% with the app vs 13.9% with usual care."**

This claim is precisely what it states—the numerical results from a preregistered RCT with an independent data monitoring board. The trial design is rigorous and controls for confounding. The claim reports a factual outcome from the trial without overreaching into causal language. While we cannot assess statistical significance or clinical meaningfulness from this summary alone, the claim itself accurately describes what the trial found.

**Claim 3: "Doctors recommend it"**

The evidence provided is that "the company's own medical advisory board endorses it." This is not representative of doctors broadly. Medical advisory boards selected by a company likely include individuals with pre-existing relationships or alignment with company interests. The claim suggests broader professional endorsement ("doctors") but the evidence is limited to a self-selected, potentially biased board. This is a meaningful gap between claim and evidence.

**Claim 4: "Patients love it"**

The evidence is a 4.8-star app store rating. This reflects a self-selected sample (only people motivated to rate leave ratings), is subject to selection bias (satisfied or very dissatisfied users disproportionately rate), provides no context for whether 4.8 is exceptionally high for health apps, and omits the denominator (how many users rated it?). "Love" is an emotional claim; 4.8 stars is a numeric metric that does not necessarily equal love. No baseline or comparison provided.

**Claim 5: "The app has 52,000 monthly active users"**

This is a factual claim about a metric from the company's analytics platform using a standard MAU definition. It reports what the company's data shows without causal inference. The use of a standard definition adds credibility. While we cannot independently verify the analytics accuracy, the claim accurately represents what the data reportedly shows.

**Claim 6: "It is safe"**

The evidence is that "no adverse events were reported to the company's support email." This confuses absence of reports with absence of events. Lack of reported adverse events could reflect: low reporting rates, users not contacting support for adverse events, underreporting of mild events, or safety issues that haven't yet surfaced. No prospective safety monitoring, no comparative safety data, no signal detection system is mentioned. The jump from "no emails about adverse events" to "It is safe" is unsupported.
