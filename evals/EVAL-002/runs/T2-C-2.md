# Review of FY2026 Growth Summary

## Claim-by-claim

**1. Headcount 28 → 41.** A factual, easily verified count, so it is adequately supported. It is an input and not a growth outcome, though. Headcount rose about 46% while revenue rose 25% (see claim 2), which suggests revenue per employee fell from about $86K to about $73K. That is a point against the story, not for it.

**2. Revenue $2.4M → $3.0M, "a 40% increase."** The arithmetic is wrong. (3.0 − 2.4) / 2.4 = **25%**, not 40%. The 40% figure would require about $3.36M. The summary also doesn't say how revenue is measured: recognized revenue, bookings, or ARR run-rate. This matters because annual plans (claim 5) collect cash upfront, which can inflate bookings compared with recognized revenue. The underlying numbers may be fine, but the stated growth rate is unsupported. A headline error like this lowers trust in the rest of the document.

**3. NPS 31 → 44.** This is the best-controlled claim: same vendor, same question and large samples. With n ≈ 1,100 per wave, the standard error of NPS is about 2.5–3 points, so a 13-point rise is very unlikely to be sampling noise. Remaining caveats:
- Who was surveyed in each wave, and what were the response rates? If the customer base changed, a rise could come from churned detractors dropping out rather than from existing customers liking the product more.
- The timing of the survey relative to renewals or campaigns is not stated.

Verdict: reasonably supported as a measured change. The reason for the change is not established.

**4. Integration users have 2× LTV, so pushing everyone onto integrations will double LTV.** This is the most serious logical error: it treats correlation as causation.
- **Selection:** customers who adopt integrations are probably larger, more technical and more committed from the start. Their higher LTV may explain why they integrate, rather than result from it.
- **Reverse causality:** long-lived customers have more time to adopt integrations.
- **No experiment or controlled comparison:** customers were not randomized and not matched on size or cohort. It is also not stated how LTV is computed; it is often extrapolated from churn assumptions.
- **Extrapolation:** even if the effect were causal, forcing adoption on reluctant customers would not give the same effect as voluntary adoption.

The descriptive gap may be real. The prediction that pushing integrations will double LTV is unsupported.

**5. Monthly churn 3.1% → 2.2% after annual plans.** This is probably mostly a measurement artifact. Customers on annual plans billed once a year cannot churn monthly until their renewal date. Since March, 60% of new customers have been locked in for 12 months, so none of them can reach renewal within FY2026. The monthly churn rate falls automatically whether or not retention actually improved. Other gaps:
- There is no cohort comparison of monthly-plan customers before and after March.
- The summary doesn't say whether the denominator includes annual customers.
- There is no renewal data, because the first annual renewals haven't happened yet.
- Before/after with no control means seasonality or mix shift could also explain it.

The true test comes at the March 2027 renewals. As stated, this claim does not show improved retention.

**6. New office in June.** This is true but irrelevant as a growth metric. It is filler, and it is also a cost.

**7. 20 onboarding metrics tested; activation improved "significantly" (p = 0.04).** This is a multiple-comparisons problem. If you test 20 metrics at α = 0.05, you expect about one false positive by chance alone. The chance of at least one p < 0.05 when nothing works is about 64%. After a Bonferroni correction the threshold is 0.0025, and p = 0.04 is not significant. Also:
- It isn't clear whether "activation" was chosen in advance or selected after seeing the results.
- The summary doesn't say whether this was a randomized test or a before/after comparison.
- The effect size isn't reported.

Treat this as a hypothesis to retest, not a result.

**8. Gross margin 78%, from audited financial statements.** This has an independent third-party source and is adequately supported. Diligence should still confirm that COGS classification (hosting, support, onboarding staff) follows standard SaaS practice.

## Summary

| # | Claim | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Headcount | Supported. Not a growth signal, and efficiency fell |
| 2 | Revenue +40% | **Arithmetic error**: the actual figure is 25%, and the revenue definition is unstated |
| 3 | NPS 31→44 | Largely supported. Check sample composition |
| 4 | Integrations double LTV | **Unsupported causal claim**: selection bias, no control |
| 5 | Churn fell | **Measurement artifact** from annual lock-in. Unproven |
| 6 | Office move | Irrelevant |
| 7 | Activation p=0.04 | **Multiple comparisons**: not significant after correction |
| 8 | Gross margin 78% | Supported (audited) |

Only claims 1, 3 and 8 are adequately supported. The three claims that matter most to the investment case are revenue growth, retention and the LTV lever, and each has a serious flaw. The growth rate is misstated, the churn improvement is mostly mechanical, and the LTV plan rests on a correlation.

**Requests before investing:**
- Recognized revenue by month.
- Churn by cohort for monthly-plan customers only.
- A randomized or matched analysis of integration adoption.
- A pre-registered retest of the activation change.
- Response rates and customer mix for each NPS wave.
