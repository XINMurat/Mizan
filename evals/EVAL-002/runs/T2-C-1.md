# Review: FY2026 Growth Summary

## Claim-by-claim

**1. Headcount 28 → 41.** Adequately supported as a plain fact that can be checked against payroll. It is an input, though, not an outcome. A 46% rise in headcount against a 25% rise in revenue (see claim 2) means revenue per employee *fell*, from about $86K to about $73K. The summary does not mention this.

**2. Revenue $2.4M → $3.0M, "a 40% increase."** The arithmetic is wrong: (3.0 − 2.4) / 2.4 = **25%**, not 40%. This is a factual error in the headline growth number, and it hurts the credibility of the whole document. Other things to check: is this recognized revenue or bookings/ARR? Is it audited (claim 8 cites audited statements, so why not here)? How much of it is one-time or services revenue? With 60% of new customers prepaying annually (claim 5), bookings and cash could run well ahead of recognized revenue, so the basis needs stating.

**3. NPS 31 → 44.** Reasonably well supported. It uses the same vendor and the same question, and both samples are large (~1,100 each). The standard error of NPS at these sizes is roughly ±2.5–3 points, so a 13-point move is very unlikely to be noise. Open questions: response rate and who got surveyed (all customers or only active ones?). If churned or unhappy customers dropped out of the sampling frame, NPS goes up without anyone becoming more satisfied. Survivorship could also play a part, since unhappy customers left and the remaining base looks happier. I'd accept the claim with those caveats noted.

**4. Integration users have 2× LTV, "so pushing every customer onto integrations will double LTV."** This is a causal conclusion drawn from a correlation, and it is the most overreaching claim in the summary. Customers who adopt integrations are self-selected: they are likely larger, more committed, more technical, or already getting more value. That confounding can produce the 2× gap on its own. Reverse causation is also plausible, since engaged, long-lived customers have more time and more reason to set up integrations. Customers who get pushed onto integrations are not the same population as those who chose them, so the effect would likely be far smaller than 2×, possibly zero. There is also no experiment, no matched comparison, and no definition of how LTV was computed (it is often extrapolated from churn assumptions). Not supported as stated.

**5. Monthly churn 3.1% → 2.2% after annual plans.** This comparison is confounded by measurement. A customer on an annual plan billed once a year *cannot* churn monthly until renewal. Since 60% of new customers since March are on annual plans, and none of them have reached a renewal date yet (at most ~7–9 months in), the drop in monthly churn is largely mechanical: churn has been deferred, not reduced. The honest test is renewal rates at the 12-month mark and cohort retention compared on a like-for-like basis, with monthly-plan customers analyzed separately. There is also no control for other changes over the same period (product, pricing, customer mix, and the NPS change). Not adequately supported; the figure may flatter the company substantially.

**6. New office in June.** A true, checkable fact that is irrelevant as a growth metric. Including it adds bulk but no evidence.

**7. "Tested changes against 20 onboarding metrics; activation improved significantly (p = 0.04)."** This is a classic multiple-comparisons problem. With 20 metrics tested at α = 0.05, you expect about one "significant" result by chance alone, and the chance of at least one false positive is ~64%. p = 0.04 on one metric out of 20 is exactly what noise looks like. A Bonferroni-corrected threshold would be 0.0025. Also missing: whether activation was the preregistered primary metric (it looks cherry-picked), the effect size, the design (randomized A/B test or before/after?), and sample size. Not supported.

**8. Gross margin 78% from audited financial statements.** The best-supported claim in the document. It has independent third-party verification and a standard definition. Minor checks: confirm what is included in COGS (hosting, support, onboarding staff), because SaaS companies vary in this, and confirm which period the audit covers. Adequately supported.

## Summary

| # | Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Headcount | Supported (fact), but it implies revenue per employee fell |
| 2 | Revenue +40% | **Wrong**: the actual figure is 25%; the revenue basis is unstated |
| 3 | NPS 31 → 44 | Mostly supported; check the sampling frame and survivorship |
| 4 | Integrations will double LTV | **Unsupported**: correlation presented as causation, with selection bias |
| 5 | Churn fell | **Misleading**: annual billing mechanically suppresses monthly churn |
| 6 | New office | True but irrelevant |
| 7 | Activation p = 0.04 | **Unsupported**: 1 hit in 20 tests is what chance produces |
| 8 | Gross margin 78% | Supported (audited) |

**Adequately supported:** 1, 6 (as facts), 8, and largely 3.
**Problems:** 2 (arithmetic error), 4 (causal overreach), 5 (measurement artifact), 7 (multiple comparisons).

The pattern is worrying for due diligence. Every error or overreach flatters the company, and the one audited number is the one with no problems. Before relying on this summary, request: the corrected revenue basis, cohort retention and renewal data, the design and full results of the onboarding tests, and the method used to compute LTV.
