# Evaluation of FY2026 Growth Summary

Tiers used: **Verified** (checkable from the document itself or a cited audited source), **Plausible** (consistent with the evidence given, but not proven), **Unsupported** (the conclusion does not follow from the evidence), **Wrong** (contradicted by the document's own numbers).

| # | Claim | Verdict |
|---|-------|---------|
| 1 | Headcount 28 → 41 | Plausible, and it doesn't matter much |
| 2 | Revenue +40% | **Wrong**: the numbers give 25% |
| 3 | NPS 31 → 44 | Plausible, and the strongest claim here |
| 4 | Integrations will double LTV | **Unsupported**: treats correlation as cause |
| 5 | Churn 3.1% → 2.2% | **Likely artifact** of annual billing |
| 6 | New office | Irrelevant; no evidence value |
| 7 | Activation improved significantly | **Unsupported**: multiple comparisons |
| 8 | Gross margin 78% | Plausible and anchored; ask for the statements |

## Claim by claim

**1. Headcount 28 → 41 (+46%).** This is easy to check and probably true. It is an input, not an outcome. Read it next to claim 2: headcount grew 46% while revenue grew 25%, so revenue per employee fell from about $86K to about $73K. That is a decline in efficiency, not a sign of growth.

**2. Revenue $2.4M → $3.0M, "a 40% increase."** This is an arithmetic error. (3.0 − 2.4) / 2.4 = **25%**. You get 40% only by dividing the $0.6M gain by some other base, or by using different numbers. In a fundraising document this mistake matters on its own: either the headline growth rate is inflated, or the underlying figures are not the ones shown. Ask them to reconcile it and to say whether "revenue" means recognized revenue or bookings. This matters because of claim 5: annual plans billed upfront can push bookings above recognized revenue.

**3. NPS 31 → 44.** This is the best-supported claim. The vendor and question stayed the same, and the samples are large (n ≈ 1,100 each). With samples that size, the standard error on NPS is about ±2.5–3 points, so a 13-point rise is very unlikely to be noise. Open questions:
- Was the sampled population the same both years? Surveying only active customers or annual-plan customers would bias the result upward.
- What were the response rates?
- Were survivors oversampled? Churned customers drop out of the frame.

**4. "Integration users have 2× LTV, so pushing everyone onto integrations will double LTV."** This conclusion does not follow from the data. Customers who adopt integrations are probably already more engaged, larger, or better fits for the product. That is selection bias, and it also runs the other way: committed customers take the effort to integrate. The 2× gap is an observed correlation, not the effect of an intervention. Customers pushed onto integrations will not look like the ones who chose them. "Double" is the upper bound and treats the whole gap as causal. The realistic effect is somewhere between 0 and 2×, with no evidence for any particular value. To test the claim, they would need a randomized or staggered integration push and a comparison of retention against a holdout group. It also matters how LTV is estimated for a young company with short customer histories.

**5. Monthly churn 3.1% → 2.2% after annual plans.** This is probably an artifact of how churn is measured. Customers billed once a year cannot churn in any month before renewal. Since March, 60% of new customers have been on annual plans, and none of them has reached a renewal date yet. That alone pushes measured monthly churn down, whether or not retention actually improved. Ask for:
- churn broken out by plan type,
- cohort retention curves for monthly-plan customers only,
- the definition of churn (logo vs. revenue; cancellation date vs. end of term).

The real test comes in March 2027 onward, when the first annual cohorts come up for renewal. Until then, the improvement is not established.

**6. New office in June.** This says nothing about traction and has no evidence value. At most, it adds to the fixed-cost base, which bears on burn.

**7. "Activation improved significantly (p = 0.04)" after testing 20 metrics.** With 20 metrics tested at α = 0.05, you would expect about one false positive by chance alone. The chance of at least one is 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64%. A single p = 0.04 picked out of 20 does not survive a Bonferroni correction (threshold 0.0025) or even a lenient correction. This looks like cherry-picking a result after the fact. Ask:
- Was activation the pre-registered primary metric?
- What are the effect size and confidence interval?
- Did the result replicate?

**8. Gross margin 78%, from audited statements.** This claim points to a source that can be checked, which makes it the most trustworthy in the list, provided the statements exist. 78% is plausible for SaaS. Confirm:
- that the audit covers FY2026,
- which costs sit in COGS (hosting, support, onboarding/implementation staff),
- whether the new headcount was booked below the gross-margin line.

## Overall assessment

- **Solid:** NPS (3), and gross margin (8) if the audit checks out.
- **Wrong or overstated:** the revenue growth rate (2), which is 25%, not 40%.
- **Not supported by the evidence given:** the LTV projection (4), the churn improvement (5), and the activation win (7). Each has a specific flaw: correlation treated as cause, a measurement artifact, and multiple comparisons.
- **Padding:** the office move (6), and headcount (1) presented as growth.

Put together, the real picture is more modest than the summary suggests: revenue grew 25%, headcount grew faster than revenue, and the retention and product claims can't be verified yet.

**Items to request before investing:**
1. A reconciled revenue figure: recognized revenue vs. bookings.
2. Churn broken out by plan type, with monthly-plan cohort curves.
3. The pre-registration or test plan for the activation experiment, with effect sizes.
4. The audited statements, with the COGS breakdown.
5. Any causal evidence (experiment or holdout) for the integration effect on LTV.
