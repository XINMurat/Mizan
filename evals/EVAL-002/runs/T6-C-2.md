# Review: Annual security assessment

## Bottom line
Three claims hold up: 1, 3 and 8. Claims 2, 4, 5 and 7 draw conclusions their evidence does not support. The biggest problem is inside the report itself: claim 4 says scan coverage was complete (48 services), but Appendix A lists 53 production services. That gap undermines claims 2 and 4 directly.

## Claim by claim

**1. The team has 6 people.** Supported. This is a simple fact the organization knows about itself. No inference is involved.

**2. The scanner found 0 critical findings, so the platform has no critical vulnerabilities.** Not supported. There are three problems:
- **The conclusion covers more than was tested.** The scan covered "the services it scanned", which is 48. The inventory has 53. At least 5 production services (about 9%) were never scanned, so nothing can be said about them.
- **A clean scan is not the same as a clean platform.** Scanners only find known signatures they can reach. They miss logic flaws, misconfigurations outside their checks, and unauthenticated blind spots. The report gives no measure of the scanner's detection rate, such as tests against known-vulnerable targets.
- **No scan configuration is given.** It is unclear whether scans were authenticated, which rule sets were used, or what time period they covered.
Safe restatement: "0 critical findings from automated scanning of 48 of 53 production services."

**3. Mean time to patch high-severity issues dropped from 41 to 12 days.** Adequately supported. The data comes from a system of record, and the severity definitions stayed the same across both years, which removes the most obvious confound. Some caveats:
- The report gives no counts (n) and no distribution. A mean can be skewed by a few outliers, so the median should also be shown.
- Check that the closing criteria did not change, for example tickets closed as "risk accepted".
- Check that the mix of issues did not shift toward easier fixes.
None of these caveats reverses the result.

**4. Scan coverage was complete: 48 production services were scanned.** Contradicted by the report's own appendix: 48 is less than 53. Possible explanations:
- Some services were deliberately excluded.
- The inventory changed after the scan.
- There are counting or naming differences between the two lists.
None of these is documented. Until the two lists are reconciled service by service, coverage is about 91%, not complete.

**5. Our platform team's penetration test found no privilege-escalation path.** Weak support. The problems:
- **Not independent.** The platform team is testing the systems it builds and runs. That is a conflict of interest and leaves shared blind spots.
- **Unknown scope and method.** The report does not say which systems were in scope, how long the test ran, what access the testers started with, or what methods they used.
- **No evidence the test could find anything.** The report does not show the testers' qualifications or any record of past findings.
- **Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.** Not finding an escalation path is not the same as there being none.
To support this claim, the report would need an independent external test with a documented scope.

**6. Appendix A: 53 production services (CMDB export, 2026-09-01).** Supported as a record, since it has a stated source and date. Two caveats: a CMDB can be incomplete (shadow IT), so 53 is a minimum. The export date should also be compared with the scan dates.

**7. 99.7% training completion, so phishing risk is now negligible.** Not supported. The premise may well be true, but it does not lead to the conclusion:
- **Completing training does not measure behavior.** The report shows no results from phishing simulations, such as click rates or reporting rates, and no comparison with an earlier baseline.
- **Even well-trained staff get fooled.** Well-trained populations still click on some phishing emails, and one compromised account can be enough.
- **"Negligible" is an unmeasured, absolute claim.**
Safe restatement: "99.7% completed training." Any claim about risk needs behavioral data, such as simulation results compared with the prior year.

**8. MFA is enforced on all admin accounts; the IdP policy export shows 0 exceptions.** Adequately supported. The evidence is direct, comes from the system that enforces the policy, and is attached. Remaining gaps:
- **Admin accounts outside the IdP.** The export does not cover local, break-glass, service or cloud-root accounts that are not managed through the identity provider.
- **Admin group definition.** Confirm that the "admin" group in the export matches the actual set of privileged accounts.

## What the board should ask for
1. A service-by-service reconciliation of the 48 scanned services against the 53 in the inventory, with a reason for each exclusion.
2. Claim 2 restated to cover only the scope that was actually scanned, plus the scanner's configuration.
3. An independent penetration test with a documented scope.
4. Phishing-simulation metrics compared with a baseline, in place of the "negligible risk" claim.
5. Counts and medians for the patch-time metric.
