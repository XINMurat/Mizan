# Review: Annual security assessment — are the conclusions supported?

**Bottom line:** Two of the headline conclusions (no critical vulnerabilities; complete scan coverage) are contradicted by the report's own appendix. Two more (no privilege escalation; negligible phishing risk) go further than their evidence allows. Claims 1, 3 and 8 are adequately supported, with small caveats.

## Claim by claim

**1. The team has 6 people.** Supported. This is a simple fact about the organisation. It says nothing about whether the team is adequate, and the report doesn't claim that it is.

**2. "0 critical findings, so the platform has no critical vulnerabilities."** Not supported.
- **Coverage gap:** The scanner covered only "the services it scanned": 48 services. The CMDB lists 53 (Appendix A). So 5 production services (about 9%) were never scanned. Zero findings on 48 services tells you nothing about the other 5.
- **Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence:** A scanner finds known signatures and misconfigurations. It does not find logic flaws, chained vulnerabilities, zero-days, or anything it has no credentials to reach. The report gives no scanner configuration (authenticated or not, which plugins, which date) and no validation, such as seeded known vulnerabilities or a false-negative rate.
- **Correct wording:** "The scanner reported 0 critical findings on 48 of 53 production services."

**3. Mean time to patch high-severity issues fell from 41 to 12 days.** Adequately supported. It has a baseline (last year), a named data source, and consistent definitions. Caveats worth asking about:
- **Averages:** A mean can hide a long tail. Ask for the median, the 90th percentile, and how many issues are still open (unpatched items drop out of an average of closed tickets).
- **Volume and mix:** Did the number or type of high-severity issues change between years?
- **Measurement:** Did ticket-closing practice change? For example, closing a ticket on mitigation rather than on the actual patch.

**4. "Scan coverage was complete: 48 production services were scanned."** Contradicted by Appendix A (53 services). Coverage was 48/53, about 90.6%. The report never names the 5 missing services or explains why they were left out. Possible explanations include out-of-scope systems, decommissioned services, or services added after the scan. Note that the CMDB export is dated 2026-09-01; the scan date isn't given, so the two numbers may come from different points in time. That timing gap needs reconciling. It does not justify calling coverage "complete".

**5. "A penetration test by our platform team found no way to escalate privileges."** Weakly supported. The conclusion goes beyond the evidence.
- **Who judged:** The testers are the internal team that builds and runs the platform. They are not independent, they share the same blind spots, and they have an incentive to find nothing. A board-level assurance claim normally calls for an independent third party.
- **No scope or method:** The report doesn't say which systems, which starting privilege level, which techniques, how much time, or which methodology was used. "Found no way" is limited to what was actually tried.
- **No control:** There is no evidence that the testers would have found an escalation path if one existed (for example, earlier findings or a planted issue).

**6. Appendix A: 53 production services (CMDB export, 2026-09-01).** Supported as a dated inventory, assuming the CMDB is accurate. Its main value in this review is that it undercuts claims 2 and 4.

**7. "99.7% completed security training, so phishing risk is now negligible."** Not supported.
- **Measures activity, not outcome:** A completion rate shows people finished the training. It doesn't show they behave differently.
- **No outcome data:** There are no phishing-simulation click or report rates and no real incident counts, either before or after the training.
- **"Negligible" doesn't follow:** Phishing succeeds even against trained staff. It takes one click, and the remaining 0.3% (and anyone who clicks despite training) is still exposed. There is no threshold defining "negligible" and no baseline to compare against.
- **Correct wording:** "Training completion was 99.7%."

**8. MFA is enforced on all admin accounts: IdP policy export shows 0 exceptions (attached).** Adequately supported. This claim comes with primary, attached, checkable evidence. Caveats:
- **Definition of admin:** It depends on how "admin accounts" is defined. Break-glass accounts, service accounts, and local or cloud-console admins outside the IdP wouldn't appear in the export.
- **Point in time:** The export shows policy, not enforcement at the moment of each login. Legacy authentication paths that bypass MFA should also be checked.

## Cross-cutting problems
- **Contradiction inside the report:** 48 scanned against 53 in inventory. The document contradicts itself, which lowers confidence in the rest of it.
- **Overreach from a metric to a risk conclusion:** Claims 2, 5 and 7 each move from a limited measurement to a sweeping statement ("no critical vulnerabilities", "no way to escalate", "negligible risk").
- **Missing baselines:** Only claim 3 compares against a prior period.
- **No independent verification:** Every assessment was produced by internal teams. None of it has been validated by a third party.

## Recommendations before the board relies on this
1. Reconcile the 48 and 53 service counts, and scan the remaining 5 or record them as explicit exclusions.
2. Rephrase claims 2, 4, 5 and 7 so they match the evidence.
3. Commission an independent penetration test with a documented scope.
4. Report phishing-simulation outcomes, not just training completion.
5. Add the median and 90th-percentile time to patch, plus the count of open high-severity issues, to claim 3.

**Adequately supported:** 1, 3, 6, 8. **Unsupported or overreaching:** 2, 4, 5, 7.
