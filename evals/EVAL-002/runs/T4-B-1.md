# Mizan Audit: Checkout outage postmortem (2026-09-18)

**Mode:** Audit (retrospective). **HARKing status:** the postmortem was written after the outcome was known, and so was this audit. Neither one preregistered anything. **Coverage:** I checked 9 of 9 claims for internal consistency. For 0 of 9 did I check the primary sources (pager logs, LB logs, deploy logs, the DNS status page, order data), because I was given only the document. So each "supported" verdict below means "consistent with the document and cites a source", and not "verified by me".

**Bottom line:** the timeline and measurement facts are mostly usable. The two conclusions that matter most are **not supported as written**: the root cause (claim 4) and "no customer data affected" (claim 5). The document also contradicts itself on the duration, and claim 6 contains a live rival explanation that the postmortem mentions and then never deals with. It should not be published as-is.

## Surviving conclusions (credit where due)
- The peak error rate of 38% is backed by a named instrument. This is the kind of sourcing the rest of the document needs.
- The start and end timestamps are specific enough to be checked.
- An alert now exists. That is a real action item, even though the evidence for its value is weak.

## What the format cannot show (the missing card)
- **Ruled-out hypotheses.** The postmortem names one cause and records no alternatives. The DNS incident is sitting in the document as an alternative that was never examined.
- **The 14:03 to 14:05 detection gap** and how the 14:03 start time was established.
- **The time from page (14:05) to rollback (14:50), 45 minutes:** what was tried during that time and why rollback came so late. Most of what can be learned from an incident is usually here, and it's missing.
- **Customer impact in numbers:** failed checkouts, lost revenue, and how many customers were affected.

## Rival hypotheses for the root cause (should be preregistered before re-analysis)
- **H-A:** the 8.2 cache eviction change caused the errors.
- **H-B:** the upstream DNS incident caused the errors, and the rollback was coincidental.
- **H-C:** the two interacted. For example, eviction caused more cache misses, more misses caused more DNS lookups, and those lookups ran into the degraded DNS provider.

A check that can tell them apart (both outcomes are informative): break the LB errors down by type (DNS resolution failures and upstream timeouts versus application 5xx), look at their timing against the DNS incident's start, and redeploy 8.2 to staging or a canary while DNS is healthy. If errors come back with DNS healthy, the evidence points to H-A. If 8.2 runs clean, H-B or H-C gains weight. Arbiter class: `instrument` (logs, canary metrics), not `author`.

## Next steps (ordered by criticality × impact/effort)
1. **Deal with the DNS confound before naming a root cause.** Until that's done, rewrite claim 4 as "suspected cause: 8.2 cache eviction change; the concurrent DNS incident has not yet been ruled out."
2. **Rewrite claim 5** to say what was actually done: which three services were checked, how, and what was found. Then list every service that writes order or customer data and check the rest, or state which ones remain unchecked.
3. **Fix the duration** (47 vs 49 minutes) and give sources for the timestamps in claims 1, 2 and 4.
4. **Re-evaluate the alert** on traffic it wasn't tuned on: measure its false-positive rate over several weeks of normal traffic. Also consider a lower or faster threshold, since firing at 14:06 is no improvement on the page at 14:05.
5. **Replace claim 8** with measurable action items, each with an owner, a due date, and a metric.
6. Add the investigation timeline for 14:05 to 14:50 and the customer-impact numbers.
