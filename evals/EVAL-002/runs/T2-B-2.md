# Mizan Audit Report — "Growth summary, FY2026" (fundraising metrics)

## Header

- **Mode:** Audit (retrospective).
- **Target:** 8-item metrics summary sent to investors during a raise.
- **HARKing status:** The document is retrospective: the company chose which metrics to report after seeing the year's outcomes. This audit is retrospective too. I set my thresholds (arithmetic must reconcile; causal claims need a design that can separate cause from selection; significance claims need a correction for multiple comparisons) while reading the claims, not before.
- **Evidence access:** I had only the summary text. I had no financial statements, billing data, survey exports or experiment logs. So a claim can only be checked for **internal consistency, arithmetic and inferential validity**. Any claim that needs source data is capped at `[H]` until that data is produced.
- **Coverage:** 8 of 8 items were atomized into 12 atomic claims. 12 of 12 were checked for internal logic. 0 of 12 were checked against primary source data, because none was available.
- **Operating constraint:** I had no tool access beyond the prompt, so I could not ask a domain owner anything (step 7). That limit is disclosed below.

## Evidence tiers used

`[K]` Kanıtlanmış / Proven · `[H]` Makul Hipotez / Plausible hypothesis · `[S]` Spekülatif / Speculative · `[R]` Reddedildi / Refuted · `[KKE]` Kritik Kontrol Eksik / Critical control missing · `[Y]` Yanıltıcı / Misleading

## Claim-by-claim

| # | Claim (atomized) | Tier | Justification |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Headcount grew from 28 to 41." | `[H]` | Plausible and easy to verify from payroll, but not verified. It matters as context for claim 2 (see Conjunctions). |
| 2a | "Revenue grew from $2.4M to $3.0M." | `[H]` | It could be verified from the audited statements cited in item 8, but those were not provided. |
| 2b | "…a 40% increase." | `[R]` | The arithmetic is wrong. (3.0 − 2.4) / 2.4 = **25%**, not 40%. 40% would need about $3.36M, which is $360k more. The headline figure overstates growth by 15 percentage points, 1.6× the true growth. One sentence contradicts itself, and the error favors the company. That is a red flag for how the whole document was prepared. |
| 3a | "NPS rose from 31 to 44." | `[H]` | Unverified raw data. The method is held constant (same vendor, same question), which deserves credit. With n ≈ 1,100 per wave, the standard error of NPS is roughly ±2.5–3 points, so a 13-point change is well beyond sampling noise *if* the two samples are comparable. |
| 3b | (implied) "Customers became more satisfied." | `[KKE]` | Same instrument does not mean same population. Control missing: response rate and respondent mix in each wave. If churned, unhappy customers dropped out of the base (claim 5), or annual-plan customers are over-represented, NPS rises through survivorship without any customer changing their opinion. |
| 4a | "Integration users have 2× the LTV of non-users." | `[H]` | Plausible as a correlation. It is unverified, and the LTV method is not stated. |
| 4b | "…so pushing every customer onto integrations will double LTV." | `[Y]` | This is a causal claim built on an observational comparison. Customers who adopt integrations are self-selected: larger, more engaged, already committed. Selection would produce the same 2× gap even if integrations caused nothing. "Pushing" customers who chose not to integrate also changes who gets treated, so the effect on them could be smaller or zero. The claim that could be defended is "integration adoption correlates with LTV". "Will double" needs an experiment, such as randomized integration onboarding, or at least a comparison matched on size and cohort. |
| 5a | "Monthly churn fell from 3.1% to 2.2%." | `[H]` | Unverified number. |
| 5b | (implied) "Annual plans reduced churn / customers are stickier." | `[Y]` / `[KKE]` | This is mostly a **measurement artifact**. A customer billed once a year cannot churn in any month except the renewal month. With 60% of new customers on annual plans since March, most of the new cohort *cannot show up* in monthly churn until March 2027 or later, when the first renewals come due. The fall from 3.1% to 2.2% is what locking customers in would produce even if retention did not change at all. Control needed: churn on monthly plans only, cohort by cohort, before and after March, plus the annual renewal rate once renewals happen. Until then the retention improvement is untested. |
| 6 | "We moved to a new office in June." | `[H]` | Probably true, but it is not a growth metric. Listing it here pads the list. It also adds a cost (rent) that the summary never mentions. |
| 7a | "We tested changes against 20 onboarding metrics." | `[H]` | Useful disclosure, and credit for making it. |
| 7b | "Activation rate improved significantly (p = 0.04)." | `[Y]` | This is a multiple-comparisons problem. With 20 metrics tested at α = 0.05, you expect about **1** false positive by chance alone, and P(at least one p < 0.05 under the null) = 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ **64%**. After a Bonferroni correction the threshold is 0.05/20 = 0.0025, and p = 0.04 does not come close. As presented, this is the expected noise winner from a batch of 20 tests. It should be replicated with activation preregistered as the single primary metric. |
| 8 | "Gross margin is 78%, computed from the audited financial statements." | `[H]` → `[K]` on inspection | This claim has an external arbiter (the auditor), which is the strongest form in the document. It becomes `[K]` once the statements are produced and the COGS definition is checked. For example: are hosting, support and onboarding staff included? 78% is plausible for SaaS. |

**Tally:** `[K]` 0 · `[H]` 8 · `[KKE]` 1 (3b) · `[Y]` 3 (4b, 5b, 7b) · `[R]` 1 (2b). No `[K]` is possible without the source data. That reflects what I had access to, not how good the company is.

## What survives (credit given precisely)

- **Item 3** controls for the instrument and reports sample sizes. It is the most methodologically careful item in the document.
- **Item 8** points to an external arbiter (audited statements). That is the right kind of evidence.
- **Item 7** discloses the number of tests (20). Without that disclosure the problem would have been invisible. The disclosure is honest. The conclusion drawn from it is not.
- Revenue did grow, by 25%. That is a real result for a company at this stage. It just is not 40%.

## Conjunctions (step 8): claims read together

- **1 × 2:** Headcount rose 46% (28 → 41) while revenue rose 25%. Revenue per employee **fell from about $85.7k to about $73.2k (−15%)**. Taken together, these two "growth" metrics describe falling efficiency. A burn increase is likely and is not reported.
- **5 × 3:** Annual lock-in suppresses the churn signal and can also change which customers answer the NPS survey. Both headline improvements may come from the same pricing change, not from a better product.
- **5 × 2:** Annual prepay brings future cash forward. Unless revenue is recognized ratably (GAAP), FY2026 revenue and cash may include pulled-forward billings. I need to know whether the $3.0M is recognized revenue or billings. This is `[KKE]`.
- **4 × 5:** If integration users skew toward annual plans, the 2× LTV gap and the churn drop are partly the same confound counted twice.

## Hit-rate note

The document makes three inferential leaps: the 40% figure, the causal LTV claim and the churn attribution. The p-value is a fourth. All four lean in the flattering direction. None errs in the unflattering direction. Four independent honest errors would not all point the same way. **Mechanism, not motive:** a fundraising summary is written under strong selection pressure, so metrics and framings are chosen *after* seeing which ones look good. That is enough to explain the skew without assuming anyone intended to mislead.

## The missing card (what this format cannot show)

- The **other 19 onboarding metrics**, including any that got worse.
- **Burn, runway, cash**, and cost growth from 13 hires plus a new office.
- **CAC, payback, net revenue retention**, and revenue concentration (top-customer share).
- Churn **by plan type and cohort**, and the annual **renewal rate**, which does not exist yet.
- Revenue recognition policy (billings vs. recognized revenue).
- NPS **response rates** per wave.

## Next steps (ordered by criticality × impact/effort)

1. **Ask the company to correct the 40% to 25%, or explain it.** This takes one email and is the most important step. If "40%" refers to some other basis (ARR, a different period), they have to say which one. An unexplained arithmetic error in a fundraising document is a diligence finding in its own right.
2. **Request churn split by plan type and cohort**, monthly plans only, before vs. after March. This settles 5b cheaply.
3. **Request the audited statements** and the COGS definition. This promotes item 8, and confirms 2a, to `[K]`.
4. **Request all 20 onboarding test results** and the analysis plan. If activation was not the preregistered primary metric, treat 7b as noise.
5. **Request the LTV method** and integration-adopter vs. non-adopter data matched on company size and signup cohort. This tests 4b properly.
6. **Request NPS response rates** and respondent mix per wave (3b).
7. Request burn/runway, CAC and NRR (the missing card).

If the company declines any of these, record the refusal. An absent control stays `[KKE]` and does not quietly turn into acceptance.
