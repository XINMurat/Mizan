# Evaluation of FY2026 Growth Summary

Tiers used: **Verified** (checkable from the document itself or a cited audited source), **Plausible** (consistent with the evidence given, but not proven), **Unsupported** (the conclusion does not follow from the evidence), **Wrong** (contradicted by the document's own numbers).

| 2 | Revenue +40% | **Wrong**: the numbers give 25% |
| 3 | NPS 31 → 44 | Plausible, and the strongest claim here |

## Claim by claim

**1. Headcount 28 → 41 (+46%).** This is easy to check and probably true. It is an input, not an outcome. Read it next to claim 2: headcount grew 46% while revenue grew 25%, so revenue per employee fell from about $86K to about $73K. That is a decline in efficiency, not a sign of growth.

**2. Revenue $2.4M → $3.0M, "a 40% increase."** This is an arithmetic error. (3.0 − 2.4) / 2.4 = **25%**. You get 40% only by dividing the $0.6M gain by some other base, or by using different numbers. In a fundraising document this mistake matters on its own: either the headline growth rate is inflated, or the underlying figures are not the ones shown. Ask them to reconcile it and to say whether "revenue" means recognized revenue or bookings. This matters because of claim 5: annual plans billed upfront can push bookings above recognized revenue.

**3. NPS 31 → 44.** This is the best-supported claim. The vendor and question stayed the same, and the samples are large (n ≈ 1,100 each). With samples that size, the standard error on NPS is about ±2.5–3 points, so a 13-point rise is very unlikely to be noise. Open questions:
- Was the sampled population the same both years? Surveying only active customers or annual-plan customers would bias the result upward.
- What were the response rates?
- Were survivors oversampled? Churned customers drop out of the frame.

**4. "Integration users have 2× LTV, so pushing everyone onto integrations will double LTV."** This conclusion does not follow from the data. Customers who adopt integrations are probably already more engaged, larger, or better fits for the product. That is selection bias, and it also runs the other way: committed customers take the effort to integrate. The 2× gap is an observed correlation, not the effect of an intervention. Customers pushed onto integrations will not look like the ones who chose them. "Double" is the upper bound and treats the whole gap as causal. The realistic effect is somewhere between 0 and 2×, with no evidence for any particular value. To test the claim, they would need a randomized or staggered integration push and a comparison of retention against a holdout group. It also matters how LTV is estimated for a young company with short customer histories.

**5. Monthly churn 3.1% → 2.2% after annual plans.** This is probably an artifact of how churn is measured. Customers billed once a year cannot churn in any month before renewal. Since March, 60% of new customers have been on annual plans, and none of them has reached a renewal date yet. That alone pushes measured monthly churn down, whether or not retention actually improved. Ask for:
- churn broken out by plan type,
- cohort retention curves for monthly-plan customers only,
- the definition of churn (logo vs. revenue; cancellation date vs. end of term).

The real test comes in March 2027 onward, when the first annual cohorts come up for renewal. Until then, the improvement is not established.

**6. New office in June.** This says nothing about traction and has no evidence value. At most, it adds to the fixed-cost base, which bears on burn.

**7. "Activation improved significantly (p = 0.04)" after testing 20 metrics.** With 20 metrics tested at α = 0.05, you would expect about one false positive by chance alone. The chance of at least one is 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64%. A single p = 0.04 picked out of 20 does not survive a Bonferroni correction (threshold 0.0025) or even a lenient correction. This looks like cherry-picking a result after the fact. Ask:
- Was activation the pre-registered primary metric?
- What are the effect size and confidence interval?
- Did the result replicate?

**8. Gross margin 78%, from audited statements.** This claim points to a source that can be checked, which makes it the most trustworthy in the list, provided the statements exist. 78% is plausible for SaaS. Confirm:
- that the audit covers FY2026,
- which costs sit in COGS (hosting, support, onboarding/implementation staff),
- whether the new headcount was booked below the gross-margin line.

## Overall assessment

- **Solid:** NPS (3), and gross margin (8) if the audit checks out.
- **Wrong or overstated:** the revenue growth rate (2), which is 25%, not 40%.
- **Not supported by the evidence given:** the LTV projection (4), the churn improvement (5), and the activation win (7). Each has a specific flaw: correlation treated as cause, a measurement artifact, and multiple comparisons.
- **Padding:** the office move (6), and headcount (1) presented as growth.

Put together, the real picture is more modest than the summary suggests: revenue grew 25%, headcount grew faster than revenue, and the retention and product claims can't be verified yet.

**Items to request before investing:**
1. A reconciled revenue figure: recognized revenue vs. bookings.
2. Churn broken out by plan type, with monthly-plan cohort curves.
3. The pre-registration or test plan for the activation experiment, with effect sizes.
4. The audited statements, with the COGS breakdown.
5. Any causal evidence (experiment or holdout) for the integration effect on LTV.

# Review of FY2026 Growth Summary

## Claim-by-claim

**1. Headcount 28 → 41.** A factual, easily verified count, so it is adequately supported. It is an input and not a growth outcome, though. Headcount rose about 46% while revenue rose 25% (see claim 2), which suggests revenue per employee fell from about $86K to about $73K. That is a point against the story, not for it.

**2. Revenue $2.4M → $3.0M, "a 40% increase."** The arithmetic is wrong. (3.0 − 2.4) / 2.4 = **25%**, not 40%. The 40% figure would require about $3.36M. The summary also doesn't say how revenue is measured: recognized revenue, bookings, or ARR run-rate. This matters because annual plans (claim 5) collect cash upfront, which can inflate bookings compared with recognized revenue. The underlying numbers may be fine, but the stated growth rate is unsupported. A headline error like this lowers trust in the rest of the document.

**3. NPS 31 → 44.** This is the best-controlled claim: same vendor, same question and large samples. With n ≈ 1,100 per wave, the standard error of NPS is about 2.5–3 points, so a 13-point rise is very unlikely to be sampling noise. Remaining caveats:
- Who was surveyed in each wave, and what were the response rates? If the customer base changed, a rise could come from churned detractors dropping out rather than from existing customers liking the product more.
- The timing of the survey relative to renewals or campaigns is not stated.

Verdict: reasonably supported as a measured change. The reason for the change is not established.

**4. Integration users have 2× LTV, so pushing everyone onto integrations will double LTV.** This is the most serious logical error: it treats correlation as causation.
- **Selection:** customers who adopt integrations are probably larger, more technical and more committed from the start. Their higher LTV may explain why they integrate, rather than result from it.
- **Reverse causality:** long-lived customers have more time to adopt integrations.
- **No experiment or controlled comparison:** customers were not randomized and not matched on size or cohort. It is also not stated how LTV is computed; it is often extrapolated from churn assumptions.
- **Extrapolation:** even if the effect were causal, forcing adoption on reluctant customers would not give the same effect as voluntary adoption.

The descriptive gap may be real. The prediction that pushing integrations will double LTV is unsupported.

**5. Monthly churn 3.1% → 2.2% after annual plans.** This is probably mostly a measurement artifact. Customers on annual plans billed once a year cannot churn monthly until their renewal date. Since March, 60% of new customers have been locked in for 12 months, so none of them can reach renewal within FY2026. The monthly churn rate falls automatically whether or not retention actually improved. Other gaps:
- There is no cohort comparison of monthly-plan customers before and after March.
- The summary doesn't say whether the denominator includes annual customers.
- There is no renewal data, because the first annual renewals haven't happened yet.
- Before/after with no control means seasonality or mix shift could also explain it.

The true test comes at the March 2027 renewals. As stated, this claim does not show improved retention.

**6. New office in June.** This is true but irrelevant as a growth metric. It is filler, and it is also a cost.

**7. 20 onboarding metrics tested; activation improved "significantly" (p = 0.04).** This is a multiple-comparisons problem. If you test 20 metrics at α = 0.05, you expect about one false positive by chance alone. The chance of at least one p < 0.05 when nothing works is about 64%. After a Bonferroni correction the threshold is 0.0025, and p = 0.04 is not significant. Also:
- It isn't clear whether "activation" was chosen in advance or selected after seeing the results.
- The summary doesn't say whether this was a randomized test or a before/after comparison.
- The effect size isn't reported.

Treat this as a hypothesis to retest, not a result.

**8. Gross margin 78%, from audited financial statements.** This has an independent third-party source and is adequately supported. Diligence should still confirm that COGS classification (hosting, support, onboarding staff) follows standard SaaS practice.

## Claim-by-claim

Survivorship could also play a part, since unhappy customers left and the remaining base looks happier. I'd accept the claim with those caveats noted.

**4. Integration users have 2× LTV, "so pushing every customer onto integrations will double LTV."** This is a causal conclusion drawn from a correlation, and it is the most overreaching claim in the summary. Customers who adopt integrations are self-selected: they are likely larger, more committed, more technical, or already getting more value. That confounding can produce the 2× gap on its own. Reverse causation is also plausible, since engaged, long-lived customers have more time and more reason to set up integrations. Customers who get pushed onto integrations are not the same population as those who chose them, so the effect would likely be far smaller than 2×, possibly zero. - Were detractors under-sampled?

The claim is likely real, but it shows sentiment, not revenue.

**4. Integrations double LTV, "so pushing every customer onto integrations will double LTV."** This is invalid causal inference. The observed 2x difference is a correlation and is almost certainly confounded by selection. Larger, more engaged, longer-tenured customers are the ones who adopt integrations. Reverse causation is also possible: customers who stay longer have more time to set integrations up. Forcing integrations on customers who don't need them won't give them the traits of self-selected adopters. The "double" projection has no support. To support it, the company would need an experiment that randomly encourages integration adoption, or at least a comparison that controls for size, tenure and plan.

Customers on annual plans billed once a year can't churn in a given month until their renewal date. The real test is:
- churn on monthly plans only, comparing like with like before and after March
- the first annual renewal rates, which don't exist yet for plans started in March 2026
