**Coverage:** I reviewed the six claims as written. I had no access to the ticket data, the trial data, or the vendor and product materials, so I checked each claim against its stated source and arithmetic only.

**1. Response time fell from 4.2 hours to 40 seconds.** `[K]` Proven
Full-population ticket timestamps, pulled by the data platform team, with a large effect. Seasonality and staffing changes are unlikely to explain a drop this size. One condition: if the timestamp is the bot's automatic acknowledgment, the board should hear "time to first automated or human response." Confirm what the field records before presenting.

**2. Customers prefer the bot.** `[Y]` Misleading
Thirty transcripts read by the product team, with no denominator, no selection method, and no preference measure. The team that built the bot also did the reading. This supports "some transcripts read positively," not a preference claim. Next step: drop the claim, or run a post-chat preference survey with a response rate reported.

**3. The bot resolves 70% of tickets.** `[Y]` Misleading
"Resolved" is defined as the customer closing the chat window. That measures abandonment as much as resolution, and the source is the vendor's dashboard. Next step: define resolution by a checkable outcome, such as no reopen within 7 days or no human contact, and report it from the ticketing system.

**4. The bot reduces repeat contacts.** `[KKE]` Critical control missing (pending)
This is the strongest design in the set: randomized, 5,000 customers, analysed by an independent team. The arithmetic doesn't reconcile, though. The point difference is 9.1 − 11.4 = −2.3 points, which sits inside the stated interval. But for two proportions near 10% with about 2,500 customers per arm, the standard error of the difference is roughly 0.86 points, giving a 95% half-width near 1.7 points. The stated half-width is about 0.7 points. Reaching 0.7 would need roughly 14,000 customers per arm, more than the 5,000 reported. Either the unit of analysis is not customers (for example, contacts or tickets), the interval is wrong, or the sample is misreported. Two further checks are needed: whether the 7-day repeat-contact outcome was preregistered, and whether low repeat contact partly reflects customers giving up after a bot dead end. The direction likely survives a corrected interval, but the stated number should not go to the board until the analytics team reconciles it. Next step: ask them for the analysis file and the unit of analysis.

**5. The bot will cut support headcount costs by a third next year.** `[S]` Speculative
A projection from the vendor's sales deck. It has no model, no baseline, and no test, and the source has a commercial interest. It can be stated as a hypothesis with a stated test, such as a cost-per-resolved-ticket target after two quarters. It cannot be presented as a forecast.

**6. Agents are happier since launch.** `[Y]` Misleading (with `[KKE]`)
No survey was run. Lower attrition is not evidence of happiness, and the hiring freeze started the same month and is an obvious alternative explanation. Next step: run a short agent pulse survey with a baseline, or drop the claim.
