## Claims with Significant Problems

**Claim 2 (Revenue growth rate):** Mathematical error. The company claims 40% growth, but the numbers show a 25% increase: ($3.0M − $2.4M) / $2.4M = 0.25 = 25%. This is a material misrepresentation, not a minor rounding difference.

**Claim 4 (Integration → LTV causation):** Conclusion far exceeds the evidence. The claim that integrations "will double LTV" rests on a correlation observed between integration use and LTV. But this observation does not establish causation. Alternative explanations:
- High-value customers may self-select into using integrations (reverse causation)
- A third variable (company size, sophistication) might drive both integration adoption and LTV
- The relationship may not be causal at all

Pushing every customer onto integrations based on this logic is unjustified. The evidence supports "customers who use integrations are higher-value"; it does not support "making all customers use integrations will make all customers higher-value."

**Claim 5 (Annual plans → churn reduction):** Temporal association, but causation is not proven. Churn fell from 3.1% to 2.2% in the same period annual plans were introduced, but no control group or baseline is provided. Other factors could explain the improvement:
- Seasonal effects (March forward might be historically lower churn)
- Simultaneous product improvements
- Market conditions
- Survivor bias (the 60% adopting annual plans may be sticky customers anyway)

The facts stated are true, but the implied causal link is unsupported.

**Claim 7 (Activation rate improvement):** Multiple serious methodological concerns:
- **Multiple comparisons problem:** 20 metrics were tested. At a 0.05 significance level, one would expect approximately one false positive by chance alone. With p = 0.04, this result is not clearly distinguishable from noise given the multiple testing.
- **Borderline p-value:** p = 0.04 is just barely under the conventional 0.05 threshold. This is weak evidence.
- **No effect size reported:** "Significantly improved" conflates statistical significance (p < 0.05) with practical significance. We don't know if the improvement is meaningful in business terms.
- **Vague causal claim:** The statement says "testing changes against 20 metrics" improved "activation rate" but does not clarify which specific changes were tested or which one improved activation. The framing obscures rather than illuminates.

This claim is inadequately supported and suggests selective reporting or p-hacking (testing many metrics and highlighting the one that showed a favorable p-value).

## Summary

- **Strong claims:** Headcount, office move, audited margin, NPS improvement
- **Weak claims:** Revenue math error (25% not 40%), activation rate (multiple testing issues), annual plans causation claim (no control)
- **Severely problematic:** Integration → LTV causation (correlation misrepresented as predictive causation)

### Claim 1: Headcount grew from 28 to 41

### Claim 2: Revenue grew from $2.4M to $3.0M year over year, a 40% increase
- **Tier:**  Misleading
- **Source:** Company financials (assumed audited; claim includes a percentage)
- **Justification:** The arithmetic is incorrect. $3.0M ÷ $2.4M = 1.25, which is a 25% increase, not 40%. The growth is real but the percentage overstates it. This is not a rounding error (40% would be $3.36M). The mismatch suggests either a calculation error or selective description.
- **Mechanism:** The company correctly reports the base numbers but miscomputes or misspeaks the percentage. This is a producer-side error (claim-writer error), not an auditor error.
- **Impact:** Investors relying on the "40%" figure are misled by 600 basis points. Over a funding round discussion, this compounds other claims' credibility.
- **Next step:** Request audited financial statements to verify the $2.4M and $3.0M figures, then correct the percentage to 25%. Ask whether any other figures in the raise document use similar estimation.

---

### Claim 3: Net Promoter Score rose from 31 to 44 (same survey vendor, same question; n = 1,120 and n = 1,085)
- **Tier:**  Plausible hypothesis
- **Source:** Survey data (vendor and sample size provided, but raw data not available for audit)
- **Justification:** The improvement is numerically substantial (13 points). Sample sizes are adequate (>1000 each) for detecting an effect. Same vendor and question reduce methodological confounds. However, the improvement could reflect seasonal timing (when were the surveys run?), selection bias (who responded?), or question-wording drift (verbatim question text not provided). NPS is also volatile and survey fatigue is common in startup samples.
- **Confounds not ruled out:** Survey timing within the year, response-rate change between cohorts, demographic shift in respondent pool.
- **Missing evidence:** Raw survey data, response rates, date ranges for each survey, full question text.
- **Next step:** Request the survey vendor's full methodology report and raw response data for both periods. Check survey dates to rule out seasonal confounds. Compute response rate for each cohort to detect selection bias.

---

### Claim 4: Customers who use integrations have twice the lifetime value of those who don't; pushing all customers onto integrations will double LTV
- **Tier:**  Critical control missing
- **Two claims atomized:**
  1. *Observation:* Customers using integrations have ~2x LTV (this may be true)
  2. *Causal leap:* Pushing all customers onto integrations will double LTV (this does not follow)
- **Source:** Company analytics (internal, not shown)
- **Mechanism of the error:** Selection bias. High-value customers may adopt integrations because they get more value from the product (reverse causality), or they may be larger organizations that always had higher LTV independent of integration use. Without a control group (randomized test, propensity-matched comparison, or at minimum a breakdown of when integrations were adopted relative to LTV trajectory), the direction of causality is unknown.
- **Missing controls:**
  - Randomized test: offer integrations to random subset, measure LTV change
  - Cohort analysis: did LTV increase *after* integration adoption, or were high-LTV customers adopting already?
  - Matched comparison: low-LTV customers who used integrations vs. high-LTV who did not
- **Why this matters:** If the startup invests in pushing all customers onto integrations (assuming causality), and the effect is not causal, the investment is wasted. The confound is plausible and high-impact.
- **Next step:** Before making integration-adoption a priority, run one of the three controls above. Minimum: segment customers by LTV trajectory *before* vs. *after* they adopted integrations, stratified by cohort-entry date, to check directionality. If a control is not feasible, demote the LTV claim to  (speculative) and do not make product decisions based on it.

### Claim 5: Monthly churn fell from 3.1% to 2.2% after annual plans were introduced in March. 60% of new customers since March chose annual plans.
- **Tier:**  Plausible hypothesis (for churn causation);  Proven (for plan uptake)
- **Two claims atomized:**
  1. *Uptake claim:* 60% of new customers since March chose annual plans — , verifiable from billing records
  2. *Causation claim:* Churn fell *because* of annual plans — , not yet proven
- **Source:** Billing system + churn analytics (company-internal)
- **Confounds not ruled out:**
  - Seasonal trend: does churn naturally fall in Q2/Q3 regardless of product changes?
  - Product improvements: unrelated bug fixes or feature launches in March could drive retention
  - Market conditions: if market expanded or competitor churn increased, baseline improved
  - Survivor bias: customers who chose annual plans may be a higher-commitment segment to begin with (selection effect, not treatment effect)
- **Observation-to-causation gap:** Churn fell; annual plans were introduced; these are correlated but not proven causal. The 60% uptake *among new customers* does not prove annual plans caused old-customer churn to fall.
- **Missing evidence:**
  - Churn rate for customers who did NOT adopt annual plans
  - Churn trend for 12 months before March (to detect seasonality)
  - Did churn fall *for customers who adopted annual plans*, or did it fall *for all customers*?
  - Cohort analysis: churn rate of pre-March vs. post-March cohorts, holding plan type fixed
- **Next step:** Run cohort analysis separating new-customer churn (March onward, by plan type) from existing-customer churn (March-to-present, by plan type). If churn fell only for annual-plan adopters and stayed flat for monthly-plan customers, causality is stronger. If it fell uniformly, the annual-plan explanation is less credible. Supply the pre-March churn trend to rule out seasonality.

### Claim 7: Tested changes against 20 onboarding metrics; activation rate improved significantly (p = 0.04)
- **Tier:**  Misleading (likely;  pending full details)
- **Source:** Internal A/B testing (data not shown)
- **Critical issue — multiple comparisons:** The company tested 20 independent onboarding metrics. If each test is conducted at p < 0.05, the probability of at least one false positive by random chance is approximately 1 − (1 − 0.05)^20 ≈ 64%. A single metric reaching p = 0.04 is expected noise in this scenario, not evidence of a real effect. This is the classic multiple-comparisons problem. Reporting one p = 0.04 finding from a 20-metric sweep *without acknowledging the family-wise error rate* is misleading.
- **Missing evidence:**
  - Were these 20 metrics preregistered before the test?
  - What is the family-wise error rate (Bonferroni correction: p < 0.0025 for 20 tests)?
  - What is the effect size (e.g., activation rate increase from X% to Y%)?
  - Was "activation rate" one of the 20 metrics, or was it a post-hoc focus?
  - How long did the test run? Was there a stopping rule?
- **Mechanism:** Selective reporting (p-hacking). If the company ran 20 tests and reported the one that came closest to significant, the appearance of significance is false.
- **Next step:** Request the full test plan (preregistered metrics and thresholds), the results for all 20 metrics, and the multiple-comparison correction. If the 20 metrics were not preregistered, the result is a post-hoc observation and should be reported as  pending replication. If activation rate was one of 20 and reached p = 0.04 uncorrected, the finding is  (misleading; likely false positive). If a proper correction was applied, the finding survives and moves to .
