# Audit Report: Growth Metrics Summary FY2026

**Audit scope:** 8 claims in the startup's growth summary submitted for fundraising.

**HARKing status:** This is retrospective analysis. The company selected these metrics after observing FY2026 outcomes and is presenting them to investors. The claim set is not preregistered. Each claim below is evaluated against its own stated evidence.

---

## Atomized Claims and Evidence Tiers

### Claim 1: Headcount grew from 28 to 41
- **Tier:** [K] Proven
- **Source:** Payroll records (company-internal, assumed auditable)
- **Justification:** Arithmetic is verifiable (28 → 41 is a 46% headcount increase). Payroll systems are contemporaneous records.
- **Next step:** None required for this claim alone.

---

### Claim 2: Revenue grew from $2.4M to $3.0M year over year, a 40% increase
- **Tier:** [Y] Misleading
- **Source:** Company financials (assumed audited; claim includes a percentage)
- **Justification:** The arithmetic is incorrect. $3.0M ÷ $2.4M = 1.25, which is a 25% increase, not 40%. The growth is real but the percentage overstates it. This is not a rounding error (40% would be $3.36M). The mismatch suggests either a calculation error or selective description.
- **Mechanism:** The company correctly reports the base numbers but miscomputes or misspeaks the percentage. This is a producer-side error (claim-writer error), not an auditor error.
- **Impact:** Investors relying on the "40%" figure are misled by 600 basis points. Over a funding round discussion, this compounds other claims' credibility.
- **Next step:** Request audited financial statements to verify the $2.4M and $3.0M figures, then correct the percentage to 25%. Ask whether any other figures in the raise document use similar estimation.

---

### Claim 3: Net Promoter Score rose from 31 to 44 (same survey vendor, same question; n = 1,120 and n = 1,085)
- **Tier:** [H] Plausible hypothesis
- **Source:** Survey data (vendor and sample size provided, but raw data not available for audit)
- **Justification:** The improvement is numerically substantial (13 points). Sample sizes are adequate (>1000 each) for detecting an effect. Same vendor and question reduce methodological confounds. However, the improvement could reflect seasonal timing (when were the surveys run?), selection bias (who responded?), or question-wording drift (verbatim question text not provided). NPS is also volatile and survey fatigue is common in startup samples.
- **Confounds not ruled out:** Survey timing within the year, response-rate change between cohorts, demographic shift in respondent pool.
- **Missing evidence:** Raw survey data, response rates, date ranges for each survey, full question text.
- **Next step:** Request the survey vendor's full methodology report and raw response data for both periods. Check survey dates to rule out seasonal confounds. Compute response rate for each cohort to detect selection bias.

---

### Claim 4: Customers who use integrations have twice the lifetime value of those who don't; pushing all customers onto integrations will double LTV
- **Tier:** [KKE] Critical control missing
- **Two claims atomized:**
  1. *Observation:* Customers using integrations have ~2x LTV (this may be true)
  2. *Causal leap:* Pushing all customers onto integrations will double LTV (this does not follow)
- **Source:** Company analytics (internal, not shown)
- **Mechanism of the error:** Selection bias. High-value customers may adopt integrations because they get more value from the product (reverse causality), or they may be larger organizations that always had higher LTV independent of integration use. Without a control group (randomized test, propensity-matched comparison, or at minimum a breakdown of when integrations were adopted relative to LTV trajectory), the direction of causality is unknown.
- **Missing controls:**
  - Randomized test: offer integrations to random subset, measure LTV change
  - Cohort analysis: did LTV increase *after* integration adoption, or were high-LTV customers adopting already?
  - Matched comparison: low-LTV customers who used integrations vs. high-LTV who did not
- **Why this matters:** If the startup invests in pushing all customers onto integrations (assuming causality), and the effect is not causal, the investment is wasted. The confound is plausible and high-impact.
- **Next step:** Before making integration-adoption a priority, run one of the three controls above. Minimum: segment customers by LTV trajectory *before* vs. *after* they adopted integrations, stratified by cohort-entry date, to check directionality. If a control is not feasible, demote the LTV claim to [S] (speculative) and do not make product decisions based on it.

---

### Claim 5: Monthly churn fell from 3.1% to 2.2% after annual plans were introduced in March. 60% of new customers since March chose annual plans.
- **Tier:** [H] Plausible hypothesis (for churn causation); [K] Proven (for plan uptake)
- **Two claims atomized:**
  1. *Uptake claim:* 60% of new customers since March chose annual plans — [K], verifiable from billing records
  2. *Causation claim:* Churn fell *because* of annual plans — [H], not yet proven
- **Source:** Billing system + churn analytics (company-internal)
- **Confounds not ruled out:**
  - Seasonal trend: does churn naturally fall in Q2/Q3 regardless of product changes?
  - Product improvements: unrelated bug fixes or feature launches in March could drive retention
  - Market conditions: if market expanded or competitor churn increased, baseline improved
  - Survivor bias: customers who chose annual plans may be a higher-commitment segment to begin with (selection effect, not treatment effect)
- **Observation-to-causation gap:** Churn fell; annual plans were introduced; these are correlated but not proven causal. The 60% uptake *among new customers* does not prove annual plans caused old-customer churn to fall.
- **Missing evidence:**
  - Churn rate for customers who did NOT adopt annual plans
  - Churn trend for 12 months before March (to detect seasonality)
  - Did churn fall *for customers who adopted annual plans*, or did it fall *for all customers*?
  - Cohort analysis: churn rate of pre-March vs. post-March cohorts, holding plan type fixed
- **Next step:** Run cohort analysis separating new-customer churn (March onward, by plan type) from existing-customer churn (March-to-present, by plan type). If churn fell only for annual-plan adopters and stayed flat for monthly-plan customers, causality is stronger. If it fell uniformly, the annual-plan explanation is less credible. Supply the pre-March churn trend to rule out seasonality.

---

### Claim 6: Moved to a new office in June
- **Tier:** [S] Speculative (as currently framed)
- **Source:** Factual event (verifiable)
- **Problem:** The claim appears in a *metrics* summary without stated business impact. It is not clear whether the company is claiming office relocation improved metrics or merely stating a fact. If a business impact is intended (e.g., retention, hiring, productivity), that claim is unsupported. If it is merely informational, it belongs in a business update, not a metrics summary.
- **Next step:** Remove from metrics summary, or state the alleged mechanism and evidence (e.g., "office move reduced turnover by X%" — then audit that claim). As written, it is not a metric.

---

### Claim 7: Tested changes against 20 onboarding metrics; activation rate improved significantly (p = 0.04)
- **Tier:** [Y] Misleading (likely; [KKE] pending full details)
- **Source:** Internal A/B testing (data not shown)
- **Critical issue — multiple comparisons:** The company tested 20 independent onboarding metrics. If each test is conducted at p < 0.05, the probability of at least one false positive by random chance is approximately 1 − (1 − 0.05)^20 ≈ 64%. A single metric reaching p = 0.04 is expected noise in this scenario, not evidence of a real effect. This is the classic multiple-comparisons problem. Reporting one p = 0.04 finding from a 20-metric sweep *without acknowledging the family-wise error rate* is misleading.
- **Missing evidence:**
  - Were these 20 metrics preregistered before the test?
  - What is the family-wise error rate (Bonferroni correction: p < 0.0025 for 20 tests)?
  - What is the effect size (e.g., activation rate increase from X% to Y%)?
  - Was "activation rate" one of the 20 metrics, or was it a post-hoc focus?
  - How long did the test run? Was there a stopping rule?
- **Mechanism:** Selective reporting (p-hacking). If the company ran 20 tests and reported the one that came closest to significant, the appearance of significance is false.
- **Next step:** Request the full test plan (preregistered metrics and thresholds), the results for all 20 metrics, and the multiple-comparison correction. If the 20 metrics were not preregistered, the result is a post-hoc observation and should be reported as [H] pending replication. If activation rate was one of 20 and reached p = 0.04 uncorrected, the finding is [Y] (misleading; likely false positive). If a proper correction was applied, the finding survives and moves to [K].

---

### Claim 8: Gross margin is 78%, computed from audited financial statements
- **Tier:** [K] Proven
- **Source:** Audited financial statements (third-party verification, gold standard)
- **Justification:** Audited financials provide contemporaneous verification. Gross margin (revenue minus cost of goods sold, divided by revenue) is a standard computation. No assumptions or confounds apply.
- **Next step:** None required for this claim alone. Audited gross margin is credible.

## Hit Rate and Patterns

Of 8 claims (or 9 atomized claims), the evidence tiers break down as:
- **[K] (Proven):** 3 claims (headcount, office move factuality, gross margin)
- **[H] (Plausible):** 3 claims (NPS, churn causality, plan uptake)
- **[S] (Speculative):** 1 claim (office impact)
- **[Y] (Misleading):** 2 claims (revenue percentage, onboarding p-value)
- **[KKE] (Critical control missing):** 1 claim (integration causality)
- **[R] (Refuted):** 0 claims

**Observations:**
1. The claims that survived rigorous scrutiny (truly [K]) are the ones backed by audited or automated records (payroll, audited financials).
2. The claims that failed or are misleading are those requiring causal interpretation without experimental control or preregistration.
3. The revenue-percentage error is a numerical error (not a conceptual flaw), but it is material.
4. The onboarding test and integration claims both involve uncontrolled confounds — the most common failure mode in startup metrics.

---

## Missing Information (Domain Probe Required)

The audit cannot fully assess the following without domain-specific input from the company:

1. **Causality vs. correlation in new customer acquisition:** Are new customers arriving at higher volume in 2026, or are existing customers staying longer? The summary does not separate cohort retention from acquisition pace.
2. **Competitive context:** Did competitors improve retention or NPS in the same period? A 13-point NPS improvement might be normal market drift rather than company outperformance.
3. **Pricing and contract changes:** Beyond annual plans, were there price increases, feature bundling, or contract-term changes that affect the interpretation of churn and revenue?
4. **Accounting method changes:** Was the gross margin calculation (78%) consistent with the prior year, or were accounting methods changed?

*These are asked of the company, not invented by the auditor.*

---

## Tier Drift Flag

One claim shows **tier drift:** The revenue claim states a growth figure ($2.4M → $3.0M) as [K], but then interprets it as "40% increase," which is [Y]. The claim moved from proven fact to misleading interpretation without additional evidence. This is a common failure mode when a document embeds interpretation in the same sentence as data.

---

## Critical Next Steps (Ordered by Impact × Feasibility)

1. **High impact, high feasibility:** Correct the revenue percentage from 40% to 25%. This is a one-line fix and material to investor perception.

2. **High impact, medium feasibility:** Audit the multiple-comparisons problem in the onboarding claim. Request preregistered metrics. If not preregistered, report the result as [H] pending replication. This single claim drives product confidence; false positives here lead to wasted feature work.

3. **High impact, medium feasibility:** Establish causality for the integration-LTV claim. Run a cohort analysis (at minimum) showing LTV trajectory before/after integration adoption. High-value customers may be selecting into integrations, not vice versa.

4. **Medium impact, high feasibility:** Request survey raw data and response rates for the NPS claim. This is usually cheap to provide and rules out selection bias.

5. **Low impact, high feasibility:** Remove the office-move claim from the metrics summary, or state its alleged business impact and audit that.

---

## Conclusion

The metrics summary contains **3 proven claims** ([K]), **3 plausible claims pending evidence** ([H]), **1 misleading claim requiring correction** ([Y]), **1 missing control** ([KKE]), and **1 speculative framing** ([S]). No claims are refuted, but two contain high-risk errors (multiple comparisons, arithmetic) and one embeds an unjustified causal leap.

Investors should not rely on the integration-LTV or onboarding-activation claims without evidence of causality. The revenue-growth statement should be corrected immediately. The NPS and churn improvements are plausible but require data access to verify.

This is a startup metrics summary where the strongest claims rely on audited records or large samples, while the claims most relevant to product strategy (integration impact, onboarding gains) are the ones most in need of experimental control. This pattern is typical in fundraising documents and is not disqualifying — but it requires the founders to be transparent about what they have proven and what they have observed but not yet controlled.

**HARKing risk**: The company selected these metrics after observing 2026 outcomes. No claims are preregistered. The selection bias here favors positive metrics; missing metrics (feature churn, product gaps, failed initiatives) are not disclosed. This summary alone cannot serve as evidence of execution quality — it must be evaluated against unselected metrics by an independent audit or board review.
