## Claims Analyzed

### 1. Headcount: 28 → 41
**Claim (atomized):**
- (1a) Headcount was 28 at start of FY2026
- (1b) Headcount is 41 at end of FY2026
- (1c, implicit) Headcount growth is evidence of value creation

**Tier:** [K] on facts (1a, 1b); [H] on implication (1c)

**Evidence:**
- (1a, 1b) Assumed from internal HR records; not externally verified but uncontroversial for an internal metric.
- (1c) Growth alone does not indicate efficiency. A 46% headcount increase paired with 25% revenue increase (see claim 2) suggests declining productivity per headcount. The document omits headcount cost data entirely. No control against sector norms.

**Justification:** The numbers are likely accurate but the framing conflates activity with value. A meaningful claim would anchor to revenue-per-head or customer-per-head.

### 2. Revenue: $2.4M → $3.0M, "40% increase"
**Claim (atomized):**
- (2a) Revenue was $2.4M in prior year
- (2b) Revenue is $3.0M in FY2026
- (2c) This represents a 40% increase

**Tier:** [K] on facts (2a, 2b); [R] on calculation (2c)

**Evidence:**
- (2a, 2b) Likely from audited financials or tax returns; not provided but standard for fundraising. Assume [K].
- (2c) **Calculation check:** ($3.0M − $2.4M) / $2.4M = 0.25 = **25% increase**, not 40%.
  - The claim "40% increase" is **mathematically false**.
  - A 40% increase would require revenue of $3.36M ($2.4M × 1.40).
  - This is either a spreadsheet error or deliberate misstatement.

**Justification:** The stated growth rate is refuted by arithmetic. The actual 25% growth is still positive but materially weaker for a fundraising summary. The error suggests either carelessness in preparation or intentional inflation.

### 3. NPS: 31 → 44 (same vendor, n=1,120 and n=1,085)
**Claim (atomized):**
- (3a) NPS was 31 (n=1,120)
- (3b) NPS is 44 (n=1,085)
- (3c, implicit) NPS improvement indicates customer satisfaction improved

**Tier:** [K] on measurement (3a, 3b); [H] on implication (3c)

**Evidence:**
- (3a, 3b) Same survey vendor and question across periods; sample sizes adequate for population estimates. Improvement is statistically significant (13-point shift, ~2% standard error per sample). Methodological consistency is noted.
- (3c) NPS improvement is a leading indicator, not proof of satisfaction. No context on: industry benchmarks (is 44 strong?), whether the vendor's respondent pool changed, survey participation bias, or whether NPS predicts retention. One survey in a metrics summary is selection bias (survivorship).

**Justification:** The measurement is sound; the inference is premature. [K] for the data; [H] for the business claim it supports.

### 4. Integration usage and LTV: "Customers with integrations have 2× LTV; pushing all onto integrations doubles LTV"
**Claim (atomized):**
- (4a) Customers using integrations have 2× lifetime value of non-users
- (4b) Moving all customers to integrations will double overall LTV

**Tier:** [KKE] on observation (4a); [R] on prediction (4b)

**Evidence:**
- (4a) This is a correlational claim. No control for selection bias.
  - **Confound:** Customers who adopt integrations self-select—they are already more engaged, more sophisticated, or less price-sensitive. They would have higher LTV *regardless of integrations*.
  - **Converse:** Customers who refuse integrations may churn early by design (product mismatch), skewing LTV downward.
  - No experimental design, randomization, or holdout group is described.
- (4b) Predicts causal effect without testing it. Assumes every customer can benefit equally from integrations; ignores that non-adopters may have good reasons.

**Justification:** The difference exists ([K]); the mechanism is unknown ([KKE]). The predicted doubling of overall LTV is unfounded. A minimal control: compare LTV trends *before and after* integration adoption for individual customers (did individual LTV increase after adoption, or were adopters always different?).

**Criticality:** This claim justifies a major product strategy. The missing control makes it high-risk.

### 5. Churn: 3.1% → 2.2% "after annual plans in March"; 60% adoption
**Claim (atomized):**
- (5a) Monthly churn was 3.1% before March
- (5b) Monthly churn is 2.2% after March
- (5c, causal) Introducing annual plans *caused* the churn reduction
- (5d) 60% of new customers since March chose annual plans

**Tier:** [K] on data (5a, 5b, 5d); [Y] on causation (5c)

**Evidence:**
- (5a, 5b) Time-stamped metrics; assume accurate.
- (5d) 60% adoption is [K] if verified.
- (5c) **Post-hoc reasoning without a control.** The churn fell 0.9 percentage points after annual plans launched. Alternative explanations:
  - Seasonal effect (March is often a churn inflection point).
  - Concurrent product improvements or customer success changes.
  - Customers who choose annual plans are self-selected for lower churn (the integrations confound again).
  - Survivorship bias: annual-plan customers are newer; all cohorts churn lower early on.
  - None of these are ruled out.

**Justification:** The churn numbers are [K]; the claimed cause is [Y]—technically true (both events happened) but framed to imply causation without evidence. A control would compare churn in a cohort *before annual plans* introduced themselves (did customers contacted about annual plans churn less even before signing up?).

### 7. Onboarding tests: 20 metrics tested; "activation improved significantly (p=0.04)"
**Claim (atomized):**
- (7a) Changes were tested against 20 onboarding metrics
- (7b) Activation rate improved
- (7c) The improvement is significant (p=0.04)

**Tier:** [Y] or [R] on statistical claim (7c), depending on preregistration

**Evidence:**
- (7a, 7b) Assume true; no source provided.
- (7c) **Multiple comparisons problem:** Testing 20 hypotheses at α=0.05 inflates false-positive rate to ~64%, not 5%. With Bonferroni correction (α=0.0025), p=0.04 is *not significant*.
  - **Was "activation rate" the preregistered target hypothesis, or discovered post-hoc** among 20 tests? If discovered post-hoc, this is HARKing.
  - If not preregistered: the correct statement is "1 of 20 tests showed p < 0.05, consistent with false-positive noise."
  - If preregistered: the claim survives but must disclose the other 19 results. None are reported.

**Justification:** The p-value is either [R] (not significant when corrected) or [Y] (presented as significant while omitting the multiple-test context). Report: "tested 20 metrics, activation showed p=0.04, no correction for multiple comparisons reported; 19 other results omitted."

## Missing Evidence (Domain Probe)

A fundraising metrics summary should answer:
1. **Profitability path:** Growing revenue 25% while headcount grows 46% suggests burn is rising. Gross margin is 78%, but what is operating margin? _Omitted._
2. **Churn causation:** What actually drove the churn improvement? Cohort analysis comparing new (with annual plans) to prior cohorts? _Omitted._
3. **Integration causation:** How many existing customers migrated to integrations? Did they churn less post-migration individually? _Omitted._
4. **Cohort retention:** NPS improved, but did retention per cohort improve, or are early cohorts naturally retaining better? _Omitted._
5. **Multiple-comparisons disclosure:** Of the 20 metrics tested, how many reached p<0.05? _Omitted._

## Next Steps (by criticality × impact/effort)

1. **Retract or correct the 40% claim** (Criticality: HIGH / Effort: MINIMAL).  
   The calculation error damages credibility across all claims. State: "25% YoY growth."

2. **Disclose the 20-metric test results in full** (Criticality: HIGH / Effort: LOW).  
   Publish the p-values for all 20 outcomes. If activation was not preregistered, label it as exploratory. If 19 of 20 were >0.05, say so.

3. **Add a profitability or burn-rate slide** (Criticality: HIGH / Effort: MEDIUM).  
   LTV and churn are vanity metrics without unit economics. Show CAC, CAC payback, or operating margin.

4. **Re-examine the integration claim with a control** (Criticality: MEDIUM / Effort: MEDIUM).  
   Partition by: (a) existing customers pre/post integration adoption, (b) new customers with/without adoption, (c) competitor integration adoption rates. This claim may be salvageable.

5. **Explain the office move or omit it** (Criticality: LOW / Effort: MINIMAL).  
   If it reduces rent or supports hiring, say so. Otherwise, it adds noise to a metrics summary.

## Overall Assessment

**The summary presents real growth, but via inflated and uncorrected claims.** A 25% revenue increase with improving NPS is solid early-stage progress. However:

- The **40% claim is false** by arithmetic.
- The **causal claims (churn, integration LTV)** are presented without controls and rest on confounds.
- The **statistical claim (p=0.04)** violates standard multiple-comparisons practice.
- The **HARKing liability** is high: metrics chosen post-hoc, no preregistration, alternative explanations not ruled out.

For investors accustomed to rigor, this summary raises flags. For investors unfamiliar with statistics, the flags are invisible—which is the whole problem. **Recommendation to user:** Prepare a second, more careful deck emphasizing the real wins (unit economics, retention cohort analysis, confirmed directional metrics) and retiring the claims that don't survive scrutiny. A transparent deck with [H] and [KKE] claims honestly labeled will be more persuasive than an optimized one where the flaws accumulate silently.
