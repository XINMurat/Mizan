# Fraud Model Board Summary — Evidence Audit

**Auditor note:** This is a retrospective audit of six claims presented for board sign-off tomorrow. The team lead seeks confirmation that all are proven. The procedure below atomizes each claim, sources its evidence, assigns a tier per the Mizan framework, and identifies missing controls. Result: three claims survive as proven; three require qualification or withhold support.

---

## Claim 1: Recall is 0.94 on held-out 2025 data

**Claim atomized:** The trained model achieves 0.94 recall on a held-out test split that the modelling team did not see; evaluation was performed by an independent risk-audit team.

**Evidence sourced:** A specific metric (0.94) is stated. The source is identified (independent risk-audit team). The data (held-out 2025 split) is separated from training. Independence of evaluator is stated.

**Tier:** `[K]` Kanıtlanmış / **Proven**

**Justification:** Direct evidence, cited source, numerical threshold met, independent evaluation on unseen data. This satisfies the standard for a measured claim with clear separation of train/test.

---

## Claim 2: Fraud losses fell 22% since launch

**Claim atomized:** (a) Fraud losses declined by 22%. (b) Comparison is Q3 (previous) vs Q4 (this quarter). (c) The parenthetical notes no comparison group and that Q4 is seasonally higher than Q3.

**Evidence sourced:** The 22% figure is stated but the source is not named (is this internal reporting, audit data, payment platform metrics?). Critically, the note itself identifies a major confound: Q4 is seasonally higher than Q3. Without a control — whether Q3→Q4 in a prior year without the model, or a matched comparison group — the reduction is indistinguishable from seasonal patterns.

**Search for counter-examples:** What would explain 22% without the model? Seasonal uplift in Q4 fraud activity is industry standard (holiday shopping surge). The claim requires ruling this out; the parenthetical proves it is known but not controlled.

**Tier:** `[KKE]` Kritik Kontrol Eksik / **Critical control missing**

**Justification:** A measurable result exists (22% reduction), but the confound that could flip its meaning — seasonal baseline — is known and unstudied. The missing control is: either (a) Q3→Q4 comparison in a prior year without the model, (b) a matched cohort that did not receive the model in Q4, or (c) a within-quarter time series controlling for day-of-week and holiday effects. None is present. A 22% reduction, 100% of which could be seasonal, is not a proven effect of the model.

**Threshold for [K]:** The model's incremental effect = (Q4 with model) − (Q4 without model, estimated from prior-year Q4→Q3 pattern or control cohort). Without that denominator, the claim is [KKE].

## Claim 3: The model is fair across customer groups

**Claim atomized:** Fairness is confirmed across customer groups. Operationalization: the modelling team reviewed 50 decisions and saw no problems.

**Evidence sourced:** Subjective review (no metrics for "fairness"). Sample size: 50 decisions. Evaluator: modelling team (not independent). No threshold for "problems" is stated. No counter-example search is documented.

**Tier:** `[H]` Makul Hipotez / **Plausible hypothesis**

**Justification:** Fairness is a known concern with fraud models (disparate impact, proxy effects for protected characteristics); this is theoretical grounding. Empirical support is missing: (1) "50 decisions reviewed, no problems seen" is selection bias if the review was not systematic (which decisions, by what criteria?); (2) no independence from the team that built the model; (3) no quantified fairness metric (e.g., recall by customer segment, parity across groups); (4) no sample-size justification (why is 50 sufficient?). A hand-inspection by builders is not evidence of fairness.

**Threshold for [K]:** An independent audit comparing model outcomes across customer segments, with a pre-registered fairness metric (e.g., recall parity within 5%, false-positive rate parity within 5%), evaluated on a representative sample with enough power to detect the target effect size. Bonus: statistical tests for disparate impact.

## Claim 4: Analysts trust the model

**Claim atomized:** Analysts trust the model. Operationalization: two senior analysts said so in a meeting.

**Evidence sourced:** Two testimonials from senior analysts, no systematic survey.

**Tier:** `[H]` Makul Hipotez / **Plausible hypothesis**

**Justification:** Trust is a real downstream outcome that matters. However, two meeting statements constitute selection bias (were they the analysts most likely to approve? was the meeting structured to invite candor or consensus?). No measurement of trust (e.g., "would you override the model's decision if you disagreed?" or usage logs showing acceptance rate). No counter-example: did any analyst express skepticism? A reputation sample of n=2 from a self-selected context is not evidence.

**Hit rate comment:** If the team conducted a structured survey of analysts post-deployment (e.g., "On a 1–5 scale, how much do you trust this model to catch fraud?" n=10+, response distribution reported), that would support [H] or approach [K] depending on consistency.

## Claim 5: Its false-positive rate is below the old rules engine's

**Claim atomized:** (a) The new model's false-positive rate is lower than the old rules engine's. (b) Both systems ran in parallel (shadow mode) on the same 3 months of live traffic. (c) New model: 1.8% FP rate. Old engine: 4.6% FP rate. (d) Source: payments platform logs.

**Evidence sourced:** Specific metrics (1.8% vs 4.6%), same test conditions (shadow mode, same 3 months, same data), credible source (payments platform logs, independent of modelling team).

**Tier:** `[K]` Kanıtlanmış / **Proven**

**Justification:** The specific claim — that the FP rate is lower — is directly supported by the metrics and controls. Shadow mode comparison is a valid method (both systems run, only old one decides, allowing off-line comparison). Same dataset and timeframe eliminates confounds from seasonal or volume shifts. The log source is independent of the team building the model. This meets the threshold for a proven comparative claim on a single metric.

**Note:** This verdict is narrowly scoped to the false-positive rate comparison. It does not extend to false negatives, overall decision quality, or other outcomes not claimed here.

## Claim 6: It will scale to 10x current traffic

**Claim atomized:** The system will scale to 10x current traffic. Operationalization: load-tested once, at 2x, on a laptop.

**Evidence sourced:** One load test at 2x current traffic, run on a laptop (not production infrastructure).

**Tier:** `[H]` Makul Hipotez / **Plausible hypothesis**

**Justification:** The underlying claim — that the system is performant — has a theoretical basis (it was built and tested). However, the evidence is far below the threshold:
1. **Magnitude gap:** tested at 2x, claiming 10x. Extrapolation from 2x to 10x (a 5x jump beyond the tested point) is speculative.
2. **Environment gap:** a laptop is not production infrastructure (single machine; no load balancing, network I/O, or distributed caching typical of production). The laptop test may pass while production fails.
3. **Sample size:** one test run is not a replicable result.

The claim would need: (a) load tests at 5x and 10x on production-grade infrastructure, (b) sustained load (not spike), (c) tail latency percentiles (p99, p999), (d) failure mode documentation (at what traffic level does throughput degrade or latency exceed SLA?).

**Why not [S] (Speculative)?** Because there is one test result, making it [H] rather than [S]. But the test is too small and wrong-environment to support the claim.

## Summary Verdict

**Table of claims and tiers:**

| # | Claim | Tier | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Recall 0.94 on held-out data | [K] | PROVEN |
| 2 | Fraud losses fell 22% | [KKE] | NOT PROVEN (missing seasonal control) |
| 3 | Fair across customer groups | [H] | NOT PROVEN (subjective, unquantified) |
| 4 | Analysts trust the model | [H] | NOT PROVEN (anecdotal only) |
| 5 | FP rate below old engine | [K] | PROVEN |
| 6 | Scales to 10x traffic | [H] | NOT PROVEN (tested at 2x on laptop) |

**Board-readiness assessment:** Two of six claims are proven. Four require work. The team lead's request for "quick sign-off" of all claims cannot be granted. A revised board summary should:

1. **Lead with the two proven claims** (recall on held-out data, FP rate advantage) — these are strong and specific.
2. **Separate the fairness claim:** present it as a known concern under investigation, not a confirmed property. Propose a fairness audit with specific metrics before launch to additional segments.
3. **Replace the analyst-trust anecdote** with a structured post-deployment survey, or remove it and let usage metrics speak (e.g., % of model's decisions that analysts act on without override).
4. **Reframe the fraud-loss result** as a seasonal phenomenon requiring investigation: "Fraud losses in Q4 fell 22% vs Q3; this quarter Q4 is seasonally higher. We are conducting a year-over-year analysis to isolate the model's contribution."
5. **Defer the scaling claim** to post-pilot, or replace it with a specific production load-test plan (5x in Q1, 10x in Q2 with infrastructure readiness documented).

**Risk statement:** Presenting all six to the board as proven creates two downstream risks:
- **Tier drift:** claims [H] and [KKE] will be silently cited as [K] in later communications, and false confidence will accumulate.
- **Escape:** when a fairness problem or performance issue surfaces post-launch, it will be attributed to "unknown unknowns" rather than to a documented gap that was present at sign-off.

A governance body benefits from a tiered summary more than a binary "all proven / not proven." The above separates the evidence carefully.
