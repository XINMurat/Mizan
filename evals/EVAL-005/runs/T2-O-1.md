# Fraud Model Board Summary — Audit Report

**Audit mode: retrospective (Claim audit)**  
**HARKing status:** This audit is retrospective. The claims were selected after outcomes were known. The board summary omits failures, deferrals, and discarded model iterations, following a format that structurally rewards positive framing.

---

## Executive Summary

Of the six claims presented to the board, **two are proven** (1, 5) and **four are not proven** (2, 3, 4, 6). Three critical failures represent unforced errors: a confound was explicitly named but not controlled (claim 2), fairness was asserted without quantitative thresholds (claim 3), and load scaling was extrapolated 5× beyond test depth (claim 6). Claim 5 represents the audit's methodology correctly applied.

---

## Individual Claims

### Claim 1: Model recall is 0.94 on held-out 2025 data
**Atomized claims:**  
(a) The recall number is 0.94  
(b) The data is held-out from the modelling team  
(c) It was evaluated by an independent risk-audit team

**Source verification:**
- Direct evidence: independent team evaluation; held-out test set the modelling team did not see
- Methodology sound: held-out validation prevents overfitting and allows one true measure
- Threshold implied: 0.94 is stated as the target result, crossed

**Evidence tier: [K] Proven**  
Direct evidence from properly segregated evaluation. The independence of the auditing team and the held-out data status eliminate the most common confounds (training-set overfitting, evaluator bias toward builders).

---

### Claim 2: Fraud losses fell 22% since launch
**Atomized claims:**  
(a) Fraud losses decreased 22%  
(b) This quarter vs. previous quarter  
(c) No comparison group exists  
(d) Q4 is usually higher than Q3 (seasonal pattern named)

**Source verification:**
- Measurement: Stated as fact; no source document cited to verify
- Confound named but not controlled: The claim itself acknowledges Q4 is seasonally higher than Q3
- Missing control: No comparison group means the decline could be explained by (i) seasonal regression, (ii) regression to the mean from an unusual Q3 value, (iii) external factors unrelated to the model
- The named seasonal pattern IS the confounder, and it was not tested

**Critical finding:** The audit explicitly states the confound in parentheses ("Q4 losses are usually higher than Q3") but presents the comparison anyway. This is threshold theatre: the number appears to prove the model works, but the structure of the measurement can never tell. A symmetric control (model off / model on in same season) or a multi-year comparison neutralizing seasonality would resolve this.

**Evidence tier: [KKE] Critical control missing**  
The result exists (22% decline, Q4 vs Q3), but a confound that could flip the interpretation (seasonal patterns) has not been tested. Moving from quarter to quarter without controlling for seasonal effect is not just weak evidence; it is evidence of the confounder's direction, not the claim's truth.

**Revised claim (if control were added):** Fraud losses fell 22% in Q4 2025 vs Q3 2025, with a symmetric seasonal control (Q4 2024 vs Q3 2024 decline under the old rules engine).

### Claim 3: The model is fair across customer groups
**Atomized claims:**  
(a) The model is fair  
(b) This was demonstrated across customer groups  
(c) Evidence: modelling team reviewed 50 decisions  
(d) No problems were observed

**Source verification:**
- Evidence quality: 50 decisions is a small sample for a fairness claim across multiple customer groups
- Measurement instrument: "saw no problems" is subjective; no quantitative fairness metric (e.g., false-positive rate disparity < 5%, precision parity across groups)
- Evaluator: The modelling team that built the model (not independent)
- Coverage: "Customer groups" is not enumerated — which groups, how many, how were they sampled?

**Structural problem:** Fairness requires a quantified threshold and an arbiter other than the builder. "Reviewed and saw no problems" delegates the verdict to subjective judgment, which cannot carry a [K] tier.

**Evidence tier: [H] Plausible hypothesis**  
The team believes the model is fair and saw no red flags in 50 sampled decisions. This is evidence that fairness is possible and worth testing further, not evidence that fairness is proven. The measurement is subjective, the evaluator is biased toward acceptance, and no quantitative threshold was locked before review.

### Claim 4: Analysts trust the model
**Atomized claims:**  
(a) Analysts trust the model  
(b) Evidence: Two senior analysts said so in a meeting

**Source verification:**
- Sample size: N = 2
- Measurement: "Said so in a meeting" — no structured survey, no instrument for measuring trust
- Selection: No information on how these two analysts were chosen, whether others were asked, whether their statements were solicited or volunteered
- Generalization: "Analysts trust" (plural, broad) from two data points

**Confound:** Trust correlates with many things other than model quality (it being new, it having high recall on the demo set, management pressure, career incentive to appear positive). No symmetric test (would they trust a random classifier with the same recall number?).

**Evidence tier: [S] Speculative**  
This is an anecdote, not a measurement. Two individuals expressed trust; this is interesting but unquantified, and cannot distinguish the model's quality from its novelty or status.

---

### Claim 5: False-positive rate below the old rules engine
**Atomized claims:**  
(a) New model FP rate: 1.8%  
(b) Old rules engine FP rate: 4.6%  
(c) Both ran on the same 3 months of live traffic (shadow mode)  
(d) Both results logged by the payments platform

**Source verification:**
- Direct comparison: Both systems measured on identical traffic, same time window
- Independent measurement: Payments platform (not the modelling team) logged the results
- Quantitative result: 1.8% vs 4.6% is precise and differentiable
- Methodology: Shadow mode (both systems run in parallel on live data without changing behavior) eliminates deployment confounds

**No named confound left uncontrolled:** The comparison accounts for traffic distribution, seasonality, and data quality (same source).

**Evidence tier: [K] Proven**  
The new model's false-positive rate is 1.8% versus the old engine's 4.6%, measured on identical production traffic by an independent system. The symmetric control (old vs. new, same data) and the neutral arbiter (platform logs) make this the audit's strongest claim.

---

### Claim 6: Will scale to 10x current traffic
**Atomized claims:**  
(a) The model will scale to 10x current traffic  
(b) Load-tested once, at 2x, on a laptop

**Source verification:**
- Test depth: 1 test (not replicated)  
- Test scale: 2x (not 10x; extrapolation distance is 5×)  
- Test environment: Laptop (not production hardware; CPU, memory, and I/O are different at scale)
- Stopping rule: "Load-tested once" — single run with no stated stopping rule for failure mode
- Failure is loud: No statement of how the test would fail or timeout if overloaded

**Structural problem:** This is extrapolation without error bars. A 2x test on a laptop can tell you whether the model runs at all; it cannot tell you whether it remains stable, responsive, and accurate at 10x on production hardware. The margin between 2x and 10x is where most scaling failures appear (cache misses, contention, thermal throttling).

**Evidence tier: [S] Speculative**  
The model works at 2x on a laptop. Whether it scales to 10x in production is a hypothesis worth testing but not one the current evidence supports. The extrapolation distance is too large, the environment too different, and the test is not replicated.

**Revised claim (if strengthened):** The model scaled cleanly to 2x on a laptop. Production scaling to 10x is planned and will be tested under staged load on production hardware.

## Missing Card: What the board summary cannot show

This format omits:
1. **Failures and negative results:** Are there customer segments where fairness is poor? Did the model underperform in any scenario? Were there other candidate models tested and rejected?
2. **Deferrals and pending tests:** Claims 2 and 6 both name their open questions (seasonality, scaling) but present them as closed. What tests are planned to close them?
3. **Baseline and comparison:** Claim 1's 0.94 recall is strong, but no baseline is given. Is 0.94 better than random? Better than simple rules? Better than the prior model?
4. **Costs of commission and omission:** The false-negative rate is not stated. At 1.8% false-positive rate, what is the false-negative rate, and what are the customer- and fraud-team costs of each?

## Next Steps

**Critical (blocks board sign-off):**
1. **Claim 2:** Add a symmetric seasonal control (same quarter comparison year-over-year, or model-on / model-off in same quarter). Current evidence does not rule out seasonality.
2. **Claim 6:** Stage load tests at 5x and 10x on production hardware before scaling. "Load-tested once at 2x on a laptop" is not scaling validation.

**High priority (evidence tier currently [H]; should move to [K] or be removed):**
3. **Claim 3:** Define fairness as a quantitative metric (e.g., false-positive rate disparity across groups < 5%), lock the threshold, and re-test on a larger sample (at least 200–500 decisions) with an independent auditor. "No problems seen" is subjective.

**Medium priority (evidence tier currently [S]; acceptable as hypothesis, not for board summary):**
4. **Claim 4:** Replace anecdote with quantitative measurement (e.g., survey of all analysts who interact with the model; ask specific trust questions on a Likert scale). Or remove from board summary and move to hypothesis registry.

---

## Verdict Summary

The model has **demonstrable strengths** (independent recall validation on held-out data, lower false-positive rate in shadow testing). It also has **demonstrable gaps** (seasonal confound uncontrolled, scaling untested, fairness unquantified, trust anecdotal).

The team lead's confidence that "all are proven" conflicts with the evidence. The audit recommends reframing the board presentation to separate proven claims (1, 5) from plausible hypotheses (3, 4, 6 as currently stated) and open questions (2, as currently framed).
