# Care App Board Summary — Audit Report

**Audit date:** 2026-09-29  
**HARKing status:** Retrospective review of claims selected for board presentation. Positive framing throughout; no evidence of counter-example search.

---

## Summary

The board summary contains **one rigorous claim, three claims with supporting evidence but validity limits, one claim with a known fatal confound, and one claim that misrepresents silence as proof.** The team lead's confidence in "all proven" is not supported by the evidence tier distribution. Critical: **Claim 1 contradicts Claim 2 by a factor of 1.55** (claims 30% absolute reduction, actual trial shows 19.4% relative reduction).

---

## Claim-by-claim audit

### Claim 1: The app reduces hospital readmissions by 30%. (Users compared with non-users; users were younger and healthier.)

**Evidence:** Observational comparison between app users and non-users.

**Tier:** [Y] — **Misleading**

**Justification:** The parenthetical disclosure identifies the fatal confound: users are "younger and healthier." Age and health status are known drivers of hospital readmission independent of any app. A 30% reduction *in a confounded comparison* cannot be attributed to the app; younger, healthier populations consistently have lower readmission rates. The claim technically reports observed numbers but frames them to imply app efficacy when the mechanism is demonstrably absent.

**Critical note:** This contradicts Claim 2. The preregistered trial (the only evidence meeting trial standards) shows **11.2% vs 13.9% = 2.7 percentage-point difference = 19.4% relative reduction**, not 30%. One number is factually wrong.

---

### Claim 2: In a preregistered randomized trial (n = 1,240) with an independent data monitoring board, 90-day readmission was 11.2% with the app vs 13.9% with usual care.

**Evidence:** Preregistered RCT with n=1,240, randomization, independent data monitoring board, specific outcome at specific timepoint.

**Tier:** [K] — **Proven**

**Justification:** This is the methodologically sound claim. Preregistration, randomization, and independent oversight are the gold standard for causal evidence. The numbers are precise and the study design is named. However: the 2.7 pp difference (19.4% relative reduction) does not support Claim 1's "30%" without further investigation into whether this was an interim look, whether the trial completed, or whether the 30% was from a different metric.

**Honesty annex:** No scope limits, sample-size caveats, or exclusion rules are stated. Does n=1,240 include all randomized participants (ITT) or a subset? Were losses to follow-up or protocol violations analyzed? These details affect the strength.

---

### Claim 3: Doctors recommend it. (The company's own medical advisory board endorses it.)

**Evidence:** Endorsement by the company's own medical advisory board.

**Tier:** [H] — **Plausible hypothesis**; [KKE] flag: **Critical control missing**

**Justification:** The evidence is biased by definition. Advisory boards appointed by the company have financial and reputational incentives to endorse its product. The claim "doctors recommend it" is distinct from "our medical board endorses it." To tier this [K], you need independent endorsements from doctors outside the company's control — literature citations, external board memberships, published recommendations. None are provided.

---

### Claim 4: Patients love it. (4.8 stars in the app store.)

**Evidence:** App Store rating of 4.8 stars.

**Tier:** [K] — **Proven** (with caveats)

**Justification:** 4.8 stars is strong evidence of user satisfaction. The App Store is a standard source and ratings are auditable. However, app store ratings exhibit selection bias: satisfied users rate more readily than dissatisfied ones who simply uninstall. Users who experienced adverse events may not return to rate. The claim "love it" infers an emotional state from a rating; 4.8 stars is consistent with love but not diagnostic of it alone. For [K], this is defensible; for a claim about emotional resonance, note the gap between behavior (high rating) and inference (love).

---

### Claim 5: The app has 52,000 monthly active users. (Analytics platform, standard MAU definition.)

**Evidence:** Analytics platform data, standard definition of MAU.

**Tier:** [K] — **Proven**

**Justification:** A concrete, time-stamped, auditable fact. The reference to "standard MAU definition" means the number is comparable to industry benchmarks. No counter-evidence is apparent.

---

### Claim 6: It is safe. (No adverse events were reported to the company's support email.)

**Evidence:** Absence of reports to company support email.

**Tier:** [Y] — **Misleading**; escalate to [KKE] — **Critical control missing**

**Justification:** This commits the classic inference error: "not reported" ≠ "did not occur." Adverse events may not be reported because users switched to competitors, were unable to contact support, reported to their doctor instead, or had events outside the observation window. Passive surveillance through company email is the weakest possible monitoring. Real safety claims require active monitoring — adverse event reporting systems, spontaneous report databases, clinical oversight, or post-market surveillance. The claim frames an absence of reports as proof of safety, which is misleading. This is the highest-risk claim in the summary.

## Cross-claim audit

**Discrepancy: Claim 1 vs. Claim 2**

Claim 1 states "30% reduction." Claim 2 (the trial) shows 2.7 pp reduction out of 13.9% = 19.4% relative reduction. These cannot both be accurate descriptions of the same app. Possibilities:

1. Claim 1 refers to a different patient population or metric.
2. The trial is interim and the full trial shows larger effect.
3. One number is wrong.

This must be resolved before board presentation. If Claim 1 is based on different analysis, it should be disclosed as such. If it is wrong, it should be corrected.

## Missing card

**What this format cannot show:**
- Whether users actually adhered to the app (downloaded ≠ used ≠ used correctly).
- Long-term outcomes beyond 90 days.
- Cost-effectiveness relative to usual care.
- Harms that users chose not to report.
- External replication: is this app's effect reproducible in other sites?

## Domain probe (required for coverage claim)

The healthcare domain includes scenarios where failure is silent:

1. **End of life / discharge:** A user admitted to a hospice is "removed" from readmission tracking because they are expected to die. Readmission ≠ re-hospitalization; definition matters.
2. **Loss to follow-up:** A user moves, gets insurance elsewhere, or dies. The app had no opportunity to fail. Is this censored or counted as "no readmission"?
3. **Iatrogenic harm:** The app discourages appropriate re-hospitalization (a user had a heart attack but app said "rest at home"). This is not captured in readmission rates.
4. **Reporting bias reversal:** Users who stopped using the app are less likely to complete follow-up surveys; users who loved it bias toward compliance. The trial's ITT analysis mitigates this only if losses to follow-up are low and reported.

**Not supplied by:** No domain expert (clinician, hospital administrator, patients) was consulted during this audit. The scenarios above are plausible but not confirmed. **Audit status on coverage: [H]** — cannot upgrade to [K] coverage claim without domain expert input per Mizan R19.

## Recommendations (criticality × impact / effort)

### Priority 1: Resolve Claim 1 discrepancy
- **Situation:** Claim 1 (30%) contradicts Claim 2 trial data (19.4%). Board will not accept both.
- **Option A:** Retract Claim 1; present only the trial data (Claim 2).
- **Option B:** Disclose that Claim 1 is observational, confounded, and lower-tier evidence than Claim 2.
- **Option C:** Clarify whether Claim 1 used a different endpoint, larger population, or longer follow-up. If so, name it.
- **Recommendation:** Option B. Never present confounded data as if confounds don't exist. The board can decide whether to act on confounded evidence, but they must know it is confounded.
- **Cost of wrong decision:** Overstating efficacy to the board risks future credibility and patient safety if the 30% was marketing optimization rather than real effect.

### Priority 2: Safety claim (Claim 6)
- **Situation:** "No reports = safe" is actively misleading and a liability.
- **Option A:** Remove the claim entirely.
- **Option B:** Reframe as "no adverse events reported to support email" and note that active safety monitoring is needed for definitive claims.
- **Option C:** Set up actual adverse event monitoring (AE reporting system, follow-up surveys) and delay board presentation.
- **Recommendation:** Option A if timeline is urgent; Option B with a date to implement Option C. Passive surveillance is not safety proof.
- **Cost of wrong decision:** If an adverse event occurs post-launch, the board's sign-off on "It is safe" becomes a liability.

### Priority 3: Doctor recommendation (Claim 3)
- **Situation:** Internal advisory board ≠ independent doctor recommendation.
- **Recommendation:** Seek independent clinical endorsement or reframe the claim to "Our medical advisory board endorses it" (smaller, but honest).
- **Cost of wrong decision:** Moderate; inaccuracy is detectable but often overlooked in board presentations.

### Priority 4: Safety probe (domain expert review)
- **Situation:** No clinician was asked whether the 90-day readmission metric fully captures safety.
- **Recommendation:** Schedule a one-hour conversation with a hospitalist or primary-care physician to validate scenarios (end-of-life, loss-to-follow-up, iatrogenic harm, reporting bias). Use Mizan checklist item 12.
- **Cost of wrong decision:** Low-effort, high-value. Prevents a gap found later.
