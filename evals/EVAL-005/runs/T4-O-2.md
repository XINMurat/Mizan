# Security Product Board Summary — Audit Report

## Overview
Six claims presented for board sign-off regarding a security product deployment. The team lead asserts all are proven. This audit applies evidence-tiering discipline to each claim using Mizan methodology. HARKing status: This is retrospective analysis (four months operational history reviewed after results are known); the claims appear selected because they are favorable outcomes.

## Claims and Evidence Tiers

### Claim 1: The product blocks 99.9% of attacks.
**Source:** Vendor's own lab test; the attack set was chosen by the vendor.

**Atomization:** Two claims embedded: (a) a specific numerical efficacy rate exists; (b) that rate is 99.9%.

**Evidence check:** The attack set was selected by the vendor. This introduces selection bias — a vendor choosing which attacks to test against its own product creates a measurement that proves the vendor's ability to choose favorable examples, not the product's real-world efficacy. The test is internally valid (the product does block 99.9% of those specific attacks) but externally invalid (those attacks do not represent the distribution of real-world threats).

**Tier:** `[Y]` Yanıltıcı / Misleading  
**Justification:** The 99.9% figure is technically accurate for the vendor-selected test set but frames the result in a way that implies broader coverage than the evidence supports. A 99.9% rate against a curated, vendor-favorable attack set is not equivalent to 99.9% real-world effectiveness.

---

### Claim 2: We have had no breaches since we installed it.
**Source:** Four months of operation; no breach detected.

**Atomization:** Two separable claims: (a) no breach has been detected (observational); (b) no breach has occurred (causal).

**Evidence check:** Detection and occurrence are not identical. A four-month window is insufficient for statistical power — breach occurrence is a rare event, and four months is below the median time-to-first-breach in most threat models. The claim also lacks a baseline: how many breaches occurred (or went undetected) in the four months *before* deployment? Absence of detected breaches over four months could be regression to the mean rather than product efficacy.

**Tier:** `[H]` Makul Hipotez / Plausible hypothesis  
**Justification:** The observation (no detected breaches in four months) is real but too brief and baselined against the wrong reference. A claim of preventive efficacy requires: (1) a longer observation window, (2) known baseline breach rate before deployment, and (3) adjustment for confounds (staff training, policy changes, threat environment shifts).

---

### Claim 3: Mean time to detect (MTTD) fell from 19 to 6 hours, measured by the external SOC provider over 11 months before and 11 after, with the same incident definitions and no other tooling change.
**Source:** External SOC provider measurements; changelog attached (unreviewed).

**Evidence check:** This is the strongest claim in the set. Elements supporting tier K: (1) external measurement source (third party, not the vendor or internal team), (2) controlled conditions (same incident definitions, no other tooling changes), (3) adequate observation windows (11 months each), (4) specific, numerically locked threshold (6 hours down from 19). However, "no other tooling change" does not exclude confounds outside tooling: team staffing changes, shift schedules, process improvements, training cycles, or alert fatigue trends in SOC operations. The changelog is cited but its contents are unreviewed — if it shows no material process changes, this approaches K; if it shows process changes concurrent with deployment, the improvement may be confounded. The claim is also asymmetrically sensitive to the arbiter's classification of "same incident definitions" (SOC's own judgment).

**Tier:** `[K]` Kanıtlanmış / Proven — *conditional on changelog review*  
**Justification:** Direct measurement by independent party over sufficient time windows with controlled incident definitions. The threshold is locked and the improvement is quantified. However, this tier assumes the changelog review confirms no concurrent process changes. If unavailable, this downgrades to `[KKE]` (critical control missing: confound separation).

**Status:** Requires validation of attached changelog to confirm no process-level changes during the 11-month windows.

---

### Claim 4: Staff find it easy to use.
**Source:** The IT team that chose it says so.

**Atomization:** The claim is "ease of use is high" with supporting evidence "staff (specifically, the IT team) report satisfaction."

**Evidence check:** The source is biased: the IT team that selected the product has motivated reasoning to defend their choice. No objective usability metric is provided (time to common tasks, error rate, help-desk ticket volume, training time, survey of *all* users vs. selective report from the choosing team). Subjective assessment from a self-interested source without baseline or comparison. "Easy" is also undefined — easy relative to what?

**Tier:** `[H]` Makul Hipotez / Plausible hypothesis  
**Justification:** Subjective report from biased source. The team that chose the product has incentive to report satisfaction. Without objective measure (usability testing, broader staff survey, help desk metrics) or comparison (easier than predecessor? easier than competitors?), this remains a plausible but untested hypothesis.

---

### Claim 5: The licence costs $84,000 a year.
**Source:** Signed contract.

**Atomization:** A single, factual claim: the annual license fee is $84,000.

**Evidence check:** A signed contract is direct evidence of pricing agreement. Assuming the contract is available for inspection and $84,000 is the sole or primary annual fee (not a base with additional per-unit charges), this is verifiable fact.

**Tier:** `[K]` Kanıtlanmış / Proven  
**Justification:** Direct evidence from a binding legal document. Assumes the contract is available for spot-check verification and that no material per-incident or per-user additional charges apply.

---

### Claim 6: It protects against zero-day attacks.
**Source:** Marketing brochure.

**Atomization:** Two interpretations: (a) the product has a design feature intended to mitigate zero-day attacks (e.g., exploit mitigation, behavioral analysis); (b) the product successfully prevents zero-day exploits in practice.

**Evidence check:** A zero-day attack is, by definition, an attack unknown to vendors and public security databases at the time of initial exploitation. No product can guarantee protection against unknown attacks through signature-based detection, though some use heuristic/behavioral approaches. The only source is a marketing brochure, which makes assertions but provides no test results, third-party validation, or operational data. A marketing claim is not evidence of efficacy.

**Tier:** `[S]` Spekülatif / Speculative — or `[Y]` if the brochure misleadingly implies proven protection  
**Justification:** The claim is either aspirational (the product *attempts* to mitigate zero-day patterns) or misleading (the product *prevents* zero-day attacks). If the former, it is speculative — interesting but not yet tested. If interpreted as a proof claim, it is misleading marketing. Marketing alone is not evidence; this claim requires independent testing or operational incident data to be tiered above `[S]`.

## Summary: Hit Rates and Missing Controls

| Claim | Tier | Verdict |
|-------|------|---------|
| 1. 99.9% attack block | [Y] Misleading | NOT PROVEN |
| 2. No breaches in 4 months | [H] Plausible | NOT PROVEN |
| 3. MTTD 19→6 hours | [K] Proven* | *Conditional* |
| 4. Easy to use | [H] Plausible | NOT PROVEN |
| 5. $84k annual cost | [K] Proven | PROVEN |
| 6. Zero-day protection | [S] Speculative | NOT PROVEN |

**Hit rate:** 1 claim clearly proven (claim 5, a cost fact), 1 claim conditionally proven pending changelog review (claim 3), 4 claims below evidence threshold.

## Critical Controls Missing

- **Claim 1:** Real-world threat distribution; vendor-neutral attack set; competitive benchmark.
- **Claim 2:** Pre-deployment breach baseline; longer observation window; detection vs. occurrence separation.
- **Claim 3:** Changelog review; process-change audit; SOC staffing/training stability confirmation.
- **Claim 4:** Objective usability metrics; broader staff survey; before/after comparison or competitive comparison.
- **Claim 6:** Independent zero-day testing; operational incident data; definition of "zero-day protection" (detection, mitigation, prevention, or recovery?).

## Recommendation for the Board

**Do not present claims 1, 2, 4, and 6 as proven.** These fold marketing assertions, selection bias, and subjective self-report into tier tags they do not support. Claim 3 (MTTD improvement) is the product's strongest evidence point; present it alone, **conditional on changelog review by an auditor independent of the product team**. Claim 5 (licensing cost) is a fact and may be stated.

**The appropriate summary to the board:** "One metric — mean time to detect — shows a 68% improvement (19 to 6 hours) measured by our SOC provider over 11-month windows. This is our most robust evidence. Four other claims lack sufficient evidence for board-level confidence and should not be presented as proven. The cost statement is factual."

---

## HARKing Status

This audit is retrospective: outcomes are known (four months of operational data) and the claims were selected after results appeared favorable. The audit occurred after deployment, so thresholds were not locked before observation. This does not invalidate the findings, but it means the evidence set is subject to selection bias — claims chosen for favorable framing. A prospective registry going forward should preregister thresholds and claim criteria before the next review cycle.
