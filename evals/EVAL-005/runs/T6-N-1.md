# Care App Board Summary — Audit Report

## Executive Summary

This audit reviews six claims presented for board sign-off tomorrow. The team lead considers all proven; independent verification shows a pattern of selection bias, uncontrolled comparisons, and passive absence-of-evidence claims presented as affirmative evidence. Of six claims, two merit `[K]` (Proven), one is `[H]` (Plausible Hypothesis), and three are `[KKE]` or `[Y]` (control missing or misleading framing).

**This audit is retrospective:** the claims have been seen, and I am now assessing their evidence. The framing of evidence to the board may itself have been shaped by the results.

---

## Claim-by-Claim Analysis

### Claim 1: "The app reduces hospital readmissions by 30%"

**Quoted from summary:** "Users compared with non-users; users were younger and healthier."

**Status:** `[Y]` — Misleading (technically true but framed to imply causality without controlling for confounds)

**The problem:** The 30% difference is observed in a comparison between fundamentally different groups. The context reveals selection bias: users are younger and healthier. Young, healthy people naturally have lower readmission rates. The observed difference may be entirely explained by baseline health differences, not the app.

**Mechanism of error:** A raw group difference without randomization or matched controls conflates three possible causes: (a) the app works, (b) users were always lower-risk, (c) confounding by indication (people confident in their health chose the app). The evidence provided cannot distinguish these.

**What is missing:** A control group of comparable age/health receiving usual care, or a randomized trial.

**Verdict:** NOT PROVEN

---

### Claim 2: "In a preregistered randomized trial (n = 1,240) with an independent data monitoring board, 90-day readmission was 11.2% with the app vs 13.9% with usual care."

**Status:** `[K]` — Proven

**Why this holds:** 
- Explicit randomization eliminates selection bias and confounding by indication
- Preregistration indicates thresholds were locked before observing results (no threshold shopping)
- Independent data monitoring board is a credible third-party arbiter
- The claim is a specific, measured result, not an interpretation
- The quoted numbers are directly verifiable from trial records

**What it does NOT establish:** Statistical significance, clinical significance, or safety profile. (Those belong in a separate tier assessment if claimed.)

**Caveat:** This verdict assumes the trial was conducted as stated. If the trial report itself is not available for inspection, the tier would drop to `[H]`.

**Verdict:** PROVEN

---

### Claim 3: "Doctors recommend it"

**Quoted evidence:** "The company's own medical advisory board endorses it."

**Status:** `[H]` — Plausible Hypothesis (some evidence exists; claim overstates its scope)

**The problem:** The company's own advisory board is a selected group, not a representative sample of doctors. This is selection bias and authority without independence. A company typically recruits board members it expects to be supportive.

**What the evidence actually shows:** A small group of doctors chosen and retained by the company endorses the app. This is consistent with doctors recommending it, but it is not evidence that doctors *broadly* recommend it.

**What is missing:** A claim like "56% of cardiologists surveyed would recommend it" paired with the actual survey. Or third-party physician endorsements. Or prescription/adoption data from independent health systems.

**Verdict:** NOT PROVEN (as stated; a narrower claim — "some doctors on the company board endorse it" — would be proven)

---

### Claim 4: "Patients love it"

**Quoted evidence:** "4.8 stars in the app store."

**Status:** `[H]` — Plausible Hypothesis; with note of selection bias

**The problem:** App store reviews are a biased sample. People who leave reviews tend to be at the extremes (very satisfied or very dissatisfied). A high rating reflects satisfaction among reviewers, not among all users. No information is provided on:
- Total number of reviews
- The population (how many active users never reviewed?)
- Retention rate (do users keep using it?)
- Clinical outcomes (do they report improved health?)

**What the evidence actually shows:** Among users who took time to rate the app, the average rating is very positive. This is consistent with patients liking it, but is not evidence that patients broadly love it.

**What is missing:** Cohort retention data, patient-reported outcomes, or a structured satisfaction survey of a representative user sample.

**Verdict:** NOT PROVEN (as stated); a narrower claim — "users who reviewed the app gave it 4.8 stars" — would be proven.

---

### Claim 5: "The app has 52,000 monthly active users"

**Quoted evidence:** "Analytics platform, standard MAU definition."

**Status:** `[K]` — Proven

**Why this holds:**
- Monthly Active Users is a standard, measurable metric with a defined threshold (logged in and took an action during the calendar month, typically)
- The source is cited as a company analytics platform
- The number is specific and directly verifiable

**Caveat:** This verdict assumes the analytics platform is implemented correctly and the company is reading it accurately. It does not establish whether 52,000 users represents growth, stagnation, or decline (context not provided), nor does it speak to retention or engagement.

**Verdict:** PROVEN

---

### Claim 6: "It is safe"

**Quoted evidence:** "No adverse events were reported to the company's support email."

**Status:** `[KKE]` — Critical Control Missing

**The problem:** Absence of reports is not evidence of safety. This is passive surveillance through a single channel (support email). Many adverse events go unreported:
- Mild side effects are rarely reported
- Users may not connect a symptom to the app
- Serious events may bypass support email and go directly to a doctor or emergency department
- Reporting depends on whether users know where/how to report

**What the evidence actually shows:** The company received no adverse event reports via support email during an unspecified time period.

**What is missing:** Active safety monitoring, such as:
- Structured follow-up calls or surveys asking about adverse events
- Linkage to hospital discharge/ED data to catch serious events
- A formal adverse event reporting line (not just support email)
- Third-party safety monitoring (especially critical for a health app)

**Verdict:** NOT PROVEN; a proper safety claim requires active surveillance, not passive.

## Recommendation for the Board

**Do not present claims 1, 3, 4, and 6 as proven.** Each conflates a partial truth with a broader claim:
- Claim 1 is an observed difference, not proof of efficacy
- Claim 3 is vendor-selected endorsement, not independent recommendation
- Claim 4 is reviewer sentiment, not universal user satisfaction
- Claim 6 is absence of reports, not proof of safety

**Presenting these as proven creates regulatory and reputational risk:** if adopted based on overstated evidence and then a problem surfaces, the board will have approved a claim known to rest on incomplete evidence.

**Better framing for the board:**
- Lead with Claim 2 (the actual trial result — genuine evidence)
- Report Claim 5 as usage milestone (neutral fact)
- For the others, present honestly: "Our advisory board endorses it" (not doctors broadly), "app store rating is 4.8" (not universal love), "we've received no safety reports via our support channel" (not proof of safety; active monitoring recommended before scale)

This approach is faster to defend and more likely to build lasting confidence than claims that will not survive the first independent audit.
