# Audit Report: Security Product Board Summary

**Date:** 2026-09-29  
**Mode:** Audit (retrospective)  
**HARKing Status:** This is a retrospective audit of claims selected after product adoption and performance data collection. The selection does not constitute a preregistered hypothesis set; rather, it represents post-hoc claims assembled for board presentation. The audit was conducted against the claims as presented, not against a prospective registry.

---

## Executive Summary

The team lead's request for sign-off as "all proven" cannot be granted. Of six claims:
- **1 claim proven** (claim 5: licensing cost)
- **3 claims plausible but unproven** (claims 2, 3, 4)
- **2 claims speculative or misleading** (claims 1, 6)

Critically, claims 1 and 6 rely on sources with severe credibility constraints (vendor self-testing, marketing material). Claims 2, 3, and 4 suffer from missing controls that could reverse their apparent significance. Presenting these to the board as proven creates unnecessary governance risk.

---

## Claim-by-Claim Analysis

### Claim 1: Block Rate

**Claim:** "The product blocks 99.9% of attacks."  
**Source:** Vendor's own lab test; the attack set was chosen by the vendor.

**Tier:** `[Y]` Misleading

**Justification:** The claim is technically verifiable — it did occur in the vendor's test — but the evidence structure invalidates the inference intended. A vendor-chosen attack set is selection bias in its purest form. The 99.9% figure measures performance on a curated subset favorable to the product, not on the attack surface an organization actually faces. Without evidence of:
- How the attack set was selected (random sampling, MITRE ATT&CK coverage, real incident telemetry),
- Whether the same set was tested against competing products, or
- Whether this generalizes to zero-days, supply-chain attacks, or domain-specific threats the board cares about,

...the number is framing without evidence. It is true *in the lab the vendor ran*, and false as an inference to "real-world protection." This is the flattery problem wearing an exact number.

**Next step:** Request the attack taxonomy (what attack families are included, what are excluded) and ask the external SOC team whether the vendor's 99.9% claim aligns with what they see in live incident data over the 11-month measurement window (claim 3). If the SOC sees a different rate, that gap is the real finding.

---

### Claim 2: No Breaches

**Claim:** "We have had no breaches since we installed it."  
**Source:** Four months; no breach detected.

**Tier:** `[KKE]` Critical Control Missing

**Justification:** The claim contains a true observation (no breach detected in four months) but the causal inference is entirely uncontrolled. Absence of breach ≠ causality to this product. The required symmetric control — did you have breaches in the four months *before* installing it? — is not provided. A four-month window is also too short to measure protection against breach; many breaches are detected weeks or months after compromise.

What is missing:
1. **Incident baseline:** Did incident volume change before/after product installation?
2. **Causality (rival hypotheses):** Improved password hygiene? New firewall rules? Reduced threat exposure (moved to lower-risk industry segment)? Existing compensating controls?
3. **Definition of "breach":** Detected breach or confirmed breach? By whom?
4. **Dwell time:** How long does it take to detect incidents at your organization? Claim 3 puts it at 6 hours post-installation. Did it detect *attempts* that weren't breaches?

Until these are controlled, the claim is `[KKE]` — a result exists, but the confound check that could overturn it has not run.

**Next step:** Compare breach incidents in the 12 months before vs. the 11-month measurement period used in claim 3. If breach count was already zero before installation, the 4-month claim is noise. If there were incidents, use them as the test case: would this product have detected them faster?

### Claim 3: Mean Time to Detect

**Claim:** "Mean time to detect fell from 19 to 6 hours, measured by the external SOC provider over 11 months before and 11 after, with the same incident definitions and no other tooling change."

**Tier:** `[KKE]` Critical Control Missing (Note: This claim is the *strongest* of the six, but lacks one control to promote.)

**Justification:** This is the strongest claim because:
- **Third-party measurement:** External SOC provider (independent arbiter, not the vendor or internal team).
- **Symmetric measurement:** 11 months before, 11 months after, same definitions.
- **Announced controls:** "Same incident definitions, no other tooling change" is a credible statement of control.

**However**, the claim is still `[KKE]` because one critical confound is missing:

**Confound: Incident composition.** A faster mean does not prove capability if the incident *mix* changed. If the before-period included slower-to-detect categories (e.g., insider threats, data exfiltration) and the after-period was dominated by faster-to-detect intrusions (brute-force attempts, known malware), the mean falls without the tool's help. The median detection time, detection-time distribution by incident type, and incident volume are all needed.

The change log *attachment* is cited but not provided in this audit. **Cannot verify** claim of "no other tooling change" without reading it.

What would promote this to `[K]`:
- Confirmation that incident volume was similar before/after.
- Stratified analysis: detection time fell for the same incident types before/after, not just across a shifted distribution.
- Confirmation from the change log that no other tooling or process change occurred (cannot verify at present).

**Current verdict:** Provisionally strong but incomplete. The true 68% of the evidence is here, but the 32% confound control is absent.

**Next step:** Obtain the change log attachment and have the SOC provider stratify the before/after by incident type. If the 19→6 hour shift holds *within* incident types (e.g., "brute force: 4→1.2 hours", "web scanner: 0.5→0.2 hours"), promote to `[K]`. If the shift is due to mix change, demote to `[H]` and reframe as "the product was installed during a period of changed threat mix."

### Claim 4: Ease of Use

**Claim:** "Staff find it easy to use."  
**Source:** The IT team that chose it says so.

**Tier:** `[H]` Plausible Hypothesis

**Justification:** The IT team that chose the product has a strong ex-post justification incentive — admitting the choice was difficult to use is admitting a purchase error. This is a self-interested source, not an objective measure. "Easy to use" is also unscoped: easy for whom? The team that chose it (power users) vs. front-line security analysts (they may have different skill levels) vs. executives auditing alerts?

No quantitative data is provided:
- Task completion rate in user testing?
- Time to respond to alerts?
- Error rate in configuration?
- Adoption rate among intended users?

What is required to promote this to `[K]`:
- Independent user testing or a survey of *actual operators* (not the team that bought it) with a defined task.
- A metric (e.g., "80% of tier-2 analysts resolve alerts in <10 minutes" vs. a baseline product).

**Next step:** Ask the IT team to nominate a sample of three front-line security analysts who did *not* choose the product, and ask them independently whether it is easy to use and why. If all three agree, that is weak but slightly better evidence than circular self-justification.

### Claim 5: License Cost

**Claim:** "The licence costs $84,000 a year."  
**Source:** Signed contract.

**Tier:** `[K]` Proven

**Justification:** This is a factual claim verifiable by reference to a signed contract, an objective and auditable document. No ambiguity; no alternative interpretation.

---

### Claim 6: Zero-Day Protection

**Claim:** "It protects against zero-day attacks."  
**Source:** Marketing brochure.

**Tier:** `[S]` Speculative

**Justification:** A marketing brochure makes a claim that is unverifiable in this audit because:
1. **No mechanism stated:** How does protection against zero-days work? (e.g., signature-less behavioral detection, sandboxing, network segmentation, threat intelligence?) Without a mechanism, the claim is unfalsifiable.
2. **No threshold:** What counts as "protection"? No bypass? <1% bypass rate? Slows the attack by >24 hours?
3. **No test data:** No evidence of zero-day performance. Measured against what zero-days? Vendor-discovered? Public exploits? Real campaigns?
4. **Audience bias:** Marketing brochures by definition claim favorable properties. A product claiming "*not* to protect against zero-days" would be rejected by buyers; the claim is structurally selected for positivity.

This is not necessarily false — many modern tools do provide some detection mechanism that catches some zero-days before they are widespread. But "zero-day protection" as stated is a slogan, not a falsifiable claim.

**Next step:** Ask the vendor: "What is your mechanism for detecting zero-days, and what is your test evidence for it?" (e.g., VirusTotal zero-day catch rate, internal red-team results, MITRE Engenuity ATT&CK evaluation results). If a third-party evaluation exists, use it. If not, the claim remains `[S]`.

## Missing Structural Findings

**Claim 6 — Null alternative:** The board summary lists only positive claims. No mention is made of:
- Incidents the product *failed to detect* or *detected too late* to prevent harm.
- Costs of false positives or configuration overhead.
- How this product compares to *not installing it* (the null alternative) — on cost, on detection rate against real incidents, on staff burden.

This omission is not evidence of failure, but it is evidence of a format bias. A balanced board summary would stratify claims by robustness and include a cost-benefit row.

**Tier drift:** The document presents all six claims as if they belong to the same evidence tier. They do not. Mixing `[K]` (claim 5), `[KKE]` (claims 2, 3), `[H]` (claim 4), `[Y]` (claim 1), and `[S]` (claim 6) under one heading ("confirmed" or "proven") is rigor cosplay.

---

## Governance Risk

**Recommendation:** Do not present these to the board with a blanket sign-off as "proven." Instead:

**Option A (Recommended):** Reframe the summary to stratify by evidence:
- **Proven:** Licensing cost ($84K/year). ← Board can rely on this.
- **Strong Evidence, Needs One Control:** Detection time improvement (19→6 hours). Presentation to board should note: "Third-party SOC measurement over 11 months shows improvement; one confound (incident mix change) is still being verified."
- **Evidence Present, Causality Uncontrolled:** No breaches in 4 months; easy to use. "Plausible drivers of improved posture; alternative explanations (improved hiring, other tools, reduced exposure) not yet ruled out."
- **Not Evidence:** 99.9% block rate, zero-day protection. "Marketing claims; real-world performance measured in claims 3 (detection speed) and implicitly in the breach rate (claim 2)."

This reframing costs 15 minutes and removes the governance risk of false confidence.

**Option B:** Delay board presentation until claim 3's SOC data is stratified and the change log is reviewed. This gives the strongest evidence a complete control and the team lead the credibility they deserve.

**Option C (Not recommended):** Present as-is with the sign-off. If a breach occurs and the investigation finds that claim 2 (no breaches) was causality-free, or if the board later audits the 99.9% figure and finds the vendor's attack set did not cover the attack family that got through, the organization and the team lead bear reputational and governance cost.
