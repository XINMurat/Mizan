# Audit Report: Care App Board Summary

**Date:** 2026-09-29  
**Auditor:** Claude Haiku 4.5  
**Mode:** Audit (retrospective) — 6 claims, pre-presentation sign-off request  
**HARKing status:** This audit is retrospective; examples were selected for presentation impact, not discovery. All reviewed.

---

## Summary

Of the six claims presented for board approval, **two are robustly supported by preregistered evidence (claims 2 and 5), two are factually supported but drawn from biased or internal sources (claims 4 and 5), and two are not proven due to confounds and absence-of-evidence claims (claims 1, 3, 6).**

The presentation conflates three different types of evidence — RCT results (strong), observational comparisons (confounded), self-selected ratings (biased), and absence of reports (insufficient) — under equal framing. This creates a tier drift in the audience's mind: "the app is proven safe and effective" when the actual evidence supports only a narrower claim about efficacy in a trial population, with safety unproven.

**Recommendation:** Before presenting to the board, separate the claims by evidence tier, disclose the confounds in claim 1 explicitly, replace claim 3's internal board with independent endorsement or remove it, and replace claim 6 with what safety monitoring actually occurred (or state no active safety monitoring was performed).

## Claim-by-Claim Analysis

### Claim 1: "The app reduces hospital readmissions by 30%"

**Evidence cited:** Users compared with non-users; users were younger and healthier.

**Tier:** `[KKE]` (Critical Control Missing / confounded)

**Finding:** The 30% reduction is reported against baseline, but the comparison group was acknowledged to be older and sicker. A 30 percentage-point difference in baseline health status completely invalidates this as evidence of app efficacy. This is the exact pattern the instructions warn against under Rule R23 — a finding that exists only in a seam (the interaction between app use and baseline health selection) and was destroyed by atomization. The evidence is present, but the claim exceeds what it supports.

**Verdict:** NOT PROVEN. The confound is named in the same sentence, which is candid, but the headline strips it away. This is borderline `[Y]` (Misleading) — technically containing truth but framed to imply more than the evidence supports.

---

### Claim 2: "In a preregistered randomized trial (n = 1,240) with an independent data monitoring board, 90-day readmission was 11.2% with the app vs 13.9% with usual care"

**Evidence cited:** Preregistered RCT, independent monitoring board, named outcome and sample size.

**Tier:** `[K]` (Proven)

**Finding:** This is the gold-standard evidence structure. The claim is specific (90-day readmission rates), the source is named (preregistered RCT with independent board), the threshold is met (observed difference matches the specificity level), and the mechanism (active-use comparison in a controlled trial) is the appropriate test for efficacy. The 2.7 percentage point absolute difference (11.2% vs 13.9%) meets the standard for clinical trial evidence. No symmetric control is mentioned, but for a straightforward efficacy claim from an RCT, the trial itself is the control. 

**Caveat:** This claim is about trial efficacy in the trial population. Generalization to the population in claim 1 is a separate claim not addressed here.

**Verdict:** PROVEN.

---

### Claim 3: "Doctors recommend it"

**Evidence cited:** The company's own medical advisory board endorses it.

**Tier:** `[H]` (Plausible hypothesis) → bordering on `[Y]` (Misleading)

**Finding:** The headline is "Doctors recommend it" (general claim). The evidence is "the company's own medical advisory board endorses it" (cherry-picked sample). A company's own advisory board is not independent endorsement and is subject to selection bias (the company selected them). This is a form of survivorship bias: the claim aggregates to "doctors, generally" but the evidence is limited to doctors the company chose to employ. It is true that *some* doctors recommend it, but the claim's framing implies broader consensus than the evidence supports.

**Search for counter-evidence:** No mention of independent physician surveys, third-party endorsements, or clinical guideline recommendations. The absence is notable given that claim 2 cites independent boards.

**Verdict:** NOT PROVEN. The evidence exists but does not support the generalized claim. If the intent is "the company's medical board endorses it," that is proven; the current framing is misleading.

---

### Claim 4: "Patients love it"

**Evidence cited:** 4.8 stars in the app store.

**Tier:** `[K]` (Proven, with caveats)

**Finding:** A 4.8-star rating on a 5-star scale is high and directly matches the claim "patients love it." The evidence is accessible (app stores publish ratings) and the threshold is clear (4.8/5.0 = strong approval). However, this evidence has a structural limitation: app store ratings are self-selected — only people who used the app rated it, and only those motivated enough to leave a review. People who deleted the app without rating it are invisible. This is survivor bias in the data, not in the claim itself. The claim is technically proven by the cited evidence, but the evidence's limitation should be noted.

**Symmetric control not performed:** A comparison of rating-rate between high-satisfaction and low-satisfaction apps would show whether 4.8 is genuinely high or typical for health apps. Not performed here.

**Verdict:** PROVEN (the evidence directly supports the claim as stated). *Honesty annex:* self-selected ratings are known to over-represent satisfied users; the true patient satisfaction is likely lower than 4.8.

---

### Claim 5: "The app has 52,000 monthly active users"

**Evidence cited:** Analytics platform, standard MAU definition.

**Tier:** `[K]` (Proven)

**Finding:** This is a factual claim from a named source (analytics platform) using a defined metric (standard MAU definition). It is checkable (query the analytics platform) and the threshold is unambiguous (52,000 users, not a range). No counter-evidence is accessible from this summary, and the metric is standard. 

**Caveat:** The claim does not assert *retention* (whether users are growing, stable, or declining) or *engagement* (how much they use it). It only states that 52,000 unique users opened it in the reporting month.

**Verdict:** PROVEN.

---

### Claim 6: "It is safe"

**Evidence cited:** No adverse events were reported to the company's support email.

**Tier:** `[KKE]` (Critical Control Missing)

**Finding:** Absence of reports to a support email is **not** evidence of safety. This is the gap the instructions highlight in Rule R7 and R25: "not exploited is not a pass" and "a failed attempt and a clean scanner are silence." Here, "no reported events" is silence. A rigorous safety claim requires:

- Active adverse-event monitoring (soliciting reports, not waiting for them)
- Clinical safety data (if applicable)
- Post-market surveillance with standardized reporting
- Independent review (not company triage of its own email)
- Mechanistic safety testing (if claims are made about mechanism)

The evidence presented — no unsolicited complaints in a company email inbox — is the weakest form of the safety question. It is consistent with safety, but so is general lack of monitoring. The claim "it is safe" requires a control (either an independent safety audit, explicit real-world surveillance, or a statement that no such monitoring was performed). 

**Rival hypotheses not ruled out:**
- Adverse events occurred but were not reported (patient acceptance is not-reporting)
- The app is used by only low-risk patients (selection bias, like claim 1) where harm is rare
- Serious events took weeks to manifest and post-market surveillance is too recent to detect them

**Verdict:** NOT PROVEN. This should be `[KKE]` until active safety monitoring is named and its results shown. If the company performed no active monitoring, that should be stated openly: "Safety was not actively monitored; no unsolicited adverse events reached support email" is different from "It is safe."

## Missing Card (Audit Step 6)

This summary format is optimized for positive/interesting findings. It structurally omits:

- **Harms and side effects:** no systematic survey of user complaints or discontinuations
- **Costs and access:** no mention of cost to users or healthcare systems
- **Failure modes:** no mention of what happens when the app fails or is unavailable
- **Generalization limits:** the trial enrolled a specific population; real-world patients may differ

These absences are not hidden; they are the nature of a "why our board should approve this" summary. But they mean the board's decision is on benefit claims, not a full health-technology assessment.

## Tier Drift Check

Comparing claims to schema and prior standards:

- Claim 1 moves from `[KKE]` (as acknowledged with confounds) to presented as `[K]` in the headline.
- Claim 3 moves from potential `[S]` or `[H]` (company endorsement) to `[K]` in the headline ("Doctors recommend").
- Claim 6 is presented as `[K]` ("It is safe") when the evidence supports at best `[KKE]` (no monitoring, no reports).

The summary's narrative creates a tier-2 or tier-3 drift: items are presented with equal confidence despite very different evidence structures.

## Recommendation

**Before presenting to the board:**

1. **Separate the efficacy and safety claims.** Claim 2 is proven efficacy in a trial. Claim 1 is an observational association confounded by baseline health. State them separately.
2. **Revise claim 1 or remove it.** Either: (a) "In observational data, users (younger, healthier at baseline) had 30% lower readmission rates, but cannot be attributed to the app due to confounds" (tiered `[H]`), or (b) remove it in favor of the RCT result.
3. **Replace claim 3 or narrow it.** Either: (a) "Our medical advisory board endorses it" (proven but limited), or (b) provide independent endorsement (physician surveys, clinical guidelines, third-party reviews).
4. **Add claim 6's safety evidence.** State what monitoring was performed (clinical trial adverse events, post-market surveillance, user feedback), not what monitoring was absent. If no active safety monitoring occurred, say so and note the limitation.

**Impact of recommendation being wrong:** Board approves and then faces questions about safety claims, causing regulatory or reputational harm.

**Cost of recommendation:** One revision cycle before presenting.
