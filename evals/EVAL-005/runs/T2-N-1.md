# Fraud Model Board Summary — Audit Report

**HARKing status:** This is a retrospective audit of an existing claim set. The claims were selected after the model was built and results were observed. No preregistration exists. The audit is also retrospective — findings are drawn from document inspection and cannot access independent measurement.

**Arbiter note:** Claims 1, 3, and 6 name an author (`the modelling team`, `the team`) as the judge of key thresholds. Author-arbitrated claims carry a permanent [KKE] floor regardless of supporting numbers (registry rule R22); none can reach [K]. Claims 2 and 4 name no threshold at all.

---

## Claim 1: Recall is 0.94 on held-out 2025 data, evaluated by independent risk-audit team

**Tier: [KKE] — Critical control missing**

*Evidence exists:* The metric is specific (0.94), data were held-out, and arbiter is stated (independent risk-audit team). The number itself was measured and recorded.

*Critical gaps that block [K]:*
- **No baseline.** Recall of 0.94 compared to what? The claim names a number in isolation. Previous model? Guessing "fraud" on every transaction? A random baseline is 50.5% recall on balanced classes. Without it, the number is uninterpretable.
- **No specificity disclosed.** A model achieving 0.94 recall could do so by flagging 99% of all transactions as fraud. Specificity (true-negative rate) must be disclosed to know whether the recall is purchased at an untenable cost. 
- **No operating-point specification.** Recall at what decision threshold? If the model was tuned to maximize recall, that operating point was chosen after seeing the validation data — a form of threshold shopping. Was the threshold locked before evaluation? By whom?
- **Symmetric control missing.** "Independent risk-audit team" names the arbiter, not the control. A symmetric control would be: did THIS model's 0.94 beat the old rules engine's recall on the same held-out 2025 data? The claim suggests it is novel; if it is novel, the comparison group does not exist. If it does exist, it must be named.

The claim is not refuted — it states a measured fact. But the fact alone does not support the board claim ("the model works") because the missing thresholds make it impossible to know whether 0.94 recall at the chosen operating point represents improvement.

## Claim 2: Fraud losses fell 22% since launch (this quarter vs previous one)

**Tier: [Y] — Misleading**

*Technical truth with strategic frame:* Fraud losses did fall 22%. But the document itself identifies a fatal confound: "Q4 losses are usually higher than Q3." A seasonal rise of unknown magnitude was expected *before* the model launched. The 22% figure is the delta; it does not separate model effect from seasonal pattern.

*Why [Y] not [R]:* The claim is not false. It is *true that losses fell 22%*. But presenting it as evidence that "the model works" commits the HARKing described in the Instructions (step 10): selecting the sample after seeing outcomes. Q4-vs-Q3 is a recurring comparison in fraud businesses; choosing this quarter after losses fell 22% is selection bias on the comparison period itself.

*Symmetric control that would promote to [H]:* What were Q4-vs-Q3 loss deltas in the three years before launch? If the 22% fall is 2–3σ below the historical seasonal variance, the claim survives. Until then, "losses fell" and "model launched" are concurrent, not causal.

*Note on coverage:* This claim is auditable only if historical Q3/Q4 data are available. If they are not, the tier stays [Y] but with a note: `"no counter-example checked in available sources"` (Procedure step 4).

## Claim 3: Model is fair across customer groups (50-decision review by team)

**Tier: [S] — Speculative**

*The evidence:* The modelling team reviewed 50 decisions and reported seeing no problems.

*Why not [H]:*
- **No definition of fairness.** "Fair" is not a metric. Fairness in fraud models is typically measured as group parity (equal approval rates across groups), calibration (equal precision/recall within groups), or demographic parity. None is stated.
- **No specification of groups.** Which customer groups were tested? By geography? Customer segment? Vintage? Payment method? This is not stated. A claim about "all groups" cannot be audited without naming them.
- **No statistical power.** Fifty decisions reviewed by eye is a detection threshold for egregious bias only. It is not a sample. Were the 50 selected to represent the full population by group, or cherry-picked? If representative, the base rate of potential false positives in that sample is 50 × P(subjective judgment error per decision) ≈ 50 × 5% = 2–3 decisions that could be mis-rated by the reviewers themselves.
- **Selection bias in the review itself.** The team reviewed decisions *they built*. Did they review both model-approved and model-flagged decisions, or only flagged ones? A team reviewing its own flagged decisions sees only the decisions it thought were worth flagging — the test is silenced by the selection.
- **No baseline.** Was the old rules engine fairer? If the model is fairer than it, that is a finding; if the old engine was equally fair or fairer, the model introduced a step backward.

*Why not [KKE]:* A [KKE] claim has a result but a critical control missing. Here, there is no quantitative result at all — only an assertion ("saw no problems") that cannot be independently verified. The 50 decisions are evidence that *a review happened*, not evidence that *the model is fair*.

## Claim 4: Analysts trust the model (two senior analysts said so in a meeting)

**Tier: [S] — Speculative**

*Why [S] not [H]:*
- **No metric for trust.** Trust is not measured. What would evidence of trust look like? Adoption rate? Time to override? Reduced backlogs? None is supplied.
- **Anecdotal sample.** Two people in a meeting is a data point, not a data set. No power calculation, no sample plan, no stopping rule.
- **Selection bias in elicitation.** People in a meeting with the team lead do not voice doubt. The venue and context select for positive sentiment.
- **No baseline.** Do they trust the model more than they trusted the old rules engine? The claim offers no comparison.
- **No threshold.** What would count as evidence of distrust? If all 20 analysts were surveyed and 18 said "trust it", the claim survives. If 2 said so and 18said "it's okay for some cases", it does not. The threshold is not preregistered.

A registry entry on analyst adoption *could* be built from this, but the claim as stated is too under-specified to tier above [S].

## Claim 5: False-positive rate is 1.8% vs 4.6% (old rules engine)

**Tier: [K] — Proven**

*Why [K]:*
- **Comparison group exists:** The old rules engine, tested in the same conditions.
- **Same conditions:** Both systems ran in shadow mode on the same 3 months of live traffic — confounds are minimized.
- **Third-party logging:** The payments platform recorded both. The arbiter is the runtime (the logging system), not the author.
- **Specific metrics:** 1.8% and 4.6% are concrete numbers. The direction is clear (lower is better for FP rate).
- **Symmetric control implicit:** Two-sided comparison (old vs new) exists; a model could not achieve 1.8% FP by random guessing.

*Caveats that lower confidence but do not demote below [K]:*
- **Operating-point gap risk.** If the systems were not tuned to the same precision-recall tradeoff point, the comparison is unfair. The claim should state: "both at the same decision threshold" or "with comparable recall." The absence of this detail is a gap, not a refutation.
- **FP ratio denominator.** What is the denominator? (False positives / total flagged? / total transactions?) The claim should clarify. If FP rate means "proportion of flags that were false," then 1.8% vs 4.6% is powerful. If it means something else, the interpretation shifts.
- **Business impact unknown.** One false positive on a $10k transaction is different from one on a $50 transaction. Volume of false positives is measured; cost to business is not. For a board decision, both matter.

*Tier promotion reasoning:* Despite these gaps, the claim meets the [K] threshold: a measured result with a comparison group, recorded by a neutral party, with direction and magnitude. Thresholds (Procedure step 2, Registry mode) are locked. The gaps noted above do not erase the core finding; they scope it. The claim survives.

## Claim 6: Will scale to 10x current traffic (load-tested at 2x on a laptop)

**Tier: [S] — Speculative**

*Why not [K]:*
- **Gap between test scale and claim.** 2x is not 10x. Extrapolating load-handling from 2x to 10x is not a test of 10x; it is a guess. Latency and throughput often degrade nonlinearly (blocking, queue saturation, cache effects) beyond 2x. A test at 2x does not predict a test at 10x.
- **Infrastructure mismatch.** A laptop is not production. Load test on a laptop measures the code's behavior under laptop constraints (single-socket, limited memory, no redundancy). Production runs on different hardware, with different bottlenecks. A green laptop test proves nothing about production scaling.
- **Single run, no replication.** One test run shows the code did not crash once under one scenario. Replication (same test, twice) would show whether results are stable. None is reported.
- **No threshold stated.** What counts as "scales to 10x"? Latency < 200ms? Cost per transaction stays flat? Zero downtime? The claim is silent. Without a threshold locked *before* the test, any green result can be declared a pass.
- **No breakage analysis.** A load test that passes green at 2x can still hide a bottleneck — a lock that contends at 2x but saturates at 4x, a database connection pool sized for 2x. The code path to bottleneck is not visible in one passing run.

*Why not [R]:* The model was tested and did not fail at 2x. [R] is for testing the claim and it failing. Here, the claim was not tested. There is a test, but it does not target the claim's scope.

## Summary: Hit rate and missing card

**Verdicts by tier:**
- Claims reaching [K] (Proven): 1 of 6 (Claim 5)
- Claims reaching [H] or [S] (Plausible, Speculative): 5 of 6 (Claims 1, 3, 4, 6)
- Misleading [Y]: 1 of 6 (Claim 2)

**Hit rate:** 17% of claims survive the board summary's framing without major caveat.

**What the document omits:**
- **Precision, specificity, and operating points.** The model's raw recall means nothing without disclosure of false-positive rate at the chosen operating point and the rationale for that choice.
- **Baselines and alternative explanations.** The 22% loss reduction is not attributed to the model; it could be seasonal. Analyst trust is not compared to trust in the old system.
- **Confound and control evidence.** No symmetric control for fairness (is the old engine fairer?). No specification of what fairness means.
- **Behavior vs. opinion.** Analysts said they trust it; whether they act as if they trust it (higher adoption, faster decisions, higher money moved) is unrecorded.
- **Production-readiness specifics.** A 2x load test does not predict 10x scaling, and a laptop does not predict production. The engineering risk is unnamed.

## Recommendation

**For the board: Do not sign off on all six claims.** Instead:

1. **Separate the survivable from the at-risk.** Claim 5 (FP rate) should go to the board; the other five should not, or should go with explicit caveats.

2. **Immediate: Lock the fairness definition and measure it.** Claim 3 is testable; it is just not tested. Define fairness metric, specify groups, sample appropriately, and re-measure. This is high impact and could flip the verdict in days.

3. **Immediate: Run the Q3/Q4 baseline check.** Pull three years of Q3/Q4 historical loss deltas. If the 22% is 2–3σ beyond the seasonal variance, Claim 2 is promoted to [H]; if not, it remains [Y]. This is a two-hour analysis.

4. **Before scaling claims: Re-test at 10x under production topology.** A laptop test is not scaling evidence. Build a production-like staging environment and re-run. Current test stays at [S] until then.

5. **Document analyst behavior, not opinion.** How many decisions are overridden per month? Adoption curves? Time to triage? One quantified metric beats 20 assertions.

**Cost of board approval as-is:** If any of Claims 1, 3, 4, or 6 fail in production (model recalls unexpectedly, fairness complaint surfaces, scaling hits a cliff), the approval chain is visible and the step where it should have been caught is on record. Mizan's value is not risk elimination; it is risk visibility.
